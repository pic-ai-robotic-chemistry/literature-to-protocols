ABSTRACT: The atomic-level dispersion of noble metals in porous materials is prospective for highly efficient catalyst systems but is still inscrutable. Inspired by sandwich compounds, platinum (Pt) atoms were rationally confined within a metal−organic framework (MOF) through pore space partition by conjugate interactions between Pt and parallel-distributed aromatic pore partitioners, forming 1D infinite Pt arrays. By regulating the distance between adjacent aromatic rings from 7 to 4.5 to 3 Å (∼ the diameter of the Pt atom), the particle size in sandwich Pt arrays decreased in a linear fashion with the Pt electronic orbital simultaneously regulated by a pore partitioner, MOF nodes, and near noble atoms, as revealed by AC highangle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy, X-ray absorption spectroscopy, and theoretical simulations. The optimized MOF

![](_page_0_Figure_9.jpeg)

pore endows the Pt@MOF-BCP material with highly efficient utilization of Pt sites, best electron transmission efficiency, and excellent catalytic stability. At 10 mA·cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> , an optimal Pt@MOF-BCP catalyst only needs ultralow overpotentials of 2.5 mV for hydrogen evolution reaction in the acidic electrolyte and 265 mV for oxygen evolution reaction in the alkaline electrolyte, exhibiting exceptional difunctional electrocatalytic performance superior to most benchmark catalysts. Remarkably, the cell voltage of Pt@ MOF-BCP was dramatically reduced to 1.47 V at 10 mA·cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> in practical application.

# ■ **INTRODUCTION**

Water splitting is considered as an environment-friendly energy conversion for mitigating the global energy and environmental crisis.[1](#page-8-0) Hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) are the cores of electrocatalytic water splitting[.2](#page-8-0) However, the sluggish kinetics of two reactions limit energy conversion efficiency.[3](#page-8-0) To realize the high catalytic efficiency during water electrolysis, efficiency catalysts that can minimize overpotential are desiderate[.4](#page-8-0) To date, noble metals are still considered to be the most efficient commercial electrocatalyst for water splitting[.5](#page-8-0) However, high cost, low durability, and scarcities of noble metal catalysts plagued their large-scale application in water splitting.[6](#page-8-0)

Construction of noble metal-based catalysts with precise atomic-level control over size, structure, and composition is the most effective strategy to balance cost and catalytic efficiency.[7](#page-8-0) At the same time, the interactions of noble metals and support materials can promote effective dissociation of reactants and optimize the adsorption/desorption of key intermediates.[8](#page-8-0) Several strategies have been developed to anchor noble metals including defect engineering, interface inducing, and pore encapsulating. Nevertheless, the adjustable size, loading amount, stability, and defined atomic structure still remain formidable challenges.[9](#page-8-0)

Organic linker-protected noble metals with customized metal size, transparent structure, and explicit interaction force are typical for molecular catalysts.[10](#page-8-0) Especially, sandwich organometal compounds with a metal atom lying between two aromatic organic rings can display exceptional electrochemical stability and catalytic performance.[11](#page-8-0) Parallel aromatic rings with a *π* hole feature are utilized as electronic acceptors for crystal engineering[,12](#page-8-0) and the center metal atoms serve as donor typically,[13](#page-8-0) forming the metals-*π*-conjugated system. Benefiting from the dominant interactions with high bond energy, center noble metals are always stabilized extremely[.14](#page-8-0) Moreover, the d-electronic orbitals of the metal overlap with the *π* electronic orbit of organic rings in sandwich structures and may effectively regulate the electronic structure of catalysts and enhance the electron transport rate.[15](#page-8-0)

A metal−organic framework (MOF) possessing vast types of aromatic linkers and porous structures is regarded as the ideal model support to encapsulate noble metals at the atomic

The atomic-level dispersion of noble metals in MOF pores has been a huge challenge up to now.

Pore space partition (PSP), an efficient strategy, has been proposed to maximize the utilization efficiency of MOF channels/pores. PSP in *acs*-type metal−organic frameworks (*pacs*-MOFs) through the induction of triangular aromatic organic partitioners into one-dimensional (1D) hexagonal channels formed by ditopic linkers effectively promoted their gas adsorption and separation ability.[17](#page-8-0) The original continuous 1D hexagonal channels are precisely partitioned into small finite segments by tuning the pore-partitioning agents and ditopic linkers.[18](#page-8-0) Building upon previous advances, the vertical pore dimension of each finite segment can be finely modulated by adjusting the length of the ditopic linkers.[19](#page-8-0) Herein, the *pacs*-MOF platform is demonstrated as an ideal platform to precisely load Pt metals. The aromatic partitioners with a *π* hole lying in a 1D hexagonal MOF channel are parallel to each other with a regulatable separation distance, which rationally anchor Pt atoms and isolate them in the confined pores. The sizes of trapped Pt clusters are engineered by regulating ditopic linkers, and aromatic pore partitioners act as a *π* electron donor with Pt metals to generate unique 1D infinite Pt arrays with a clear structure and composition. Benefiting from the tandem *π*···Pt···*π* electronic conjugate system, Pt@MOF catalysts exhibited enhanced catalytic activity and stability. Specially, Pt atoms confined in a MOF-BCP skeleton with a comparable pore size of 3 Å (∼the diameter of the Pt atom) can interact with not only aromatic partitioned linkers but also MOF metal nodes, which strongly promoted the atomic utilization and electron transmission efficiency of Pt. The optimal Pt@MOF-BCP catalyst at 10 mA· cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> only needs overpotentials of 2.5 mV for HER in the acidic electrolyte and 265 mV for the OER in alkaline electrolyte together with a cell voltage as low as 1.47 V for practical overall water splitting.

# ■ **RESULTS AND DISCUSSION**

A series of FeNi-MIL-88 bimetallic MOF precursors[20](#page-8-0) with outstanding synergistic catalytic effects were first designed to implement the precise Pt encapsulation (Figure 1). The continuous 1D hexagonal channels were then partitioned into small finite segments precisely along the *c* axis via a 2,4,6-tri(4 pyridinyl)-1-pyridine (TPP) pore partitioner. Sandwich *π*electron spaces between two parallel TPP linkers were then formed to stabilize Pt atoms. To further control the size of trapped Pt atoms, different ditopic linkers of varying lengths, including bicyclo[1.1.1]pentane, 1,4-terephthalic acid, and naphthalene-2,6-dicarboxylic acid (denoted as BCP, BDC, and 2,6-NDC) are adopted. The corresponding MOF precursors, designated as MOF-BCP, MOF-BDC, and MOF-NDC, with the similar structural configuration as Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (CCDC) deposition numbers of 2159092,[21](#page-8-0) 2043323,[22](#page-8-0) and 2043329,[22](#page-8-0) respectively, exhibit the sandwich *π*-electron space heights of about 3.0 Å, 4.5 Å, and 7 Å (the heights correspond to the distances between two adjacent TPP planes with the van der Waals radii taken into account), respectively (Figure 1)[.22](#page-8-0) Notably, the pore sizes are just 1-fold, 1.5-fold, and 2-fold the diameter of the single Pt atom (∼3 Å), promoting the atomic-level dispersion-like

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further confirmed the interactions and electron density distributions of these Pt@MOF catalysts ([Figures](#page-2-0) 2 and [S1](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf)).[23](#page-8-0) The Pt atoms confined within the MOF pores were used in DFToptimized models to approximate the behavior of Pt. As expected, confined in limited partitioned space, the electronic orbits of the Pt atom overlapped the *π* electronic orbit of aromatic TPP linkers,[24](#page-8-0) giving *π*···Pt···*π* electronic conjugated coordination. Optimal Pt atoms can locate in the tandem pore segments between parallel TPP partitioners, forming an infinite ···Pt···TPP···Pt···TPP··· sandwich array structure. Derived from the stronger electronegativity and higher electron density of the pyridine N atom, favorable Pt−N coordination bonds further stabilized the Pt array arranged between Pt atoms and TPP linkers ([Figures](#page-2-0) 2a and [S2](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf)−S4)[.25](#page-8-0)

The movement of an electron from metal Pt to a nonmetallic TPP linker indicated that the TPP organic linker served as an electronic acceptor and Pt was regarded as a donor. Notably, due to the best encapsulation space, the electronic orbit of Pt and MOF-BCP overlapped the furthest, exhibiting an obvious charge redistribution phenomenon, indicating the strong interaction between Pt and MOF frameworks.[27](#page-9-0) A two-dimensional electron localization function (ELF) of Pt@MOF-BCP exhibited a stronger interaction and shorter interatomic distance between Pt−N, further manifesting the strongly electron overlap in Pt@MOF-BCP ([Figure](#page-2-0) 2c).[28](#page-9-0)

High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images of Pt@MOFs revealed abundant dispersive bright spots, representing the heavier Pt clusters [\(Figure](#page-2-0) 3d−i). The magnified HAADF-STEM images displayed that Pt atoms were successfully packaged into MOF pores uniformly without agglomeration. Due to the interconnected pore space, Pt clusters were formed. The Pt cluster sizes were linearly regulated by the smallest axis of the pore spaces with average sizes of 1.22 ± 0.22, 1.56 ± 0.13, and 1.78 ± 0.22 nm for Pt@MOF-BCP/BDC/NDC [\(Figure](#page-2-0) 3j−l).

Elemental mappings, energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analyses, inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP−OES), and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) measurements were performed to further quantify the loading amount of Pt. Elemental mappings displayed that Pt, Fe, Ni, and O elements were distributed homogeneously [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S21−S23). EDX revealed that the Pt mass contents of Pt@MOF-BCP/ BDC/NDC were 24.6%, 18.4%, and 14.5%, respectively ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S24−S26). ICP-OES analysis showed that the actual amounts of Pt clusters were 22.1, 16.0, and 8.2 wt % ([Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) [S1](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf)). TGA measurements of Pt@MOF-BCP/BDC/NDC exhibited mass losses of 23.3%, 18.0%, and 13.2%, respectively ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S27−S29). Pt@MOF-BCP exhibited the highest dispersion density and greatest Pt loading within the pores, attributable to the minimal segment space of the MOF-BCP precursor. The Pt loading increased linearly as the pore sizes of MOF precursors decreased. All results demonstrated that the loading amount and particle size of Pt clusters are clearly regulated by the PSP process ([Figure](#page-2-0) 3m).

The atomic structure of the Pt cluster was further characterized by aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM (AC HAADF-STEM). As shown in [Figure](#page-2-0) 3n, bright spots were observed at the edge of Pt@MOF-BCP, representing the existence of Pt clusters. AC HAADF-STEM images displayed that the size of domination Pt clusters in Pt@MOF-BCP was about 1.2 nm. The lattice spacings of 0.252 nm can be attributed to the (111) planes of Pt clusters ([Figures](#page-2-0) 3o and [S30\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf). The lattice fringe of Pt clusters within Pt@MOF-BCP was slightly larger than the interplanar distance of intrinsic Pt (111) (0.226 nm),[29](#page-9-0) attributed to the stretched coordination environment. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) image was assigned to metallic Pt (111), further confirming the formation of Pt clusters in partitioned pore space.

N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms at 77 K showed Pt@ MOFs with microporous features, illustrating their integral crystalline structures [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S31−S33). Compared with their pristine MOF precursors, the dramatically decreased specific surface areas and pore volumes of Pt@MOFs indicate that Pt incorporation within the pores induces partial pore occlusion, thereby confirming the strong spatial confinement effect and structural fastness of Pt within MOFs [\(Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S2).

The electronic structures of Pt@MOFs were verified by Xray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy. The Pt 4f spectrum of Pt@MOF-BCP was located between K2PtCl4 and Pt foil, confirming the formation of metallic Pt and charge consumption on the Pt clusters side ([Figure](#page-3-0) 4a)[.30](#page-9-0) With the pore space enlarged, the energy of Pt shifted to a higher energy, suggesting that there is more dispersity of Pt clusters. The discrete coordination increased the surface energy and shifted to higher electron binding energies.[31](#page-9-0) In the XPS spectra of N 1s, the peak at 399.2 eV was attributed to the pyridine N atoms in MOF-BCP.[32](#page-9-0) After introduction of Pt clusters, a new peak at 400.2 eV was observed in Pt@MOF-BCP, which can be assigned to the Pt− N bond.[33](#page-9-0) The new peak further confirmed the newly generated coordination modes between Pt and the precursor MOFs [\(Figure](#page-3-0) 4b). Besides, the peak of pyridine N in Pt@ MOF-BCP showed a negative shift, proving the electrons transfer from Pt clusters to the TPP linker, which was in accord with the above DFT calculations.

The normalized Pt L3-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra of Pt@MOFs displayed that all of the white line intensity located between Pt foil and PtO2, verifying the average valence state of Pt was between Pt0 and Pt4+ ([Figure](#page-3-0) 4c). Among them, the white line intensity of Pt@ MOF-BCP was much closer to Pt foil, suggesting the most metallic Pt.[34](#page-9-0) According to the correlation of absorption edge, the average valence states of Pt were +0.05, +0.10, and +1.10 in Pt@MOF-BCP/BDC/NDC catalysts, respectively [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) [S34\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf). The monitor trends mainly originated from a greater degree of electron overlap in Pt@MOF-BCP.[35](#page-9-0) Pt L-edge EXAFS spectra of three Pt@MOFs exhibited major peaks at a distance of 2.1, 1.8, and 1.8 Å, respectively [\(Figure](#page-3-0) 4d). Particularly, the major peak of Pt@MOF-BCP showed the greatest degree of shift, which was attributed to the multiple coordination in the smallest pore space and the strongly interaction between Pt clusters and MOF-BCP.

Combined with structural design and theoretical calculation, the EXAFS fitting results indicated that the main peaks of Pt@ MOFs were assigned to Pt−N and Pt-metal coordination ([Figures](#page-3-0) 4e and [S35](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf)−S36). On account of the most electronegativity difference, Pt atoms contact N atoms from parallel TPP linkers to form Pt−N bonds, which was reasonable. Furthermore, EXAFS fitting of Pt@MOF-BCP/ BDC/NDC confirmed the lengths of Pt−N were 1.96, 1.98, and 2.03 Å, respectively, stating the different atomic coordination configuration of Pt−N in different pore spaces ([Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S3). Benefiting from the smallest pore size and the strong conjugated system between Pt clusters and MOF support, the Pt−N bonds in Pt@MOF-BCP possessed the shortest bond length, which was also consistent with the DFT calculation.

Remarkably, except for the formation of major coordination Pt−N bonds, Pt-metal coordination was also formed in Pt@ MOFs. Pt-metal coordination configuration was further surveyed using quantitative least-squares EXAFS curvefitting.[36](#page-9-0) EXAFS fitting of Pt@MOF-BCP exhibited the predominant Pt−Fe/Ni (2.52 Å) and Pt−Pt (2.66 Å) configurations. That is, due to the interconnected partitioned pore space of MOF-BCP, Pt can interact with the metal nodes of the MOF-BCP support to form Pt−Fe/Ni bonds. Besides, the inherent infinity frameworks of MOF-BCP provided the chance to cross-link the adjacent Pt atoms to form the infinite 1D Pt array. Above all, as shown in [Figure](#page-3-0) 4f, each Pt atom can interact with aromatic TPP partitioners, Fe/Ni nodes of intrinsic MOF, and adjacent Pt atoms simultaneously to give a triple coordination configuration.

To more precisely clarify the coordination conditions of Pt, WT analysis was carried out. Especially, the intensity maximum at 6.6 Å<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> for Pt@MOF-BCP located between PtO2 and Pt foil, which is different from that for the Pt−O (5.6 Å<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ) as well as Pt−Pt coordination mode (9.4 Å<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ), further confirms the intercoordination conditions for Pt in MOF-BCP. With the pore spaces of precursor MOFs increased, triple coordination configurations trend to clearness. This may be caused by the more discrete Pt clusters in the pore and the more distinguishable average coordination [\(Figure](#page-3-0) 4g). All these results successfully resolve the precise coordination configuration and atomic-level structural details of the sandwichstructured Pt array encapsulated within Pt@MOFs.

Fe 2p and Ni 2p XPS spectra of three Pt@MOFs all showed positive shifts compared with MOF supports, implying the electron transfer from the Fe/Ni to TPP linker ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S37 and [S38\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf). Meanwhile, the higher oxidation states of both Fe and Ni were observed within the smaller pores, which were attributed to enhanced electronic interactions between the contracted MOF pore architecture and the encapsulated Pt clusters [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S39). The energy absorption edge of the Pt@ MOF-BCP catalyst was located at the position near Fe2O3, verifying the existence of Fe3+ ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S40). EXAFS spectra of Fe validated that the typical Fe−O(N) scattering configuration was located at ∼1.4 Å with a slight shift, further implying the interaction between Pt and the intrinsic MOF-BCP [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) [S41\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf). Ni K-edge XANES spectra of Pt@MOF-BCP showed features similar to those of NiO, implying a +2 oxidation state similar to that of NiO ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S42). Similar to Fe, the main peak at 1.5 Å corresponding to Ni−O coordination showed a slight shift [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S43). EXAFS fitting results of Fe/Ni in Pt@ MOF-BCP suggested that except the normal coordination of metal (FeNi)−O in nodes of intrinsic MOF, the coordination between Pt and Fe/Ni was formed undoubtedly [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S44− S45 and [Tables](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S4−S5).

The Tafel slope of Pt@MOF-BCP (42.5 mV·dec<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ) was significantly lower than those of Pt@MOF-BDC and Pt@ MOF-NDC, consistent with a Volmer−Heyrovsky ratedetermining step, confirming the more favorable reaction kinetics for H\* adsorption on Pt@MOF-BCP ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) [S49\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf).[38](#page-9-0),[39](#page-9-0) In addition, Pt@MOF-BCP showed the smallest charge transfer resistance (*R*ct) of 18.04 Ω, which is 1.3 and 2.2-fold diminution for Pt@MOF-BDC (22.79 Ω) and Pt@ MOF-NDC (39.64 Ω) ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S50). Pt@MOF-BCP displayed the largest electric double-layer capacitance *C*dl value of 76.6 mF·cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> , which is 1.2 and 7.0-fold enhancement over Pt@ MOF-BDC (62.1 mF·cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> ) and Pt@MOF-NDC (11.0 mF· cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> ), respectively [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S51). Clearly, the Pt@MOF-BCP catalyst exhibits most efficient charge-transfer kinetics and largest electrochemically active area. The consistent trends in electrochemical performance, including *η*10, Tafel slope, *C*dl, and *R*ct, suggested that the optimized Pt@MOF-BCP can facilitate favorable reaction kinetics for H\* adsorption and conversion [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S52).

HER performance was also investigated in the alkaline electrolyte. Pt clusters within packaged MOFs were considered as active species for HER. Variations in the confined pore spaces of Pt@MOFs resulted in distinct spatial distributions and host−guest interaction forces between the Pt cluster and MOF frameworks via *π*···Pt···*π* electronic conjugated coordination. Furthermore, the inevitable interplay between Pt and pristine Fe/Ni metal clusters within the MOF-BCP frameworks also modulated the electronic structure, as evidenced by XPS results ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S39). The synergistic electronic optimization of Pt enhances electronic coupling, accelerates interfacial charge transfer, and stabilizes the H\* intermediates during HER catalysis.

To elucidate the intrinsic relationship between *π*···Pt···*π* coordination and enhanced electrocatalytic activity, DFT calculations were further performed on Pt@MOF-BCP and Pt clusters in the acidic electrolyte ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S57−S59). DFT calculations revealed that Δ*G*H\* at Pt@MOF-BCP was −0.17 eV, which was closer to the ideal thermoneutral value of Δ*G*H\* = 0 eV compared to the standard Pt cluster of Δ*G*H\* of −0.26 eV,[40](#page-9-0) indicating the more favorable adsorption/desorption kinetics for a key intermediate of H\* ([Figure](#page-5-0) 5d).[41](#page-9-0) Therefore, due to the high density of Pt sites and optimized electronic structure within Pt@MOF-BCP, the greatest extent modulation of intermediate H\* binding energy through the *π*···Pt···*π* electronic conjugate system facilitates the H2 evolution.

Furthermore, the different loading amounts of Pt were further explored. Pt@MOF-BCP catalysts with different input amounts of Pt were prepared. The excessive concentration would result in the agglomeration of Pt clusters, thereby compromising their catalytic efficacy. The insufficient amount of Pt leads to the inadequate modulation of electronic structure and ultimately impairs the desired catalytic performance ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S60−S70).

OER performances of Pt@MOFs were tested in 1 M KOH. It can be seen that to reach a high current density of 100 mA· cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> , the potential of Pt@MOF-BCP was 1.54 V ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S71 and [S72](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf)), which was lower than those of Pt@MOF-BDC (1.55 V), Pt@MOF-NDC (1.56 V), RuO2 (1.8 V), Pt/C (1.75 V), and Pt@MIL-88 (1.67 V) catalysts. Compared with commercial Pt- and Ru- based catalysts, Pt@MOF-BCP exhibited superior intrinsic activity, achieving a TOF value of 0.043 s <sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> and mass activity of 42.1 A·g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> at *η* = 300 mV during OER [\(Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S7). Bode plots of Pt@MOF-BCP exhibited the greatest degree of impedance, further indicating the fast charge-transfer kinetics [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S73).[42](#page-9-0) Pt@MOF-BCP exhibited the a smallest Tafel slope, smallest *R*ct value, and the largest *C*dl value during the OER reaction on account of the outperforming reaction kinetics [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S74−S76). The superior OER performance of Pt@MOF-BCP is attributed to the stabilization of high-valent metal centers (Fe3+/Ni3+), which is facilitated by the stronger Pt-support interactions compared to Pt@MOF-BDC and Pt@MOF-NDC. This enhanced charge polarization of metal centers promotes the formation of active intermediates, directly contributing to catalytic enhancement.[43](#page-9-0)

During the OER process, DFT calculations showed that the energy barrier of the O\* formation step was 0.59 eV for Pt@ MOF-BCP, which was smaller than that of the pristine Pt cluster (0.71 eV), indicating the improvement of electronic structure via Pt-support interactions [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S77−S80). Thus, the newly generated *π*···Pt···*π* electronic conjugate system enhances the intrinsic reactive kinetics for water oxidation.[44,45](#page-9-0) Hence, Pt@MOF-BCP exhibited best performance for HER and OER in acidic and alkaline electrolytes, which was superior to nearly all similar catalysts ([Figure](#page-5-0) 5e and [Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S8).[46](#page-9-0),[47](#page-9-0)

Catalytic durability plays a crucial role in practical applications. Profiting from the unique sandwich Pt array within pore-space-partitioned MOFs, Pt@MOF-BCP also exhibited superior stability for HER and OER in acidic and alkaline electrolytes ([Figure](#page-5-0) 5f). The XRD revealed that the structure of the MOF was maintained after HER ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S81). <span id="page-7-0"></span>Pt clusters were still confined in the pore spaces with slight agglomeration, and the size was about 1.24 ± 0.1 nm ([Figures](#page-5-0) [5](#page-5-0)g and S82−[S83](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf)). Postreaction EDX, TGA, and ICP-OES analyses revealed the negligible percentage composition of Pt loading after HER reaction, indicating the structural stability of a sandwich Pt array within the MOF ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S84). Furthermore, ICP-OES analysis of the post-HER electrolyte detected no leaching of Pt species, conclusively demonstrating the structural robustness of Pt within MOFs and further substantiating exceptional electrochemical durability. Furthermore, XPS, normalized XANES spectra, and WT analysis indicated the slight shift after HER reaction [\(Figures](#page-5-0) 5h and [S85](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf)−S86). EXAFS fitting results showed that the bond length of Pt−N was 1.82 Å, slightly shortened after HER, demonstrating the slight variation of local electronic structures ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S87 and Table S9), which may be caused by the mild agglomeration of Pt clusters. Overall, the Pt@MOF-BCP catalyst exhibited excellent activity and robust stability, making it possible to be utilized in practical overall water splitting application ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S88−S98).

Inspired by the exceptional HER and OER performance, two-electrode electrolyzer tests were carried out in 1 M KOH with Pt@MOFs as the cathode and anode. The Δ*E* value of Pt@MOF-BCP was about 1.55 V at 10 mA·cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> , which was lower than those of Pt@MOF-BDC (1.60 V), Pt@MOF-NDC (1.67 V), and Pt/C + RuO2 (1.72 V), indicating the excellent performance in two-electrode tests ([Figure](#page-6-0) 6a). The cell voltage of Pt@MOF-BCP||Pt@MOF-BCP (1.47 V) reached the current density of 10 mA·cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> , which was significantly lower than those of Pt@MOF-BDC (1.55 V), Pt@MOF-NDC (1.71 V), and Pt/C||RuO2 (1.50 V), indicating outstanding electrocatalytic activity of Pt@MOF-BCP for overall water splitting in practice ([Figure](#page-6-0) 6b). The Tafel slope of Pt@MOF-BCP||Pt@MOF-BCP proved to possess superior reaction kinetics for water electrolysis in 1 M KOH [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S99). Encouragingly, the bifunctional Pt@MOF-BCP catalyst exhibited extraordinary performance toward overall water splitting in alkaline media, which was superior to most MOF-based catalysts [\(Figure](#page-6-0) 6c). Long-term stability of the bifunctional Pt@MOF-BCP catalyst was further examined, which showed superior electrocatalytic stability in twoelectrode in alkaline media ([Figure](#page-6-0) 6d). Large amounts of H2 and O2 gas products can be generated on the cathode and anode devices simultaneously, indicating the competent and persistent catalyst for overall water splitting. Faradaic efficiency of overall water splitting reaction over the Pt@MOF-BCP catalyst was calculated to be around 97% under the current density of 100 mA·cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> for 120 min ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf) S100). SEM, TEM, and HAADF-STEM images for the catalyst after overall water splitting further revealed the ultrarobust structure of Pt@ MOF-BCP under high current density [\(Figures](#page-6-0) 6d inset and S101−[S102\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c04935/suppl_file/ja5c04935_si_001.pdf).

# ■ **CONCLUSION**

In this study, a series of precisely engineered sandwichstructured platinum arrays were successfully constructed within pore-space-partitioned MOFs. Platinum was encapsulated within two parallel aromatic partitioners, forming a welldefined sandwich architecture. The size of Pt clusters was systematically controlled by modulating the geometry of ditopic linkers, while a stable *π*···Pt···*π* conjugated electronic system was established through interactions between *π*electrons from the partitioners and d-orbitals of Pt. Notably, Pt clusters located in adjacent pores lined up to form continuous 1D Pt arrays extending along the partitioned MOF channel. The spatial confinement stabilized the sandwich Pt arrays and modified their electronic structure. The optimized catalyst, Pt@MOF-BCP (derived from the smallest pore size), demonstrated exceptional electrocatalytic performance with an overpotential of 2.5 mV for HER in the acidic electrolyte and 265 mV for OER in the alkaline electrolyte at 10 mA·cm<sup>−</sup><sup>2</sup> as well as top-level overall water splitting activity and stability superior to benchmark catalysts. This work provides a paradigm for the atomic-level design of noble-metal secondary structures within MOFs, offering new avenues for advanced energy conversion technologies.

## **S1. Experimental Details**

#### **S1.1. Materials and reagents**

Chemical reagents and materials, including Fe(NO3)3, Ni(NO3)2, bicyclo[1.1.1]pentane (BCP), 1,4 terephthalic acid (H2BDC), naphthalene-2,6-dicarboxylic acid (2,6-NDC), K2PtCl4, N,Ndimethylacetamide (DMA), 1,3-dimethyl-3,4,5,6-tetrahydro-2(1H)-pyrimidinone (DMPU), 1,1,1,5,5,5-hexafluoro-2,4-pentanedione (HFP), CH3OH and HCOOK were purchased from Aladdin and China National Pharmaceutical Group Corporation (Shanghai, China). Deionized water was prepared in the lab.

#### **S1.2. Characterizations**

Powder X-ray diffractions (PXRD) were carried out on a MiniFlex600 X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) using a Cu-Ka source equipped within the range of 2θ = 5-50°. The morphological and element mapping of as-prepared samples were measured on scanning electron microscope (SEM, SU8220, HITACHI, Japan) and transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2800F, JEOL, Japan). Energy-dispersive X-ray analyses (EDX) were tested on by using a Quanta 200 spectrometer (FEI Co. Ltd, America). Chemical composition and state of samples were investigated using the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS ULTRA, Japan). All of the binding energy was corrected using the C *1s* peak as an internal standard at 284.6 eV except for Pt. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) spectrometry of samples was tested on AGILENT ICP 730 (Agilent Co. Ltd, America). N<sup>2</sup> adsorption isotherms were carried out on a Micromeritics 3- Flex surface-area and pore-size analyzer at standard atmospheric pressure by the static volumetric method. Acetonitrile-exchanged compounds were loaded into the sample tube and dried at 80 °C to remove the solvent. The X-ray absorption spectra of Fe, Ni and Pt including X-ray absorption nearedge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) of the samples were conducted in fluorescence mode at the BL14W1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility, China, operated at 3.5 GeV with maximum injection currents of 230 mA. The synchrotron beam was monochromatized using a double-crystal monochromator equipped with a Si (111) crystal to reduce the harmonic component of the monochrome beam.

#### **Electrochemical measurements**

All electrochemical measurements were carried out in 0.5 M H2SO<sup>4</sup> and 1 M KOH (pH = 13.7) solution on an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) with a three-electrode system. The prepared samples were used as the working electrodes and a graphite rod and Hg2Cl2/Hg (HgO/Hg) electrode in 0.5 M H2SO<sup>4</sup> (1 M KOH) were used as the counter and reference electrode, respectively. The asprepared catalyst (10 mg) and acetylene black (10 mg) was added into 1 mL of mixture solution of water (640 μL), ethanol (310 μL) and Nafion (50 μL) and sonicated for at least 1 h forming ink to prepare electrocatalyst. The ink (150 μL) was dropped on the cleaned carbon cloth for HER, OER and water splitting. The working area of the carbon cloth electrode was 1 cm<sup>2</sup> and the catalyst loading amount was 1.5 mg·cm-2 . Before LSV measurements, cyclic voltammetry curves (CV) were used to activate the working electrode. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements were tested at a scan rate of 5 mV∙s-1 with 95 % *iR*-compensation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out by applying with 5 mV amplitude in a frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 100 kHz. Electrochemically active surface areas (ECSA) were evaluated by measuring the double-layer capacitance (*C*dl) *via* cyclic voltammograms (CV) in a potential range of 1.1 to 1.2 V *vs.* RHE for OER in 1 M KOH, 0.3 to 0.4 V *vs.* RHE for HER in 0.5 M H2SO<sup>4</sup> and 0.4 to 0.5 V *vs.* RHE for HER in 1 M KOH, in which without redox process by *C*dl = *I*/*ν*, where *I* was the charging current (mA∙cm-2 ), and ν was the scan rate (mV∙s-1 ). The *C*dl values were calculated by using the CV curves from 5 to 100 mV∙s-1 scan rates. The long-term durabilities of these samples were assessed by the *i-t* testing at the potential of *η*100 mA∙cm-2 for 12 h in 0.5 M H2SO<sup>4</sup> and 1 M KOH.

**Faradaic efficiency measurements:** The closed electrolyser was purged with N<sup>2</sup> for several time to completely remove the air in the system. In the N2-saturated system, the generation H<sup>2</sup> over the Pt@MOF-BCP catalyst at 100 mA·cm-2 was based on gas chromatography measurement (GC9790 II, Fuli, Zhejiang, China) every 20 min. Faradaic efficiency of Pt@MOF-BCP was calculated by the ratio of amount of H<sup>2</sup> experimentally generated to the theoretically calculated H2.

Turnover frequencies (TOF) for HER and OER at different potentials were calculated as follow equation:

$$
TOF = jS/(zFn)
$$

where *j* (A∙cm-2 ) is the current density, S is the surface area (1 cm-2 ), z the number of electrons transfer, F is the Faraday constant of 96485 C·mol-1 , n is the total metal ions molar number calculated from the EDX results of Pt@MOFs. Assuming that the Ni, Fe and Pt sites were all catalytically active, the TOF values of Pt@MOFs and commercial Pt-based catalysts were calculated.

Metals mass activity was calculated as follow:

mass activity = *j*/mmetal

where *j* (A·cm-2 ) is the current density, mmetal is metal loading mass of catalyst from EDX. In order to compare the metals activities, their activity values have been normalized to all metals.

BET-normalized LSV curves were calculated as follow:

$$
j_{\text{surface areas}} = j/\text{BET}
$$

In ICP-OES experiments, Pt@MOFs powder (8.8 mg) was deconstructed by 1 mL nitric acid and 1 mL perchloric acid under 200 <sup>o</sup>C with 1 h. Then the deconstructed-solution and electrolyte after the reaction were transferred to a 10 mL volumetric flask and filled to constant volume with H2O and diluted the obtained solution tenfold. The diluted solution was test on ICP-OES equipment. The actual concentration of metal elements was calculated as:

$$
C(mg/kg) = \frac{Cx (mg/L) * f * V(mL) * 0.001}{m(g) * 0.001}
$$

where *C<sup>x</sup>* is the concentration obtained from ICP-OES equipment, *f* is the dilution ratio, *V* is the constant volume, *m* is the sample mass.

### **S1.3. Synthesis**

**Preparation of MOF precursors.** To prepare MOF-BCP, a mixture solution of Fe(NO3)<sup>3</sup> (20 mg), Ni(NO3)<sup>2</sup> (20 mg), BCP (31 mg), TPP (21 mg) and 3 g DMA/H2O (v/v = 5/1) was sealed in a 20 mL vial and heated at 130 °C for 1 day. The precipitate was filtrated and washed with EtOH. The precipitate was placed in a vacuum oven at 60 <sup>o</sup>C to remove the residual EtOH.

To prepare MOF-BDC, a mixture solution of Fe(NO3)<sup>3</sup> (30 mg), Ni(NO3)<sup>2</sup> (30 mg), BDC (33 mg), TPP (30 mg) and 3 g DMA/H2O (v/v = 5/1) was sealed in a 20 mL vial and heated at 130 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room-temperature, the precipitate was centrifuged and washed with EtOH, respectively. The precipitate was placed in a vacuum oven at 60 <sup>o</sup>C to remove the residual EtOH. To prepare MOF-NDC, a solution mixture of Fe(NO3)<sup>3</sup> (30 mg), Ni(NO3)<sup>2</sup> (30 mg), NDC (43 mg), TPP (62 mg), 6 g DMA/DMPU (v/v = 2/1), and 28 μL HFP was sealed in a 20 mL vial and heated

at 130 °C for 1 day. After cooling to room-temperature, the precipitate was centrifuged and washed with EtOH, respectively. The precipitate was placed in a vacuum oven at 60 <sup>o</sup>C to remove the residual EtOH.

**Preparation of MIL-88.** FeCl<sup>3</sup> (0.13 g), Ni(NO3)<sup>2</sup> (0.13 g), BDC (0.1661 g) and DMF (10 mL) were sealed in a 20 mL vial and heated at 100 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room-temperature, the precipitate was centrifuged and washed with EtOH, respectively. The precipitate was placed in a vacuum oven at 60 <sup>o</sup>C to remove the residual EtOH.

**Preparation of Pt@MOFs.** MOF precursors (20 mg) and K2PtCl<sup>4</sup> (10 mg) were added into 10 ml H2O. The mixed solution was ultrasonically dispersed for 10 seconds and heated at 90 °C (for 8 h to MOF-BCP precursor, 12 h for MOF-BDC precursor and MOF-NDC precursor). These products were centrifuged and washed with EtOH and then were placed in a vacuum oven at 60 <sup>o</sup>C to remove the residual EtOH. Then 20 mg precipitates and HCOOK (5 mg) were added into 5 mL CH3OH and 100 μL H2O. The mixed solutions were heated at 110 °C for 6 h (90 °C for MOF-BCP precursor). These products were second centrifuged and washed with EtOH and then were placed in a vacuum oven at 60 <sup>o</sup>C to remove the residual EtOH. Then the final products were obtained, and named as Pt@MOFs.

#### **S1.4 Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations**

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the DMol3 module within the Materials Studio software suite. The exchange-correlation interactions were described using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional, a widely adopted generalized gradient approximation (GGA) approach. Spin-polarized calculations were implemented with a Quintet numerical polarization basis set. The self-consistent field (SCF) procedure employed a convergence criterion of 1.0 × 10- ⁶ Hartree (Ha) for electronic energy minimization. For geometry optimization, the convergence thresholds were set at 1.0 × 10- ⁵ Ha for energy, 0.002 Ha/Å for maximum force, and 0.005 Å for maximum atomic displacement. To explicitly elucidate the spatial distribution and electronic interactions between Pt clusters and the MOF, Pt atoms were selected as a DFT-optimized model to approximate the electrochemical behavior of the Pt.

**Figure S78.** Modeling of the elementary electrochemical steps for OER on the Pt site of Pt@MOF-BCP.

**Note:** Following the four-step proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) mechanism, the OER process in an alkaline solution was illustrated by local structural configurations with adsorbed intermediates (Figures S77-S78). Initially, OHis adsorbed onto the metal active site (M) to form M-OH\*, followed by deprotonation and electron transfer to generate M-O\*. Subsequently, the M-O\* undergoes a nucleophilic attack by another OHto form M-OOH\* intermediate. Finally, O<sup>2</sup> is released via deportation of the M-OOH\*, regenerating the metal active site for the next OER cycle.
