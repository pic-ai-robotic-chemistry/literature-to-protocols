## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post-processing Steps

The article describes a two‐stage synthesis approach for MOF materials. First, MOF 1 is synthesized via a solvothermal method, and then a post‐synthesis ion exchange converts it to MOF 1Zn. A comparative analysis shows that while the solvothermal route provides highly crystalline products with controlled phase purity, its longer reaction times and specialized solvent systems pose scalability challenges. In contrast, the ion-exchange step offers precise metal incorporation and improved ionic conductivity but requires iterative processing and additional equipment.

#### Solvothermal Synthesis of MOF 1

1. Precursor Mixing/Dissolution  
   - • Reagents: 0.5 mmol ZrOCl₂·8H₂O and 0.5 mmol Li·[H4L] are dissolved in a solvent system comprising 60 mL DMF and 10 mL TFA.  
   - • Equipment: Reaction performed in a sealed autoclave (or 100 mL vial) ensuring controlled pressure and temperature.

2. Modulator or Structure-Directing Agent Addition  
   - • TFA is incorporated during mixing and acts as a modulator to influence crystal growth and purity.  
   - • In some procedures, triethylamine is also employed as an additive for reaction control.

3. Crystallization  
   - • Process: The mixture is heated at 100 °C for 24 h under solvothermal conditions, leading to the formation of colorless crystals.

4. Cooling and Collection  
   - • The reaction mixture is cooled to room temperature, and the crystals are collected by filtration.

5. Washing and Solvent Exchange  
   - • The collected crystals are washed with methanol to remove unreacted species and exchange residual solvent molecules.

6. Drying  
   - • Drying is achieved under vacuum at 80 °C, preparing the sample for activation.

7. Activation  
   - • Activation is performed by vacuum degassing at 160 °C for 10 h to remove trapped solvents and enhance framework openness.

#### Ion-Exchange Process for MOF 1Zn

1. The pristine MOF 1 is immersed in 2 mL of a 2.0 M aqueous solution of Zn(NO₃)₂ using a Schlenk tube.
2. Temperature and Duration  
   - • The process is conducted at 80 °C for 4 h per cycle and repeated five times until ICP-MS confirms the complete replacement of Li⁺ by Zn²⁺.
3. Post-treatment  
   - • The ion-exchanged product is washed with water and dried under vacuum at 120 °C. 

This structured sequence ensures reproducibility and clarity, enabling researchers to precisely replicate conditions for high-performance single-ion conductors in rechargeable zinc battery applications.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### MOF Preparation [1]: Pristine Anionic MOF (MOF 1)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Lithium tetraphenylborate ligand (0.5 mmol, 251 mg) and ZrOCl₂·8H₂O (0.5 mmol, 161 mg) are used. Additional solvents include DMF (60 mL) and TFA (10 mL).
- **Conditions**: The reagents are dissolved in the solvent mixture in a 100 mL vial sealed with a screw cap. Temperature is maintained at room temperature initially to ensure homogeneity.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the lithium tetraphenylborate ligand in water, then add ZrOCl₂·8H₂O.
  - Mix these with the DMF/TFA solvent blend.
  - Ensure complete dissolution and uniformity of the mixture by stirring at room temperature.
- **Key Steps**: Confirm complete solvation before sealing and proceeding.

##### Step 2: MOF Assembly/Crystallization
- **Reagents**: The precursor mixture prepared above.
- **Conditions**: The sealed vial is heated at 100 °C for 24 hours under solvothermal conditions.
- **Process**:
  - Place the vial in an oven and maintain the set temperature.
  - Allow the reaction to proceed for 24 hours, during which crystallization occurs.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain a consistent temperature and monitor timing closely.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**: 
  - After cooling, collect the formed crystals by vacuum filtration.
  - Wash the product with methanol (MeOH) to remove unreacted species.
- **Key Steps**: Use filtration and ensure thorough solvent exchange.

##### Step 4: Drying & Activation
- **Drying**: Dry the washed crystals under vacuum at 80 °C.
- **Activation**: Further activate by vacuum degassing at 160 °C for 10 hours to remove residual solvents.

#### MOF Preparation [2]: Zn²⁺ Ion‐Exchanged MOF (MOF 1Zn)

##### Step 1: Ion Exchange
- **Reagents**: Use a 2.0 M aqueous solution of Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (2 mL).
- **Conditions**: The purified MOF 1 is immersed in the solution inside a Schlenk tube at 80 °C.
- **Process**:
  - Immerse 60 mg of MOF 1 in the Zn(NO₃)₂ solution.
  - Incubate for 4 hours. Repeat this cycle five times.
  - Monitor ion exchange progress via ICP-MS to ensure complete Li⁺ replacement.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain 80 °C and strictly adhere to the cyclical immersion protocol.

##### Step 2: Final Purification
- **Process**:
  - Wash the exchanged MOF (MOF 1Zn) with water.
  - Dry under vacuum at 120 °C for 10 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete removal of solvents to support high ionic conductivity.

Critical parameters such as temperature, reagent quantities, and cycle repetitions are maintained to ensure reproducibility and framework integrity throughout both synthesis routes.

## 5. Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation of the MOF materials is a critical step aimed at ensuring complete removal of residual solvents and guest molecules from within the porous framework. This procedure, performed immediately after synthesis and washing, is essential for “opening” the MOF channels and thereby optimizing ion conduction for subsequent applications.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
Activation temperature is crucial for driving off residual solvent molecules without compromising the framework’s integrity. In the described process, the MOF is dried under vacuum at 120°C. This precise temperature is selected to ensure efficient solvent removal while maintaining the crystallinity and permanent porosity of the material. Observations from subsequent PXRD and gas sorption analyses confirm that this temperature condition effectively “opens” the framework channels without causing structural collapse.

#### 5.1.2 Vacuum Conditions
The activation process is conducted under vacuum, which significantly enhances the evaporation of any remaining guest molecules. Although no exact numerical pressure is provided, the use of vacuum drying facilitates a reduced atmospheric pressure environment that promotes efficient solvent removal. The low-pressure conditions prevent potential re-adsorption of solvent molecules, thereby preserving the well-defined porous structure required for high-performance ion conduction.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Equipment and Protocol
A vacuum oven is employed to achieve and maintain the necessary temperature and vacuum environment during activation. This equipment allows for precise control over both heating and pressure, ensuring a uniform activation process. Prior to vacuum drying, the MOF samples were thoroughly washed with deionized water and acetone, thereby minimizing the presence of residual impurities. Such detailed activation conditions have been validated by the high BET surface areas observed post-activation—for instance, surface areas around 995 m²/g for pristine samples—demonstrating that the protocol efficiently opens the MOF pores without destabilizing the framework.

Overall, the combination of a controlled temperature of 120°C, vacuum conditions, and precise equipment ensures that the activated MOF materials exhibit both high porosity and robust structural integrity, key to their exceptional performance in subsequent applications.

### 5.2 Application/Performance Evaluation

The performance evaluation of the MOF-based electrolyte focused on parameters critical for battery operation, addressing the influence of temperature on ionic conductivity, the configuration of coin cells, and key electrochemical indicators.

#### 5.2.1 Test Temperature and Ionic Conductivity
Temperature plays a pivotal role in ion mobility within the MOF electrolyte. The evaluation was conducted across a wide temperature range to reliably capture its performance. Specifically, conductivity measurements revealed values of 0.83 mS/cm at −40 °C, 2.75 mS/cm at 25 °C, and 7.9 mS/cm at 120 °C. These results demonstrate that ionic conductivity increases as temperature rises, confirming enhanced Zn²⁺ transport at elevated temperatures. Alternating current (AC) impedance measurements were employed, with each temperature condition maintained for one hour to ensure thermal equilibrium, thereby guaranteeing the reproducibility of the results.

#### 5.2.2 Cell Configuration and Battery Testing
Battery performance was assessed using CR2025 coin cells. In these tests, the MOF-based electrolyte was paired with a polished Zn anode and an appropriate cathode (e.g., NH₄V₄O₁₀). The cell assembly featured careful electrode preparation and conditioning, including a resting period of 6 hours at 30 °C prior to galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) measurements. At a current density of 0.3 A/g, the battery delivered an initial capacity of 497 mAh/g. The cycle tests further confirmed excellent long-term stability, maintaining near-100% capacity retention over 2500 cycles and demonstrating high Coulombic efficiency.

#### 5.2.3 Zn²⁺ Transference and Activation Energy
The Zn²⁺ transference number was quantified using potentiostatic polarization methods, yielding a value of 0.90. This high transference number underlines the efficiency of Zn²⁺ transport through the anionic framework. Additionally, the activation energy was measured to be 0.19 eV, a low value that supports rapid ion migration even at lower temperatures. These parameters collectively highlight the material’s robustness and suitability for high-performance rechargeable zinc batteries.

Overall, the evaluation protocol—encompassing strict control of temperature, current density, and cell configuration—provides a clear, reproducible framework for assessing the performance of MOF-based electrolytes in practical battery applications.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of the MOF materials were determined using single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). These techniques are essential for confirming the framework topology, space group, and the absence of impurities.
- **Findings:** SC-XRD analysis of the pristine MOF (1) revealed a tetragonal I4/m space group with unit cell parameters a = b = 15.3170(3) Å, c = 27.7377(6) Å, and α = β = γ = 90°. PXRD patterns for both 1 and the Zn²⁺-exchanged MOF (1Zn) closely matched the simulated patterns derived from SC-XRD data, with no additional peaks observed.
- **Observations:** The retention of the tetragonal I4/m structure and the absence of impurity peaks after ion exchange confirm high phase purity and successful preservation of the framework during Zn²⁺ incorporation.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were evaluated using N₂ adsorption isotherms at 77 K, with BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) analysis for surface area and NLDFT (nonlocal density functional theory) for pore size distribution. These parameters are critical for assessing the accessibility and capacity of the MOF channels.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area was 995 m²/g for 1 and 667 m²/g for 1Zn. Pore size distribution analysis indicated regular pores of approximately 8.6 Å for 1, with the framework featuring a large dodecahedral cage (14.0 × 18.2 × 20.4 Å³). After Zn²⁺ exchange, the surface area decreased, consistent with partial pore occupation.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and well-defined porosity are retained after ion exchange, supporting the framework’s suitability for ion transport applications.

#### 6.1.3 Thermal Stability
- **Description:** Thermal stability was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) in open air and variable temperature PXRD (VT-XRD), which monitor weight loss and crystallinity as a function of temperature.
- **Findings:** The MOF frameworks maintained their crystallinity and structural integrity up to 300 °C. Decomposition and significant weight loss occurred above this temperature.
- **Observations:** The robust thermal stability up to 300 °C ensures the MOF’s applicability in demanding electrochemical environments.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** Morphology and elemental distribution were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and SEM-EDS (energy dispersive spectroscopy) mapping. These analyses provide insights into crystal shape, size uniformity, and the spatial distribution of key elements.
- **Findings:** SEM images showed well-defined, uniformly sized crystals for both 1 and 1Zn. SEM-EDS mapping of 1Zn demonstrated homogeneous distribution of Zr and Zn throughout the crystals, indicating successful and uniform ion exchange.
- **Observations:** The pressed 1Zn powder exhibited a densely packed morphology, and post-cycling analysis revealed clean, dendrite-free surfaces, highlighting the material’s morphological stability.

#### 6.1.5 Chemical Composition and Ligand Integrity
- **Description:** Chemical composition was determined by ICP-MS and elemental analysis, while ligand integrity and ion exchange were probed by solid-state NMR (⁷Li and ¹¹B MAS NMR).
- **Findings:** ICP-MS confirmed a Zn:B:Zr molar ratio of approximately 0.47:1:3.11 in 1Zn, with residual Li⁺ below 0.05 wt%. Solid-state ⁷Li NMR showed the disappearance of the Li⁺ signal after exchange, and ¹¹B NMR confirmed the ligand remained intact.
- **Observations:** These results verify efficient Zn²⁺ incorporation, minimal residual Li⁺, and preservation of the framework’s chemical structure during and after ion exchange.



### 6.2 Performance Results

#### 6.2.1 Zn²⁺ Ionic Conductivity
- **Description:** Ionic conductivity (σ) quantifies the ability of the MOF-based electrolyte to transport Zn²⁺ ions, a critical parameter for battery performance. It is typically measured in millisiemens per centimeter (mS cm⁻¹) using AC impedance spectroscopy.
- **Experimental Data:** The Zn²⁺-exchanged MOF (1Zn) exhibited conductivities of 0.83 mS cm⁻¹ at −40 °C, 2.75 mS cm⁻¹ at 25 °C, and 7.9 mS cm⁻¹ at 120 °C. These values were obtained from pressed pellets of hydrated 1Zn (12.8 wt% water) sandwiched between Zn electrodes.
- **Observations and Trends:** Conductivity increased with temperature, demonstrating thermally activated Zn²⁺ transport. The high conductivity across a wide temperature range highlights the suitability of 1Zn for solid-state zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs) operating under diverse conditions.

#### 6.2.2 Zn²⁺ Transference Number
- **Description:** The Zn²⁺ transference number (t_Zn²⁺) represents the fraction of total ionic current carried by Zn²⁺ ions, calculated as \( t_{Zn^{2+}} = \frac{I_{Zn^{2+}}}{I_{total}} \).
- **Experimental Data:** The 1Zn electrolyte achieved a high transference number of 0.90.
- **Observations and Trends:** This value indicates that 90% of the ionic current is due to Zn²⁺ migration, minimizing polarization and suppressing dendrite formation, which are essential for stable and efficient battery operation.

#### 6.2.3 Activation Energy for Ion Transport
- **Description:** Activation energy (Ea) reflects the energy barrier for Zn²⁺ migration within the MOF, determined from Arrhenius plots of conductivity versus temperature.
- **Experimental Data:** The activation energy for Zn²⁺ conduction in 1Zn was measured as 0.19 eV.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low Ea supports rapid Zn²⁺ transport and high conductivity, contributing to the electrolyte’s superior rate performance.

#### 6.2.4 Electrochemical Performance in Zinc-Ion Batteries
- **Description:** Key battery metrics include reversible capacity (mAh g⁻¹), rate capability (capacity at various current densities), and cycling stability (capacity retention over repeated cycles).
- **Experimental Data:** In CR2025 coin cells (Zn|1Zn|NH₄V₄O₁₀), the initial capacity reached 497 mAh g⁻¹ at 0.3 A g⁻¹. At 5 A g⁻¹, the battery delivered 349 mAh g⁻¹ and retained 99.7% of its capacity after 2500 cycles, with Coulombic efficiency approaching 100%. Even at extreme rates (10 A g⁻¹ at −20 °C, 20 A g⁻¹ at −10 °C), capacity retention exceeded 94% after 1000 cycles.
- **Observations and Trends:** The MOF-based electrolyte enabled robust rate performance and exceptional long-term cycling stability, outperforming many benchmark materials.

#### 6.2.5 Interfacial Compatibility and Structural Stability
- **Description:** Interfacial compatibility assesses the stability and uniformity of the electrolyte–electrode interface, while structural stability ensures the MOF maintains crystallinity and porosity during operation.
- **Experimental Data:** Post-cycling SEM and PXRD analyses confirmed smooth Zn anodes and intact MOF structure. BET surface area remained high (561 m² g⁻¹ after cycling), and no dendrite formation was observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** These results demonstrate excellent interfacial compatibility and structural robustness, supporting the electrolyte’s long-term performance in ZIBs.
