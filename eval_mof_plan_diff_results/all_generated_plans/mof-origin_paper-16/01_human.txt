# **An elastic metal–organic crystal with a densely catenated backbone**

<https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03880-x>

Received: 25 February 2021

Accepted: 6 August 2021

Published online: 13 October 2021

 [C](http://crossmark.crossref.org/dialog/?doi=10.1038/s41586-021-03880-x&domain=pdf)heck for updates

**Wenjing Meng1,8,9, Shun Kondo2,9, Takuji Ito3 , Kazuki Komatsu4 , Jenny Pirillo5 , Yuh Hijikata5 , Yuichi Ikuhara2 , Takuzo Aida1,6** ✉ **& Hiroshi Sato1,6,7** ✉

What particular mechanical properties can be expected for materials composed of interlocked backbones has been a long-standing issue in materials science since the frst reports on polycatenane and polyrotaxane in the 1970[s1](#page-4-0)[–3](#page-4-1) . Here we report a three-dimensional porous metal–organic crystal, which is exceptional in that its warps and wefts are connected only by catenation. This porous crystal is composed of a tetragonal lattice and dynamically changes its geometry upon guest molecule release, uptake and exchange, and also upon temperature variation even in a low temperature range. We indente[d4](#page-4-2) the crystal along its *a*/*b* axes and obtained the Young's moduli of 1.77 ± 0.16 GPa in *N*,*N*-dimethylformamide and 1.63 ± 0.13 GPa in tetrahydrofuran, which are the lowest among those reported so far for porous metal– organic crystal[s5](#page-4-3) . To our surprise, hydrostatic compression showed that this elastic porous crystal was the most deformable along its *c* axis, where 5% contraction occurred without structural deterioration upon compression up to 0.88 GPa. The crystal structure obtained at 0.46 GPa showed that the catenated macrocycles move translationally upon contraction. We anticipate our mechanically interlocked molecule-based design to be a starting point for the development of porous materials with exotic mechanical properties. For example, squeezable porous crystals that may address an essential difculty in realizing both high abilities of guest uptake and release are on the horizon.

A variety of mechanically interlocked structures have been found in nature[6](#page-4-4) , of which the geometries and large potential to exert unique functions are of major scientific interest. Historically, the concept of 'chemical topology' was introduced for the first time in 1961 and afforded a new challenge in chemistry[7](#page-4-5) . Unlike covalently constructed molecules, mechanically interlocked molecules (MIMs) such as catenanes[8](#page-4-6),[9](#page-4-7) and rotaxanes[10](#page-4-8),[11](#page-4-9) bear spatially restricted freedom of motion[12](#page-4-10),[13](#page-4-11), and a wide variety of synthetic molecular machines and switches based on MIMs have been developed so far[14](#page-4-12)[,15.](#page-4-13) Meanwhile, since the first success in the synthesis of polycatenane and polyrotaxane in the 1970[s1](#page-4-0)[–3](#page-4-1) , such mechanically interlocked polymers have attracted particular attentio[n16](#page-4-14)–[21](#page-4-15) as their densely populated mechanical bonds are expected to move coherently along the main chains, providing anomalous mechanical properties[22](#page-4-16),[23](#page-4-17) (Supplementary Table 1). Although peculiar mechanical properties are yet to be reported, precision synthesis of poly[*n*]catenanes, whose chains are composed solely of catenanes, has been impressiv[e19.](#page-4-18) We envisaged that if the backbone of a metal–organic crystal were composed of a catenane structure, such a porous crystal might exhibit rubber-like elastic properties. This idea may address the dilemma of porous materials: the higher the guest uptake ability, the less easily guest release occurs. Although most practically useful porous materials are mechanically robust[24](#page-4-19)[,25,](#page-4-20) elastic porous materials, if available, could be mechanically squeezable for the forcing of guest molecules out of the pores. Several different metal–organic crystals[26](#page-4-21),[27](#page-4-22) carrying MIMs as backbone pendants[28](#page-4-23)[–35](#page-5-0) have been reported, exploring their rotar[y32](#page-5-1),[33,](#page-5-2)[35](#page-5-0) and shuttlin[g34](#page-5-3) motions in the solid state. However, as for metal–organic crystals composed of MIM-based backbones, only a few examples have been reported without exploration of their mechanical properties[36–](#page-5-4)[39.](#page-5-5)

Here, we report a porous metal–organic crystal, [2]catenane-based metal–organic framework (CTNMOF), that is exceptional in that all of its warps and wefts are three-dimensionally (3D) connected only by catenation (Fig. [1b](#page-1-0)), and it can reversibly deform its crystalline framework in response to an externally applied pressure. The crystal adopts a thin rectangular shape, which technically allows for compression only along the crystallographic *a*/*b* axes (crystallographically equivalent to each other), producing Young's moduli of 1.77 ± 0.16 GPa in *N*,*N*-dimethylformamide (DMF) and 1.63 ± 0.13 GPa in tetrahydrofuran (THF). These values are among the smallest of the metal–organic crystals so far reported[5](#page-4-3) , whereas the crystal, when placed under a high hydrostatic pressure, predominantly contracts along the crystallographic *c* axis. For the synthesis of CTNMOF, we designed a [2] catenane-base[d40](#page-5-6) ligand, H4 CTNL, (Fig. [1a](#page-1-0), Supplementary Figs. 9–15 and 18), where each of its catenated benzylamide macrocycles carries

Unlike the catenated macrocycles in the H4 CTNL crystal (Supplementary Fig. 19), those in CTNMOF all adopt a boat conformation, where two pairs of intramolecular hydrogen bonds are formed between the benzylamide groups. As a result, the crystal composed of the mechanically interlocked warps and wefts carried 3D-interconnected channels (Supplementary Fig. 25) with cross-sectional areas along the *c* and *a*/*b* axes of 6.2 × 6.2 Å<sup>2</sup> (Supplementary Fig. 21c) and 4.5 × 4.0 Å<sup>2</sup> (Fig. [1c\)](#page-1-0), respectively. Accordingly, CTNMOF was able to accommodate gaseous molecules into its crystalline channels. For example, its CO2 adsorption isotherm at −78 °C (Supplementary Fig. 41) showed the presence of a permanent microporous structure with a CO2 sorption capacity of 65 ml STP g−1 at *P*/*P*0 = 0.98.

Crystalline CTNMOF is structurally dynami[c42](#page-5-8) and undergoes geometrical changes upon guest release, uptake and exchange. As-synthesized CTNMOF in DMF included DMF molecules as guests in its interconnected channels. As observed by thermogravimetric analysis (Supplementary Fig. 39), CTNMOF lost its included DMF molecules upon heating to 100 °C. Notably, guest-free CTNMOF thus obtained showed a high thermal stability and survived up to a temperature of nearly 360 °C without decomposition. Our motivation for the synthesis of crystalline CTNMOF was to explore the mechanical properties of metal–organic crystals with densely catenated backbones. Encouraged by the dynamic and adaptive nature of crystalline CTNMOF described above, we investigated its deformation properties by means of nanoindentation. To confirm the maximum load that can be applied to the crystal for the nanoindentation test, CTNMOF was subjected to a fracture test, where a fatal fracture of the crystal occurred when a load of 33 mN was applied to a CTNMOF single crystal (Supplementary Fig. 30). For the nanoindentation test, a single crystal of CTNMOF was placed on a low melting-point (47 °C) alloy firmly attached to a glass plate (Supplementary Fig. 29a). Because the CTNMOF crystal adopted a thin rectangular shape, only pressing along the *a*/*b* axes was technically possible. We pressed the crystal surface at 30 °C using a triangular pyramidal Berkovich diamond tip and released to obtain the loading and unloading mechanical features (Supplementary Fig. 32, Supplementary Table 17b), respectively (Fig. [4a,](#page-3-1) red trace). From the unloading curve, the Young's modulus of CTNMOF was determined to be 6.31 ± 0.14 GPa (Fig. [4a](#page-3-1), red trace). By contrast, the Young's moduli of HKUST-1 (Supplementary Table 20) evaluated in air (Fig. [4c](#page-3-1), red trace, Young's modulus: 10.65 ± 0.11 GPa) and DMF (Fig. [4c,](#page-3-1) blue trace, Young's modulus: 10.24 ± 0.33 GPa) differed only slightly from each other. Also, air-dried Zn-MOF (Fig. [4e](#page-3-1), red trace, Young's modulus: 2.04 ± 0.26 GPa), when soaked in DMF, even became 1.5 times less deformable (Fig. [4e](#page-3-1), blue trace, Young's modulus: 3.03 ± 0.35 GPa, Supplementary Table 21). However, to our surprise, no indentation was left behind on the pressed parts of the CTNMOF surface (Fig. [4b](#page-3-1)). This observation may reflect the elastic nature of CTNMOF.

Upon immersion in THF of DMF-including as-synthesized CTNMOF, we successfully obtained THF-including CTNMOF (Supplementary Fig. 28), whose load–displacement curve indicated an elastic nature with the Young's modulus of 1.63 ± 0.13 GPa (Supplementary Fig. 34). Again, the vibrational band due to the hydrogen-bonded amide moieties of CTNMOF showed a 13 cm−1 shift from 1,631 cm−1 in air to 1,644 cm−1 in THF (Supplementary Fig. 37). Upon immersion in an additional 15 hydrogen bond competing and non-competing solvents, as-synthesized CTNMOF cracked, thereby making the nanoindentation test impossible. Therefore, we performed molecular dynamics simulations for a [2] catenane in hexane (Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45) as a representative of non-hydrogen bond competing solvents as well as in DMF (Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43) and gas phase (Supplementary Figs. 46 and 47), to explore how the hydrogen bonds between the catenated macrocycles in gas phase are modulated by solvation. The results clearly indicate that the hydrogen bonds are not attenuated in hexane but partially cleaved in DMF.

In relation to the above-mentioned nanoindentation experiments (Supplementary Fig. 31), we tested the behaviour of CTNMOF by applying a high isotropic pressure in a hydrostatic medium using a diamond anvil cell[47,](#page-5-13)[48.](#page-5-14) For this test, a single crystal of DMF-containing CTNMOF was placed in a diamond anvil cell with a diameter of 400 µm (Supplementary Fig. 26), and pressure-induced structural changes were investigated by in situ SCXRD. Unexpectedly, this elastic porous crystal was the most deformable along the *c* axis, rather than the *a*/*b* axes tested for nanoindentation: upon compression up to 0.88 GPa, 5% contraction occurred along the *c* axis without structural deterioration, whereas less than 0.4% contraction occurred along the *a*/*b* axes (Fig. [4g,](#page-3-1) Supplementary Tables 13–15). The crystal structure obtained at 0.46 GPa revealed that the observed anisotropic structural change was caused by the mutual sliding motions of the catenated macrocycles along the *c* axis, where the distance between the interlocked arms was extended by about 3%, resulting in 5% anisotropic contraction along the *c* axis (Supplementary Table 14 and Supplementary Fig. 27). Meanwhile, in THF, we found that THF-including CTNMOF showed a pressure-induced anisotropic deformation analogous to DMF-including CTNMOF in DMF (Supplementary Table 16) despite a smaller level of deformation than that of DMF-including CTNMOF in DMF. For spectroscopic understanding of this pressure-induced deformation, we carried out in situ Fourier transform infrared) spectroscopy of CTNMOF to investigate how the applied pressure affects the behaviour of the NHCO hydrogen bonds in the catenated macrocycles. For the guest-including CTNMOF, we chose the one including THF (Supplementary Fig. 28), rather than DMF, because included DMF interferes with the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 38a, the vibrational band due to the hydrogen-bonded NHCO groups showed an expected shift to a higher wavenumber region upon increasing the applied pressure to 2.4 GPa. By contrast, dried CTNMOF, upon being pressurized up to 2.1 GPa, did not show any shift in the NHCO vibrational band (Supplementary Fig. 38b).

Although the first polycatenane and polyrotaxane were synthesized more than 40 years ago, mechanical properties of such MIM-based materials have been little explored to date, because 3D materials that are densely composed of MIMs are very rar[e36.](#page-5-4) CTNMOF, as one of the first backbone-catenated 3D metal–organic crystals[39,](#page-5-5) allows for switching between the mechanically rigid and elastic states by solvent-assisted modulation of the H-bonding lock of the MIM parts. If the elastic properties can be further enhanced by elaborating the MIM design, a squeezable MOF may be realized that can address the dilemma of porous materials where highly affinitive guests are not easily released.

## **Article**

### **Methods**

#### **SCXRD**

Analysis for H4 CTNL and Me4 CTNL was performed using a Rigaku model VariMax Dual diffractometer equipped with a fine-focus sealed-tube X-ray source (*λ* = 0.71075 Å) and a confocal graphite monochromator. Data were collected and processed using CrystalClear (Rigaku[\)49.](#page-6-0) The analysis for CTNMOF was performed using a Rigaku XtaLAB P200 diffractometer with Mo-Kα radiation. Data were collected and processed using CrysAlisPro (Rigaku Oxford Diffraction)[50](#page-6-1). All data were corrected for Lorentzian and polarization effects. The structure was solved by direct methods[51](#page-6-2),[52](#page-6-3) expanded using Fourier techniques. The non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically. Hydrogen atoms were refined using the riding model. The final cycle of fullmatrix least-squares refinement (SHELXL97: ∑*w*(*F*<sup>o</sup> 2  − *F*<sup>c</sup> 2 ) 2 where *w* is least-squares weights) on *F*<sup>2</sup> was converged (the largest parameter shift was 0.00 times its estimated standard deviation (esd)) with unweighted and weighted agreement factors calculated based on the following equations: *R*1 = ∑||*F*o| − |*F*c||/∑|*F*o| and *wR*2 = {∑[*w*(*F*<sup>o</sup> 2  − *F*<sup>c</sup> 2 ) 2 ]/ ∑[*w*(*F*<sup>o</sup> 2 ) 2 ]}½. Neutral atom scattering factors were taken from table 6.1.1.4 of ref. [53.](#page-6-4) Anomalous dispersion effects were included in *F*calc (calculated structure factor) (ref. [54](#page-6-5)); the values for ∆*f*′ (the real part of the anomalous-scattering factor) and ∆*f*″ (the imaginary part of the anomalous-scattering factor) were taken from table 4.2.6.8 of ref. [55.](#page-6-6) The values for the mass attenuation coefficients were taken from table 4.2.4.3 of ref. [56](#page-6-7). All calculations were performed using the Crystal-Structur[e57](#page-6-8) crystallographic software package, except for refinement, which was performed using SHELXL9[758.](#page-6-9) The heavily disordered solvent molecules were not modelled, and the SQUEEZE protocol inside PLA-TON[41](#page-5-7) was used to remove the void electron density at the end of the structure refinement. The crystallographic data have been deposited with the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Center (CCDC) (2080330– 2080336, 2080245–2080247, 2080270). Details about structures and their corresponding CCDC numbers are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

#### **PXRD**

PXRD measurements were carried out using a Rigaku model RINT-ULTIMA III X-ray powder diffractometer equipped with a Cu sealed tube (*λ* = 1.54056 Å), which was operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The PXRD data were collected in a 2*θ* range of 3° to 30° by using a step-scan mode with a step size of 0.02°. The powder sample was placed in a borosilicate glass capillary (WJM-Glas/Müller) with a diameter of 0.5 mm. For experiments shown in Fig. [2a–c,](#page-2-0) a small amount of the sample was taken for PXRD measurement at each step, namely, pristine, heated in vacuo and resoaked. For the experiments shown in Fig. [2d, e,](#page-2-0) all of the spectra were obtained from the same sample within a capillary. The solvent was added to/removed from the sample by centrifugation (3,000*g*, 2 min).

#### **Thermogravimetric analysis**

Thermogravimetric analysis was conducted using a Mettler-Toledo model TGA/SDTA851<sup>e</sup> in a temperature range from 25 °C to 600 °C at a heating rate of 1 °C min−1 under N2 with a flow rate of 20 ml min−1.

#### **Gas sorption**

A CO2 (99.995%) sorption experiment was conducted by using an automatic volumetric adsorption apparatus (BELSORP-max12-G-SP; MicrotracBEL) equipped with a cryostat system at −78 °C. A weighed sample (26 mg) of powdery CTNMOF was placed into a metallic copper cell and then kept under high vacuum for 6 h at 80 °C to remove the guest molecules. The sample cell was then placed into a cryostat system and evacuated at <10−2 Pa. The internal pressure change was monitored, and the amount of CO2 adsorbed was evaluated at each equilibrium point.

#### **Nanoindentation**

Experiments were carried out using an ENT-NEXUS (ELIONIX) instrument that is capable of performing nanoindentation tests in both dry and liquid environments. Nanoindentations were performed under the load-control mode at 30 °C. The CTNMOF crystals were fixed onto a glass plate using a Bi-based fusible alloy (Osaka Asahi U-47) to rigidly fix the specimens, even in DMF or THF. A diamond indenter tip with a Berkovich geometry was used. A maximum load value of 15 mN (except for Zn-MOF, where a maximum load of 8 mN (ref. [44](#page-5-10)) was applied) and a loading/unloading rate of 1.5 mN s−1 was used for all of the experiments. The holding time at the maximum load was set to be 5 s. The indentation moduli were obtained from the load–displacement curves using the standard Oliver–Pharr method[59](#page-6-10), where we assumed a Poisson's ratio of 0.433 for the specimen[44](#page-5-10).

#### **In situ high-pressure SCXRD**

In situ SCXRD measurements under high pressure were performed using a Rigaku model Synergy custom diffractometer with a hybrid photon-counting detector (HyPix-6000HE). The X-ray source was a rotating Mo anode (*λ* = 0.7107 Å, Micromax-007), and the incident X-ray was focused on the sample position using a Rigaku model VariMax Mo confocal mirror. The single-crystal sample was loaded into a diamond anvil cell made of CuBe alloy equipping Boehler-Almax-type diamond anvils with a culet size of 800 µm and an opening angle of 40°. The gasket was a plate of CuBe alloy with a hole of 400 µm diameter and 200 µm thickness. DMF or THF was used as a pressure-transmitting medium. The sample pressures were estimated by a ruby fluorescence method[60](#page-7-0). The diffraction data were analysed using CrysAlisPro (Rigaku Oxford Diffraction[\)50](#page-6-1) and the obtained cell parameters.

#### **In situ high-pressure Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy**

THF-containing CTNMOF and air-dried CTNMOF were used. THF-containing CTNMOF was prepared from as-synthesized CTNMOF by washing the crystals with THF several times and then soaking in THF for 24 h. CTNMOF and KBr were ground to fine powder in a mortar with a pestle. When THF-containing CTNMOF was used, THF was added to the sample mixture to prevent it from drying out. For THF-containing CTNMOF, an Inconel gasket was used with a hole of 300 µm diameter and 100 µm thickness. The gasket used for air-dried CTNMOF was a plate of CuBe alloy with a hole of 400 µm diameter and 200 µm thickness. A pair of Boehler-Almax-type conical diamonds with 400 µm culet were used as anvils. The sample pressures were estimated by a ruby fluorescence metho[d60.](#page-7-0) Spectra were collected using a Bruker Hyperion 1000 infrared microscope.

#### **2. Synthesis**

2.1. Synthesis of the ligand

![](_page_5_Figure_2.jpeg)

The synthesis of compound 5-(methoxycarbonyl)isophthalic acid was adapted from a literature procedure3 :

Trimethyl benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylate (25.4 g, 0.10 mol, 1 equiv.) and NaOH solution (0.5 M in MeOH, 400 mL, 0.2 mol, 2 equiv.) were mixed in a 500 mL round-bottomed flask. The resulting mixture was heated under reflux at 80 °C for 1 hour and then stirred at 20 °C overnight. Then, the solvent was removed; the residue was dissolved in water and acidified by 1 M HCl first to pH 6. The resulting solution was extracted with EtOAc to remove the mono-acid byproduct. The remaining aqueous solution was subsequently acidified to pH 4 and extracted with EtOAc five times. The organics were combined, dried over MgSO4 and concentrated. The solid precipitate was filtered and dried under reduced pressure to afford the desired product as a white solid (12.6 g, 55%).

1 H NMR (500 MHz, DMSO-*d*6): *δ* (ppm) 8.65 (t, *J* = 1.0 Hz, 1H, Ph-*H para* to CO2Me), 8.63 (d, *J* = 2.0 Hz, 1H, Ph-*H ortho* to CO2Me), 3.92 (s, 3H, C*H*3). 13C NMR (126 MHz, DMSO): *δ* (ppm) 165.75, 164.86, 133.91, 133.37, 132.07, 130.67, 52.70.

![](_page_5_Figure_6.jpeg)

5-(Methoxycarbonyl)isophthalic acid (4 g, 17.8 mmol, 1 equiv.) was dissolved in anhydrous THF (15 mL) in a preoven-dried two-necked 50 mL round-bottomed flask. Ten drops of anhydrous DMF were added to the resulting mixture. Separately, oxalyl chloride (3.5 mL, 41.0 mmol, 2.3 equiv.) was mixed with anhydrous THF (5 mL) in a preoven-dried two-necked 10 mL round-bottomed flask. The mixture containing oxalyl chloride was added dropwise *via* a syringe to the THF solution of 5(methoxycarbonyl)isophthalic acid prepared as described above. The reaction mixture was stirred at 20 °C for 1 hour before removing the volatile content under reduced pressure. The residue was washed with a small amount of anhydrous toluene and then anhydrous DCM to afford the desired product, methyl 3,5-bis(chlorocarbonyl)benzoate, (3.98 g, 85%) as an off-white solid. The product was used for the next step without further purification immediately after isolation.

1 H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3): *δ* (ppm) 9.02 (d, *J* = 1.5 Hz, 1H, Ph-*H ortho* to CO2Me), 8.98 (t, *J* = 2.0 Hz, 1H, Ph-*H para* to CO2Me), 4.03 (s, 3H, C*H*3). 13C NMR (126 MHz, CDCl3): *δ* (ppm) 166.76, 164.16, 137.58, 136.77, 135.08, 132.76, 53.33.

The synthesis of Me4 **CTNL** was adapted from a literature procedure4,5:

A solution of methyl 3,5-bis(chlorocarbonyl)benzoate in anhydrous CHCl3 (amylene added 150 ppm, 230 mL, 33 mM) and a solution of 1,4-phenylenedimethanamine in anhydrous CHCl3 (amylene added 150 ppm, 230 mL, 33 mM, 1 equiv.) were added to a solution of freshly distilled triethylamine in anhydrous CHCl3 (amylene added 150 ppm, 4.2 mL in 230 mL CHCl3, 4 equiv.) *via* a syringe pump over 1 hour. The reaction mixture was stirred at 20 °C for 12 hours. The resulting mixture was then filtered, and the filtrate was concentrated and subjected to silica gel column chromatography (1% to 4% v/v MeOH in CH2Cl2 as the eluent). MALDI-TOF-MS: *m*/*z* found: 1319.433 ([M + Na+] calcd: 1319.434).

The synthesis of H4 **CTNL**:

Me4 **CTNL** (123 mg, 0.099 mmol, 1 equiv.) was dissolved in DMF (3 mL) and diluted with MeOH (3 mL), and aqueous NaOH solution (5% w/v 1.5 mL, 19 equiv.) was added to the resulting mixture. The resulting reaction mixture was placed above a 90 °C oil bath and stirred for 12 hours. The volatile content was removed under reduced pressure; the residue was dissolved in water and acidified to pH 2 with 1 M HCl. The precipitate was filtered, washed with water, and dried in vacuo to afford the product as a white solid (117 mg, 82%) (Supplementary Figs. 9–15 and 17). Crystals suitable for single crystal X-ray analysis were obtained by diffusion of water vapor into a DMF solution of H4 **CTNL** (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 3).

An alternative method can be used based on the reported procedure6 : Me4 **CTNL** (65 mg, 0.05 mmol, 1 equiv.) was dissolved in DMF (1.5 mL) in a 30 mL round bottom flask. The residue was diluted with a small amount of water and then the mixture was acidified using diluted HCl to pH 1. #### 2.2. Synthesis of **CTNMOF**

Equal volumes of a solution of H4 **CTNL** (50 mM in DMF, 100 *μ*L) and a solution of Co(NO3)2·6H2O (400 mM in DMF, 100 *μ*L) were mixed in a glass tube. The resulting mixture was heated at 90 °C for 18 hours. Dark-emerald-green needle-shaped crystals were formed, which were subsequently isolated by filtration (8 mg, 90% based on the formula obtained in the elemental analysis). The green crystal quickly turned pink in the air, which is attributed to the absorption of water. Elemental analysis for the sample dried under vacuum at room temperature for 2 hours: Found: C, 54.32; H, 5.48; N, 9.53%. Calc. for C68H52Co2N8O16·7H2O·4DMF(C3H7NO): C, 54.18; H, 5.34; N, 9.48%.

Note on the colour change of **CTNMOF**: If exposed to air for a while, as-synthesized **CTNMOF** crystals will become pink. A colour change from green to pink will also occur when as-synthesized crystals are immersed in a solvent such as acetonitrile and hexane. When as-synthesized **CTNMOF** crystals are dried under vacuum, they become purple.

#### **7. Nanoindentation**

For nanoindentation experiments, a flat, smooth crystal surface is necessary. The single crystallinity of metal–organic crystals with guest-filling pores is highly sensitive to guest (solvent) exchange. **CTNMOF** is highly porous and tends to crack upon solvent exchange. Therefore, it is highly challenging to conduct nanoindentation experiments in different solvents. After many trials with 17 different solvents (i.e. DMF, acetone, acetonitrile, acetyl acetone, ethyl acetate, *n*-hexane, toluene, 1,4-dioxane, 1,2-dichloroethane, 2 butanone, cyclohexanone, methylcyclohexane, chloroform, dichloromethane, dimethylacetamide, nitromethane, and THF), we found that, in only DMF and THF, **CTNMOF** was able to maintain its single crystallinity; therefore, the crystals immersed in DMF or THF were subjected to nanoindentation.

Air-dried **CTNMOF** was prepared by placing freshly prepared **CTNMOF** onto a watch glass that was covered by another watch glass inside a fume hood for several days until all the crystals turned pink. Crystals without cracks on their surface are selected for the experiment. N.B. Crystals are easily cracked during the drying process; therefore, slow evaporation of the solvent could be helpful to maintain their quality.

Crystals of as-synthesized **CTNMOF** were used for the experiments performed in DMF.

For the experiments performed in THF, as-synthesized **CTNMOF** was fixed on to a glass plate using a Bi-based fusible alloy (Osaka Asahi Co., Ltd. U-47), and then it was soaked in THF for 24 hours before the measurement.

#### **12. Computational studies**

#### 1. Model construction for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

For MD simulation, we constructed the model structure consisting of catenane moiety, where the four carboxylate groups originally coordinated to the cobalt atoms in the MOF were replaced with methyl groups. Three systems were prepared to perform MD simulation in gas phase, in hexane, and in DMF. The model was placed at the center of 8 Å cubic box for gas phase simulation, while the model was solvated by 1693 hexane molecules or 3997 DMF molecules in the cases of simulation in solutions.

#### 2. Details of MD simulations

First, we performed quantum mechanical calculations at HF/6-31G\* level of theory by Gaussian 16 Rev. C.013 software to obtain atomic charges. The topology files of the model and solvent were generated by the antechamber package14 with GAFF15 force field. Amber topology files generated by the package were converted into a GROMACS format using the python script, Acpype16 . Second, equilibration of the systems in gas phase and in the solvents was conducted by following procedure. Energy minimization of the systems was performed based on the conjugate gradient algorithm with the threshold of the maximum force, 10.0 kJ/mol/nm. Next, NVT equilibration for 100 ps at 300 K followed by NPT ensemble equilibration for 100 ps at 1 bar pressure were carried out for relaxation of solvent molecules, while the atomic positions of the model are restrained with a harmonic potential. Third, the final MD simulations were performed without any restraints and NPT ensemble. The temperature and pressure are kept constant (300K and 1 bar) by using the velocity rescale thermostat17 and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat18 , respectively. The cutoff value of 14 Å was assigned for non-bonded interactions. Longrange electrostatic interactions were treated using a particle-mesh Ewald scheme19 . Dynamics were propagated with a leapfrog integrator using a time step of 0.2 fs, while preserving bond lengths using LINCS20. All simulations were carried out under periodic boundary conditions using GROMACS software version 2020.521 . To analyze the results, we used GROMACS inbuilt tools, where we defined that the hydrogen bond has shorter N-O distance than 3.5 Å and smaller angle of N-H-O than 30°. The MD simulation for 2000 ps was conducted, and the snapshots every 1ps were collected. Then, the analysis of the hydrogen bonds in each structure the series of the snapshots were analyzed to summarize the number of the hydrogen bonds between the macrocycles (Supplementary Figs. 42, 44 and 46). Typical snapshots of the simulations in gas phase, in hexane and in DMF are shown as Supplementary Figs. 43, 45 and 47.
