ABSTRACT: Metal−organic frameworks (MOFs) are well-known porous materials owing to their useful adsorption properties; however, some MOFs have limited adsorption capabilities, which can significantly undermine their success as porous materials. Therefore, maximizing their porosity is critical for unlocking their full potential and expanding their practical utilization, such as gas storage, separation, and removal. In this study, flexible MOFs with defined defects were synthesized using a ligand-mixing strategy to improve their porosity and maximize their adsorption capacities. Specifically, we employed a combination of two organic linkers, 4,4′ biphenyldicarboxylic acid (H2BPDC) and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (H2BDC), in various ratios, to fabricate flexible In-MIL-53D hybrids containing controllable defects within the structure due to the

![](_page_0_Picture_9.jpeg)

incorporation of the short linker (H2BDC) compared to the original linker (H2BPDC). These structural defects in the In-MIL-53D hybrids activated their gate-openings and enhanced gas adsorption capacities for N2 and CO2. Moreover, the gate-opened activated hybrids exhibited excellent adsorption capacity for the harmful chemical warfare agent simulant, 2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide (CEES). However, excessive incorporation of defects disrupted the framework's integrity, compromising its stability and increasing the risk of collapse. Therefore, achieving an optimal level of defect incorporation is essential to balance structural stability with enhanced functionality. Among the hybrids, the sample with approximately 39% incorporation of the short linker exhibited up to an 11-fold increase in adsorption capacity for CO2 at 1 *P*/*P*0. In addition, this hybrid demonstrated up to 5-fold higher CEES adsorption capacity compared to the pristine In-MIL-53D, highlighting its potential for advanced utilization in relevant fields.

# ■ **INTRODUCTION**

Metal−organic frameworks (MOFs) have garnered significant attention because of their exceptional porosity, high surface area, and versatile structural design, which make them invaluable for a wide range of applications, including gas storage, separation and removal, catalysis, and drug delivery.[1](#page-8-0)−[14](#page-8-0) MOFs with outstanding adsorption properties are critically important for the selective removal of toxic chemicals that pose severe risks to human health and the environment.[5](#page-8-0)−[9](#page-8-0) Despite their potential, many MOFs suffer from intrinsic structural limitations that restrict their adsorption capacities, thereby limiting their efficacy in applications that demand substantial porosity. Addressing these limitations and maximizing the adsorption capacity of MOFs is essential for advancing their utility in environmental and industrial applications. Recent advancements in MOF synthesis have enhanced our ability to tailorspecific porosity and adsorption properties. For physisorption, an effective approach involves the incorporation of larger, bulkier ligands, which increases the pore volume and pore size.[15](#page-8-0)−[17](#page-8-0) In contrast, chemisorption can be enhanced by introducing specific chemical sites or controlled structural defects that provide additional adsorption sites and increase pore accessibility.[18](#page-8-0)−[24](#page-9-0) An approach known as defect engineering has been developed that involves ligand mixing, modulation of reaction conditions, and postsynthetic modifications.[18](#page-8-0)−[24](#page-9-0) Among these, the ligand mixing strategy has proven effective for precise control of the defect structures within MOFs, enabling fine-tuning of the overall properties of the framework.[19](#page-8-0)−[21](#page-8-0) Flexible MOFs can adapt their conformations in response to changes in the guest molecules, pressure, or temperature and they can exist in either closed or open conformations.[25](#page-9-0)−[35](#page-9-0) They exhibit fascinating properties. For example, flexible MOFs have shown considerable promise with adsorption capacities that adapt in response to external stimuli.[25](#page-9-0)<sup>−</sup>[35](#page-9-0) However, a notable limitation of flexible MOFs is that they tend to remain closed after drying ([Scheme](#page-1-0) [1](#page-1-0)a)[.27](#page-9-0)<sup>−</sup>[29,33](#page-9-0) This can occur due to *π*−*π* interactions between the aromatic rings of the linkers, which stabilize the closed form and require higher energy to reopen the structures.[27](#page-9-0)−[29](#page-9-0),[33](#page-9-0)

MIL-53, a well-studied flexible MOF synthesized by reacting dicarboxylic acids with trivalent metal ions, features onedimensional (1D) diamond-shaped channels that impart structural flexibility[.32](#page-9-0)<sup>−</sup>[35](#page-9-0) An analog, MIL-53D (also known as DUT-5), can be synthesized by reacting 4,4′-biphenyldicarboxylic acid (H2BPDC) and trivalent metal ions. Recently, we synthesized an indium-based variant of MIL-53D (In-MIL-53D)[.32](#page-9-0) This MOF displayed minimal porosity upon drying, presenting challenges for its adsorption applications. This is a typical problem for flexible MOFs and an effective strategy must be developed to overcome this limitation. Herein, we demonstrate an approach for creating structural defects within the nearly nonporous In-MIL-53D framework by incorporating a shorter organic linker, which optimizes the gate-opening of the channels and the adsorption characteristics of the MOF. A series of hybrid MOFs incorporating defined ratios of H2BPDC and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylic acid (H2BDC as a shorter linker) were synthesized, and N2 and CO2 adsorption measurements were conducted for Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) analysis. The judicious incorporation of H2BDC into the In-MIL-53D framework created structural defects, activated gate-opening, and generated open metal sites. However, an excessive number of defects undermined the framework's integrity, compromising its stability and heightening the risk of collapse. Thus, it was crucial to attain an optimal defect level to maintain structural stability while enhancing functionality. These structural and chemical modifications of the MOFs result in a significant enhancement of CO2 adsorption with up to an 11-fold increase in adsorption capacity at 1 *P*/*P*<sup>0</sup> compared to that of the pristine MOF. Enhanced adsorption performance was also confirmed

during the capture of chemical warfare agent (CWA) simulant. In-MIL-53D hybrids exposed to 2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide (CEES) vapor in a jar-in-jar setup showed a steady increase in the adsorption with increasing numbers of structural defects, with up to 5-fold enhanced adsorption performance for CEES compared to that of pristine In-MIL-53D. By adjusting the defect levels, both the pore structure and the adsorption capacity can be fine-tuned to obtain a hybrid MOF with optimal performance for applications requiring enhanced porosity and structural flexibility.

The transition from an open to a closed structure during the guest evacuation process is a typical characteristic of flexible MOFs[.25](#page-9-0)<sup>−</sup>[35](#page-9-0) Once dried, these MOFs adopt a closed form and sometimes cannot return to their open form, limiting their suitability for applications that require sustained flexibility ([Scheme](#page-1-0) 1a). This behavior highlights the need for in-depth studies on flexible MOFs to better understand the processes involved in reopening so that their full potential can be realized in practical applications.

To activate the gate-opening behavior of In-MIL-53D for enlarged pore volume and enhanced adsorption performance, we employed a defect-engineering strategy by introducing H2BDC, a shorter linker than the original H2BPDC, during the synthesis of In-MIL-53D. The incorporation of this shorter linker created structural defects in the In-MIL-53D framework ([Scheme](#page-1-0) 1b), facilitating gate-opening and improving the adsorption capacity, which was otherwise constrained in the pristine closed structure. A schematic representation of the defect-containing In-MIL-53D structure is shown in [Scheme](#page-1-0) 1b. The structural conformation of MIL-53, whether in a closed or open form, is governed by two key factors: long-range dispersion interactions (*π*−*π* interactions between organic linkers) and vibrational entropy[.33](#page-9-0),[39](#page-9-0)<sup>−</sup>[41](#page-9-0) Both experimental and theoretical studies suggest that *π*−*π* interactions between organic linkers stabilize the closed form of MIL-53, while the open form is favored when entropy effects dominate.[33](#page-9-0),[39](#page-9-0)−[41](#page-9-0) Therefore, weakening or reducing the *π*−*π* interactions between organic linkers within MIL-53 would destabilize the closed form and facilitate the gate-opening. The partial replacement of the longer BPDC2<sup>−</sup> linkers with shorter HBDC<sup>−</sup> linkers will reduce these *π*−*π* interactions due to the decreased number of aromatic rings within the structure. The incorporation of shorter linkers into the framework also increased the number of open metal sites, enhancing the accessibility of the adsorption sites. Controlled defect incorporation within the MOFs was achieved through a series of solvothermal reactions by varying the ratio of the two linkers, H2BPDC and H2BDC, with In(NO3)3 [\(Scheme](#page-1-0) 1b). <sup>1</sup> H NMR spectroscopic analysis of the In-MIL-53D hybrids digested in acetic acid-*d*<sup>4</sup> and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-*d*<sup>6</sup> confirmed the incorporation ratios of the two linkers in the In-MIL-53D hybrids and showed that the short linker (H2BDC) was incorporated into the framework with ca. 10, 20, 30, 39, and 50% (denoted as Hybrid-1−Hybrid-5, respectively). The ratios of H2BDC used in the solvothermal reactions and the ratios of the incorporated short linkers within Hybrid-1−Hybrid-5 are depicted in [Figure](#page-2-0) 1e (see also [Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S1), indicating that the incorporated amount of the short linker could be precisely controlled. The reproducibility and controllability of the production of the In-MIL-53D hybrids were validated through four independent experiments. Each experiment yielded small error bars, demonstrating the precise control that was achieved for the incorporation of the two linkers within each hybrid, thereby allowing control over the number of defects and open metal sites. Additional analysis was performed to characterize defect formation. IR spectra of a series of samples confirmed the presence of defects within the frameworks. Specifically, the partial substitution of BPDC2<sup>−</sup> with HBDC<sup>−</sup> resulted in the uncoordinated −CO2H group and open metal sites. Consequently, in the In-MIL-53D hybrids, bare −CO2H group should be incorporated into the framework. The presence of −CO2H group[42](#page-9-0) in the In-MIL-53D hybrids was verified by IR spectra [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S3).

SEM images of the products of these reactions revealed rodlike structures similar to those of pure In-MIL-53D ([Figure](#page-2-0) 1f− j). No signs of phase separation or production of byproducts, such as the distinct morphological characteristics of In-MIL-68B produced from In3+ and H2BDC ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S4), were observed. PXRD analysisfurther confirmed these observations, as no peaks corresponding to In-MIL-68B were detected [\(Figure](#page-2-0) 1k). In particular, the strong peaks characteristic of In-MIL-68B at 4.69 and 9.38° for the (110) and (220) planes, respectively, were absent. For hybrids with up to 20% incorporation of the short linker (Hybrid-1 and Hybrid-2), the PXRD pattern remained nearly identical to that of the pristine form, indicating that the crystallinity of the framework was mostly preserved. In addition, the unit cell parameters of Hybrid-1 and Hybrid-2 (Table 1), calculated from their PXRD patterns by whole pattern fitting ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S5 and S6), were similar to those of pristine In-MIL-53D. However, as the incorporation of the short linker increased beyond this point (Hybrid-3−Hybrid-5), many defects and open metal sites were created, gradually affecting the overall crystallinity. This change was reflected in a decrease in the PXRD peak intensity and by peak broadening, suggesting slight destabilization of the framework. These results indicate that the incorporation of an excessive amount of the short linker can compromise the stability of In-MIL-53D, presumably because a higher proportion of the short linker disrupts the original framework, leading to structural instability and potential

To quantify the impact of the incorporation of the short linker on structural recovery to its open form, N2 sorption isotherms were measured after pretreatment at 250 °C for 3 h (Figure 2a). By introducing defects, we aimed to facilitate the gate-opening behavior of the flexible MOF that initially persisted in their closed forms. As expected, the N2 sorption isotherms increased as In-MIL-53D containing more of the short linker were used, reaching the highest values for Hybrid-4. Specifically, a BET surface area and total pore volume of 77.7 m2 g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> and 0.097 cm<sup>3</sup> g−1 , respectively, were measured for pristine In-MIL-53D ([Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) [S2](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf)). As the amount of short linker incorporation increased, the BET surface areas and total pore volumes of Hybrid-1 (196.2 m<sup>2</sup> g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> and 0.134 cm3 g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ), Hybrid-2 (649.1 m<sup>2</sup> g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> and 0.295 cm3 g−1 ), Hybrid-3 (1033.7 m<sup>2</sup> g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> and 0.509 cm3 g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ), and Hybrid-4 (1680.2 m<sup>2</sup> g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> and 0.747 cm3 g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ) showed a steady increase ([Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S2). However, Hybrid-5, with 50% incorporation of the short linker, displayed a slight decrease in BET surface area and total pore volume (1534.8 m<sup>2</sup> g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> and 0.710 cm3 g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ) compared to the highest values at Hybrid-4. Thus, the incorporation of too many short linker units introduces severe structural instability in the MOFs and results in a decrease in porosity.

The relative N2 adsorption capacities at 1.0 atm across the In-MIL-53D hybrid series exhibited a consistent upward trend, peaking at Hybrid-4 (Figure 2b). This enhanced performance, up to 773% higher than that of pristine In-MIL-53D, indicates that approximately 39% incorporation of the short linker is optimal for this series of MOFs. To further understand the observed N2 sorption behavior, nonlocal density functional theory (NLDFT) was used to analyze the pore-size distributions of the In-MIL-53D hybrids. As expected, pristine In-MIL-53D exhibited minimal porosity (Figure 2c). However, with the incorporation of the short linker, the pore volume increased (Figure 2d−g). With the introduction of excessive defects, the overall pore volume began to decrease (Figure 2h). These

Given the success of the incorporation of the short linker in facilitating the gate-opening of In-MIL-53D, we sought to determine the maximum adsorption capacity that could be achieved for the In-MIL-53D hybrids and assess the potential for precise control over gate-opening. A new series of In-MIL-53D hybrids were synthesized with intermediate ratios between those previously established [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S9); thus, the incorporation of the short linker into the framework with proportions of approximately 5, 15, 25, 36, and 45% (denoting as Hybrid-0.5−Hybrid-4.5, respectively) was undertaken and confirmed by <sup>1</sup> H NMR spectroscopic analysis. Four independent experiments were conducted for each sample to establish the reproducibility, confirming the intended linker ratios. The ratios of H2BDC used in the reactions and the ratios of its incorporation are depicted in [Figure](#page-6-0) 4a, indicating that the incorporation ratio of the two linkers is quite controllable. This level of control supports the reliability of our synthesis approach, indicating the ability for the fine-tuning of the MOF structure. Such control over the MOF structure through deliberate defect incorporation may provide new opportunities for the optimization of flexible MOFs for gas storage and separation and for catalysis applications.

The SEM images of this new series show the formation of rodlike structures ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S9), consistent with the expected morphology. With up to 25% incorporation of the short linker (Hybrid-0.5−Hybrid-2.5), the PXRD pattern remained almost identical to that of the pristine form ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S9f), indicating that the crystallinity of the framework was mostly preserved. The unit cell parameters of these products ([Table](#page-3-0) 1), calculated from their PXRD patterns by whole pattern fitting [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S10− [S12\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf), revealed that the values are similar to that of pristine In-MIL-53D. Having demonstrated that MOFs with activated gateopening, increased porosity, and enhanced site accessibility can be effectively engineered, we assessed their potential for removing toxic chemicals. The release of CWAs poses an immediate and severe risk to human health and safety.[5](#page-8-0)<sup>−</sup>[9](#page-8-0) Rapid detection and removal of these toxic agents from the air are essential to prevent exposure because CWAs can cause debilitating effects, including severe skin rashes, respiratory damage, and even fatality. The gate-opening behavior of the In-MIL-53D hybrids shows promise for applications requiring responsive adsorption. Unlike pristine In-MIL-53D, which tends to remain closed once its gate is closed, the incorporation of a short linker into the In-MIL-53D framework enables a persistent gate-opening. This structural adaptation suggests that the In-MIL-53D hybrids can provide continuous and enhanced adsorption, making them highly suitable for real-time CWA removal from the environment. Due to the hazards associated with actual CWAs, CEES was selected as a safe laboratory test agent for the CWA adsorption experiments. CEES is a wellknown simulant of sulfur mustard (bis(2-chloroethyl)sulfide), which is a typical CWA that can cause severe tissue damage upon exposure[.6](#page-8-0)<sup>−</sup>[9](#page-8-0) The relative CEES adsorption capabilities of pristine In-MIL-53D and its hybrid variants were evaluated using the jar-in-jar setup at 23 °C ([Figure](#page-6-0) 5a). In this setup, a small beaker containingCEES was placed at the center of a larger glass chamber, with six crucible pans containing the MOF samples positioned around, thereby exposing the samples to CEES vapor for defined time intervals.

The adsorption of CEES within the MOF samples was confirmed using both energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and <sup>1</sup> H NMR spectroscopy. The EDX spectra of the samplesrevealed the presence of sulfur and chlorine atomsin the adsorbed CEES, whereas neither was detected in the samples before the CEES adsorption experiment [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S17 and S18). To quantify the amount of CEES adsorbed by each sample, the samples exposed to CEES vapor for various time intervals were dissolved in acetic acid-*d*<sup>4</sup> and DMSO-*d*<sup>6</sup> for <sup>1</sup> H NMR spectroscopic analysis. The number of CEES molecules adsorbed per In-MIL-53D unit was calculated by comparing the integrated <sup>1</sup> H NMR peaks of CEES with those of the organic linkers from In-MIL-53D ([Figure](#page-6-0) 5). The quantities of CEES adsorbed at different exposure times are plotted in [Figure](#page-6-0) 5b. After reaching saturation following 3 h of exposure to CEES vapor, the adsorption capacity of pristine In-MIL-53D was determined to be 0.05 mol of CEES per mole of In-MIL-53D (0.05 molCEES/molMOF) [\(Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S6). All In-MIL-53D hybrids displayed enhanced CEES adsorption capacities compared to that of pristine In-MIL-53D, both during the early stage of CEES exposure and after saturation ([Figure](#page-6-0) 5c−e). The increase in CEES adsorption capacity occurred in a stepwise, progressive manner. However, a steep increase was observed with the hybrid containing approximately 39% incorporation of the short linker, which achieved approximately 5-fold higher adsorption capacity relative to pristine In-MIL-53D, reaching 0.24 molCEES/molMOF after 3 h exposure ([Figure](#page-6-0) 5b and [Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S6). This dramatic enhancement highlights the effective combination of the activated gate-opening and additional open metal sites, resulting in the maximized adsorption capacity of Hybrid-4. The adsorption kinetics of pristine In-MIL-53D and its hybrids are illustrated in [Figure](#page-6-0) 5 and summarized in [Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c01399/suppl_file/ja5c01399_si_001.pdf) S7. The adsorption of CEES onto In-MIL-53D and a series of defectcontaining In-MIL-53D hybrids followed a pseudo-first-order kinetic model.

# ■ **CONCLUSION**

Flexible MOFs with deliberately introduced defects were synthesized using a ligand-mixing strategy. The samples exhibited enhanced porosity and increased adsorption capacity. By introducing a shorter organic linker into the nearly nonporous In-MIL-53D framework, structural defects were introduced that optimized the channel gate-opening and improved the adsorption performance. A series of ten In-MIL-53D hybrids were prepared with precisely controlled ratios of the original linker (H2BPDC) and the shorter linker (H2BDC; ranging from 5% to 50% in 5% intervals). The structural defects activated the gate-opening, significantly enhancing the gas adsorption capacities of the MOFs for N2 and CO2. Additionally, the hybrids demonstrated excellent adsorption performance for the CWA simulant, CEES. The incorporation of a shorter linker created open metal sites, further contributing to improved adsorption. However, the incorporation of too many defects disrupted the framework's integrity and compromised its stability, leading to potential collapse. Thus, achieving an optimal level of defect incorporation is crucial for balancing structural stability and enhanced functionality. By fine-tuning the defect levels, both the pore structure and the adsorption capacity were optimized, resulting in a hybrid MOF with superior performance. Among the synthesized hybrids, the sample with approximately 39% short linker incorporation exhibited the best results, showing an 11-fold increase in CO2 adsorption capacity at 1 *P*/*P*<sup>0</sup> and up to a 5-fold increase in CEES adsorption capacity compared to pristine In-MIL-53D. These enhancements arose from the synergistic effects of the activated gate-opening and additional open metal sites. The softened, flexible framework opens up more readily in response to environmental changes, highlighting the potential of these tailored hybrid MOFs for advanced applications. This study emphasizes the critical balance between defect incorporation and framework stability, demonstrating the versatility of engineered MOFs for diverse practical applications.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained using a JEOL IT-500HR field-emission SEM and a JEOL JSM-7610F-Plus field-emission SEM. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were obtained using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 instrument equipped with Cu Kα radiation source (40 kV, 15 mV). Crystalmaker software (version 2.2.2) was used to render the schematic representation (ball-and-stick version) of the samples. The simulated structures of the samples were obtained from the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Center. For samples without available simulated structures, modifications were made using the calculated cell parameters in the VESTA software package. <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Avance III HD 300 spectrometer (<sup>1</sup>H NMR, 300 MHz) with acetic acid-*d*<sup>4</sup> and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-*d*6. Infrared (IR) spectra of the solid samples were measured utilizing a Jasco FT/IR 4200 spectrometer equipped with the attenuated total reflection module. The sorption isotherms of N<sup>2</sup> (77 K) were measured in the gaseous state using BELSORP Max and BELSORP II-mini volumetric adsorption equipment. The pore-size distribution was calculated using the non-local density functional theory method with BELMaster version 6.3.0.0 software. *Preparation of 4,4'-biphenyldicarboxylic acid (H2BPDC) solution:* A solution of H2BPDC was prepared by adding the required amount of H2BPDC powder to *N*,*N*-dimethylformamide (DMF) to achieve the desired concentration. The mixture was then sonicated to form a homogeneous mixture then heated in an oil bath at 120C for 30 min. When the solution became transparent, it was cooled to room temperature for further use.

*Synthesis of In-MIL-53D:* A 0.055 M solution of In(NO3)3·*x*H2O (*x* = 3, determined by TGA; 156 mg, 0.44 mmol) and a 0.045 M solution of H2BPDC (87 mg, 0.36 mmol) were mixed in DMF (16 mL). The mixture was transferred to a 70 mL vial, placed in an oil bath, and heated at 120°C for 30 min. The product was then cooled to room temperature, isolated, and washed with fresh DMF and methanol using a centrifugation–redispersion cycle.

*Synthesis of In-MIL-53D hybrids, Hybrid-X (X = 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, and 5):* In(NO3)3·*x*H2O (156 mg, 0.44 mmol), H2BPDC (0.36 × (1–*x*) mmol), and 1,4 benzenedicarboxylic acid (H2BDC; 0.36 × *x* mmol) were dissolved in DMF (16 mL) (where *x* = 0.02, 0.07, 0.12, 0.17, 0.22, 0.27, 0.32, 0.37, 0.42, and 0.47). The mixture was transferred to a 70 mL vial, placed in an oil bath, and heated at 120°C for 30 min. The product was cooled to room

temperature, isolated, and washed with fresh DMF and methanol using a centrifugation– redispersion cycle.

*Synthesis of In-MIL-68B:* A 0.055 M solution of In(NO3)3·*x*H2O (156 mg, 0.44 mmol) and a 0.045 M solution of H2BDC (60 mg, 0.36 mmol) were mixed in DMF (16 mL). The mixture was transferred to a 70 mL vial, placed in an oil bath, and heated at 120°C for 30 min. The product was cooled to room temperature, isolated, and washed with fresh DMF and methanol using a centrifugation–redispersion cycle.

*Preparation of 2,6-naphthalenedicarboxylic acid (H2NDC) solution:* A solution of H2NDC was prepared by adding the required amount of H2NDC powder to DMF to achieve the desired concentration. The mixture was then sonicated to form a homogeneous mixture then heated in an oil bath at 120C for 5 min. When the solution became transparent, it was cooled to room temperature for further use.

*Synthesis of Fe-MIL-88C:* A 0.055 M solution of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (178 mg, 0.44 mmol) and a 0.045 M solution of H2NDC (78 mg, 0.36 mmol) were mixed in DMF (16 mL). The mixture was transferred to a 70 mL vial, placed in an oil bath, and heated at 120°C for 30 min. The product was then cooled to room temperature, isolated, and washed with fresh DMF and methanol using a centrifugation–redispersion cycle.

*Synthesis of Fe-MIL-88C hybrid (X%) (X = 12%, and 26%):* Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (178 mg, 0.44 mmol), H2NDC (0.36 × (1–*x*) mmol), and H2BDC (0.36 × *x* mmol) were dissolved in DMF (16 mL) (where *x* = 0.17 and 0.37). The mixture was transferred to a 70 mL vial, placed in an oil

bath, and heated at 120°C for 30 min. The product was cooled to room temperature, isolated, and washed with fresh DMF and methanol using a centrifugation–redispersion cycle.

*Adsorption of CEES:* The adsorption of CEES was conducted at 23°C using a custom-made jarin-jar setup. For the experiments, In-MIL-53D and hybrid In-MIL-53D powders (2.0 mg) were placed in a ceramic crucible pan, while CEES (2.0 mL) was placed in a 5 mL beaker. The crucible pan and beaker were positioned inside a large jar, which was sealed with a lid. After exposure to CEES vapor for defined time intervals, the samples were dissolved in acetic acid-*d*<sup>4</sup> and DMSO-*d*<sup>6</sup> and analyzed by <sup>1</sup>H NMR spectroscopy to quantify the amount of CEES adsorbed by each sample.

*Adsorption kinetics of Hybrid-X:* The adsorption of CEES was fitted to a pseudo-first-order model using the equation:

$$
q_t = q_s(1 - e^{-kt}),
$$

where, *q<sup>e</sup>* is equilibrium adsorption capacity (mg g –1 ), *q<sup>t</sup>* is the adsorption capacity (mg g –1 ) at time *t*, and *k* is the pseudo-first-order rate constant (h–1 ).
