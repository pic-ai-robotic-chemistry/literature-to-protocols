The construction of superlattices with a spatial modulation of chemical compositions allows for the creation of artifcial materials with tailorable periodic potential landscapes and tunable electronic and optical properties[1–](#page-6-0)[5](#page-6-1) . Conventional semiconductor superlattices with designable potential modulation in one dimension has enabled high-electron-mobility transistors and quantum-cascade lasers. More recently, a diverse set of superlattices has been constructed through self-assembly or guided assembly of multiscale building units, including zerodimensional nanoclusters and nanoparticles[6](#page-6-2),[7](#page-6-3) , one-dimensional nanorods and nanowires[8](#page-6-4)[,9](#page-6-5) , two-dimensional nanolayers and nanosheets[10](#page-6-6)[–13,](#page-6-7) and hybrid twodimensional molecular assemblies[14](#page-6-8)–[17](#page-6-9). These self-assembled superlattices feature periodic structural modulation in two or three dimensions, but often lack atomic precision owing to the inevitable structural disorder at the interfaces between the constituent units. Here we report a one-pot synthesis of multi-dimensional single-crystalline superlattices consisting of periodic arrangement of zero-, oneand two-dimensional building units. By exploiting zirconium (IV) metal–organic frameworks as host templates for directed nucleation and precise growth of metalhalide sublattices through a coordination-assisted assembly strategy, we synthesize a family of single-crystalline porous superlattices. Single-crystal X-ray crystallography and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy clearly resolve the highorder superlattice structure with deterministic atomic coordinates. Further treatment with selected amine molecules produces perovskite-like superlattices with highly tunable photoluminescence and chiroptical properties. Our study creates a platform of high-order single-crystalline porous superlattices, opening opportunities to tailor the electronic, optical and quantum properties beyond the reach of conventional crystalline solids.

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs)[18,](#page-6-10) also known as porous coordination networks (PCNs)[19](#page-6-11), feature a highly ordered three-dimensional (3D) structure and may serve as an interesting template for constructing high-order superlattices[20](#page-6-12). By utilizing reticular chemistry to link multiple organic ligands and metal ions by coordinate bonds, a wide range of crystalline structures have been created[21](#page-6-13)[–23](#page-6-14), providing an elaborate nanospace with tailorable chemical microenvironments in which various guests can be incorporated with controlled sizes and shapes[24](#page-6-15)[–29](#page-6-16). For example, MOFs have been exploited as a crystalline sponge to capture organic molecules in the porous crystalline templates for single-crystal X-ray structure determination without crystallizing the molecules themselves[30,](#page-6-17)[31.](#page-6-18) Recent studies have further demonstrated the MOF-templated synthesis of isolated metal-halide sheets with unique magnetic behaviours[28](#page-6-19) and the growth of titanium dioxide nanoparticles with enhanced photocatalytic activity[29.](#page-6-16) We envision that the controlled nucleation and extended growth of electronically or optically active guest materials within the ordered channels or cavities of crystalline MOFs could produce a continuous framework with highly tunable chemical and structural motifs, enabling a versatile family of superlattices with a customizable potential landscape and precisely tailored electronic or optical functionalities[5](#page-6-1) .

Here we report a one-pot synthesis of high-order single-crystalline porous superlattices by confined growth of selected metal halides (PbI2, PbBr2, CdI2 and NiBr2) within a series of 3D zirconium (IV) MOFs. We show that the pore environments, such as cavity sizes, shapes and direction of adjacent anchoring sites, have a critical role in tailoring

#### **Synthesis and X-ray crystal structures**

For illustrative purpose, five types of zirconium (Zr) MOF (NU-1000, NU-600, PCN-700, PCN-609 and PCN-606) were used as templates for the nucleation of PbI2 to produce single-crystalline PbI2@MOF superlattices (see Methods for details). This process produces well-faceted crystals (about 100 mm) with distinct colour and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns from the starting MOF crystals (Fig. [1\)](#page-2-0). SC-XRD studies allowed us to resolve the precise atomic coordinates for all atoms, showing atomically precisely ordered superstructures (Fig. [1](#page-2-0) and Extended Data Figs. 1–5). We predict that the distance and space between each pair of adjacent Zr nodes may influence the confined growth of metal halides and determine the superlattice structures. To validate this hypothesis, we carefully analysed and crystallographically resolved the atomic structures of metal-halide units formed within the distinct coordinative environments of the MOF templates.

First, the smaller rhombic nanochannel of NU-1000 can be filled with distorted cuboid PbI2 clusters (4.3 × 4.5 × 7.4 Å3 ; Fig. [1b](#page-2-0)). However, such coordination behaviour compartmentalizes the large hexagonal-shaped nanochannel (31.6 Å) because the extended centre is too far away for ordered extension of PbI2 clusters, resulting in a 0D PbI2@NU-1000 superlattice (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Similar behaviour is also observed in PbBr2@NU-1000, NiBr2@NU-1000 and CdI2@NU-1000 superlattices (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). In the case of the NU-600 template, cubic cages composed of 8 Zr6 clusters, each featuring 24 pairs of uncoordinated OH– groups oriented towards a central pore, are sequentially interconnected by metal halides. This arrangement results in quasi-spherical hollow metal-halide superlattices of varying sizes (Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). To achieve more complex metal-halide@MOF superlattices, we identified the more flexible PCN-700 MOF that has tetragonal-like nanochannels with relatively homogeneous shapes and sizes. This template allows structural adaptation to form unique 1D chains within the confined channels, producing high-order vertical-1D PbI2@PCN-700, lateral-1D PbBr2@PCN-700 and 0D NiBr2@PCN-700 superlattices with varying bond lengths and angles (Fig. [1d](#page-2-0), Extended Data Figs. 3 and 4, and Supplementary Fig. 8).

These initial results suggest that the framework's flexibility and the distance between adjacent Zr nodes are key factors in determining the multi-dimensional metal-halide@MOF superlattices. With this consideration, we predict that four pairs of adjacent H2O/–OH groups on the equatorial plane could, in principle, incorporate 2D sheet-like metal-halide crystals throughout all of the nanopores and produce high-order 2D metal-halide@MOF superlattices. Indeed, our hypothesis is supported by using two low-symmetric Zr MOFs (namely, PCN-609 and PCN-606) as the growth templates.

PCN-609 contains three nanochannels: one along the *a* axis (about 10.8 Å) and two along the *c* axes (about 6.1 Å and 10.3 Å; Supplementary Fig. 9a). Crystallographic studies reveal that eight distinct types of coordinated Pb site build a bridge to adjacent Zr6 clusters, resulting in a complete filling of all three channels by PbI2. However, the centre of the two larger nanochannels remains quite empty, thus leading to the formation of a half-filled 2D PbI2@PCN-609 superlattice with a partial Pb–O bridge (Fig. [1f](#page-2-0) and Supplementary Fig. 10), presumably because the rigid structure of PCN-609 lacks the ability to self-adjust the relatively smaller nanopore (about 12.0 Å) for orderly arrangements of PbI2 crystals.

To this end, the flexible PCN-606 with an adaptable framework can address this key issue and produce the fully filled 2D PbI2@PCN-606 superlattice. This material shows a perfectly ordered, porous 2D superlattice consisting of six distinct types of crystallographically coordinated Pb site (Fig. [1h\)](#page-2-0). When treated with PbI2 solution, the PCN-606 framework continuously adjusts itself to accommodate PbI2 guests from the edge of the Zr nodes to the centre pores over time, leading to the formation of 2D PbI2 layers in the whole PCN-606 framework (Supplementary Fig. 11). Substituting iodine with bromine results in an analogous 2D PbBr2@PCN-606 superlattice (Extended Data Fig. 5).

Detailed structural analysis reveals that eight pairs of –OH groups are coordinated with Pb sites to form the edge of 2D Pb-based layers (Fig. [1h,](#page-2-0) sites I and II along the *a* axes, and sites III and IV along the *c* axes), effectively controlling each monolayer's thickness at approximately 7.2 Å. The fifth and sixth sites are located at the centre pore region bridging sites I, III and IV along the [100] direction through the iodine coordination (site III), completely filling up [010] crystal face area.

#### **Confined growth mechanism of superlattices**

To further understand the confined nucleation and growth process, we performed time-dependent SC-XRD experiments to monitor the growth of PbI2 within MOFs using PbI2@PCN-606 as a representative case study, in which the formation process can be precisely monitored over time. Overall, this process involves the evolutionary transformation from partially filled 0D nanoclusters (PbI2@PCN-606-I, -II and -III) or 1D nanorods (PbI2@PCN-606-IV) to fully filled 2D nanolayers (PbI2@PCN-606-V) (Fig. [2c\)](#page-3-0). At the initial stage, each pair of –OH groups in the Zr nodes were blocked by four Pb2+ ions and further coordinated with two I− ions (stage I), confirming the cluster nucleation at these sites. By measuring the distance between each pair of –OH groups, we found that the smaller pocket undergoes a slight shrinkage (from 8.1 Å to 7.7 Å) owing to bridging by the growth of PbI2, whereas the larger one expands from 10.9 Å to 11.9 Å. Subsequently, another eight Pb2+ ions fully coordinate with a pair of –OH groups, which is further bridged by I<sup>−</sup> ions or OH<sup>−</sup> ions (stages II and III), following a progressive inwards growth process from the Zr nodes to the centre pores.

Upon filling the larger central pocket with PbI2 clusters across the entire plane, the smaller one is stretched slightly to liberate the restrained PbI2 (stage IV). Finally, the flexible 3D framework contracted again to uniformly accommodate PbI2 on the entire [010] facet, resulting in the 2D PbI2 layer (stage V).

Furthermore, we have shown that three other MOF-templated superlattices also followed a similar confined growth mechanism, producing diverse shapes, sizes and dimensions of PbI2 structures in the MOF templates (Supplementary Figs. 1, 7 and 9). These PbI2 sublattices showed greater stability than PbI2 clusters in comparable superstructures (Extended Data Figs. 6 and 7) and distinct Pb coordination geometries compared with template-free PbI2 nanorods (Extended Data Fig. 8). These studies demonstrate that the coordination-assisted nucleation and growth strategy is scalable for constructing single-crystalline, high-order porous superlattices. The structural transformation induced by the stepwise incorporation of PbI2 can also be reflected by the distinct shifts in the PXRD patterns (Fig. [2b](#page-3-0) and Supplementary Figs. 14a and 15a–e). #### **Tailoring optical properties of superlattices**

The high-surface-area porous nature of the PbI2@MOF superlattices allows a great deal of flexibility to further tailor their chemical and physical properties, for example, through molecular modifications. Inspired by organo-lead-halide perovskites, the incorporation of an organic amine ligand into PbI2 lattices can produce perovskite-like systems with profoundly modified electronic band structures, tailorable charge-carrier dynamics and photoluminescence properties.

We treated the PbI2@MOF superlattices with different amines (including ammonium hydroxide, phenethylamine, and chiral *R*- and *S*-α-methylbenzylamine (*R*- and *S*-MBA); *R*- and *S*-α-methyl-1-naphthalenemethanamine (*R*- and *S*-NEA) and so on) through a straightforward wet chemical molecular diffuse process (Fig. [4a](#page-5-0)). The SC-XRD studies showed an unaltered unit cell and the PXRD studies also showed no notable difference (Fig. [4c](#page-5-0) and Supplementary Fig. 47), indicating that the incorporation of amines in 3D superlattice framework does not affect the overall crystalline structures. In contrast, the amine-modified superlattices showed notable dimensionalitydependent photoluminescence emission (Supplementary Fig. 48) and light up under 405-nm laser irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 49). Timedependent photoluminescence studies revealed that the photoluminescence emission increases with treatment time and it takes longer to reach the photoluminescence maximum for larger amine molecules (Fig. [4b,](#page-5-0) Supplementary Fig. 50 and Supplementary Video 1). Power- and temperature-dependent photoluminescence spectra (Supplementary Figs. 51–54), along with time-resolved photoluminescence decay curves (Supplementary Fig. 55) of *S*-MBA/PbI2@MOF, indicate that the photoluminescence originates from exciton emission.

In general, the amine-treated PbI2@MOF superlattices show a relatively broad emission peak with a room-temperature photoluminescence quantum yield of about 1–15% (Supplementary Figs. 56 and 57). The broad photoluminescence peak might be attributed to lattice distortion in the PbI2 sublattice. A systematic photoluminescence peak shift was observed across different superlattices, which is attributed to the convoluted effects of quantum confinement[33,](#page-6-21)[34](#page-6-22) and lattice distortion-induced bandgap narrowing[35](#page-6-23) (see Supplementary Text for details). Mott–Schottky curves (Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Fig. 58–60) and theoretical analysis (Supplementary Fig. 61) indicates that the dimensionality of the PbI2 sublattices determines the electronic band structures of PbI2@MOF superlattices. In the optimized *S*-MBA/PbI2@MOF host–guest structures, the higher lowest unoccupied molecular orbital energy of *S*-MBA leads to partial electron transfer to the PbI2@MOF superlattice[36](#page-6-24) (Supplementary Fig. 62 and Supplementary Table 1). This was confirmed by the appearance of a new peak at 700 cm−1 and 1,630 cm−1 in the FTIR spectrum of the product, which can be attributed to C–N and N–H bending vibration, respectively (Fig. [4e\)](#page-5-0). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy studies showed shifts in the Pb 4*f* band towards lower binding energy and in the N 1*s* band towards higher binding energy owing to the sharing of electrons from N (in *S*-MBA) with Pb2+ (ref. [37](#page-6-25); Supplementary Fig. 42).

The molecular molar ratio of PbI2/*S*-MBA was calculated according to inductively coupled plasma and <sup>1</sup> H NMR data for the *S*-MBA/PbI2@MOFs (Supplementary Figs. 63 and 64). Circular dichroism spectroscopy studies revealed that the chiral-amine-treated PbI2@PCN-606 is optically active and shows distinct preferential absorption of the different circularly polarized light in the wavelength range 260–560 nm (Fig. [4g\)](#page-5-0). Partial circular dichroism spectral peak positions remain unchanged from those of the pure chiral molecules (250–310 nm), suggesting the preservation of intrinsic molecular chirality. By using a series of MOF templates with tailorable structure motifs, we demonstrate that the metal-halide building units can be incorporated into the MOF host lattices with various structures to form high-order superlattices consisting of 0D, 1D and 2D metal-halide building units. The atomic structures and the nucleation process of the porous superlattices are well characterized by SC-XRD and HR-TEM with deterministically resolved atomic coordinates. The amine-modified porous superlattices show dimensionality-dependent photoluminescence and chirality-induced circularly polarized luminescence. Considering the structural versatility of the MOF templates and composition richness of the inorganic building units, this study opens a general pathway to a broad family of 3D porous superlattices with widely tunable structural motifs and composition modulations, achieving designable physical properties beyond the reach of conventional crystalline solids.

### **Methods**

#### **General materials and methods**

All reagents and solvents are commercially available and used without further purification. The FTIR (KBr pellet) spectra were recorded (400– 4,000 cm−1 region) on a Nicolet Magna 750 FTIR spectrometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy data were collected using an AXIS Ultra-DLD. The ultraviolet–visible diffuse reflectance spectra were recorded on a Lambda 20 UV/Vis Spectrometer (Perkin Elmer). Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry was carried out on Avio 500. Thermogravimetric analyses were carried out in a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 on a thermogravimetric analyser (Q5000IR). PXRD data were collected on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation. The powder samples were prepared by grinding bulk crystals with a mortar and pestle. The calculated PXRD patterns were produced using the Mercury program and single-crystal reflection data. 1 H NMR experiments were carried out on a Bruker 400 spectrometer operating at resonance frequencies of 400 MHz. The N2 gas adsorption isotherms were measured using a Micrometritics ASAP 2020 system at 77 K with a liquid N2 bath. (Before the measurement, the fresh crystals were washed with *N*,*N*-dimethylformamide (DMF) three times, then further soaked in acetone for solvent exchange with replacement of fresh acetone three times. Subsequently, the samples were evacuated at 100 °C for 10 h.) Scanning electron microscopy images were obtained on a NOVA NanoSEM 230 instrument equipped with an energy-dispersive spectroscopy detector. Transmission electron microscopy images were acquired on a Talos F200X/TALOS F200X instrument. Annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy maps were acquired using a beam current of 100–300 pA at room temperature. The electrochemical measurements were carried out on an electrochemical work station (CHI760E). Optical and fluorescence images were taken with an optical microscope (Olympus BX53M) equipped with a fluorescence module (U-FWBS). The photoluminescence and Raman spectra were performed on a home-built Raman spectrometer (iHR-550 Horiba) with 600 g mm−1 and 1,800 g mm−1 gratings. The samples were encapsulated in a liquid N2 bath cryostat (Cryo Industries of America) to measure the low-temperature and temperaturedependent photoluminescence spectra. The optical signals were collected by using ×50 objective (numerical aperture 0.6, Nikon). The photoluminescence polarization was determined by a quarterwaveplate (Thorlabs SAQWP05M-700), a half-waveplate (Thorlabs AHWP05M-600) and a polarizer (Thorlabs WP25M-UB).

#### **Synthesis of PbI2@MOF superlattices**

Five MOFs were synthesized as a bulk crystal by using a previously published procedur[e19](#page-6-11) and washed with DMF (20 ml × 3) and methanol (20 ml × 3), and soaked for 6 h between cleaning intervals. Then, the activated MOF crystals (5 mg) were dispersed in a 4 ml 10–20 mM PbI2 solution in a DMF/methanol mixture (2:1 v/v) in a 20-ml vial. The vial was then placed in a stable, undisturbed position to allow sufficient time (25–30 days) for confined growth of the PbI2 sublattice within the MOF pores. Finally, the obtained PbI2@MOF superlattice was thoroughly washed with DMF ten times to eliminate any isolated PbI2 residue. The crystals were then characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.

#### **Synthesis of PbBr2@MOF superlattices**

The activated MOF crystals (5 mg) were dispersed in a 4 ml 15–25 mM PbBr2 solution in a DMF/methanol mixture (1:1 v/v) in a 20-ml vial. The vial was then placed in a stable, undisturbed position to allow sufficient time (25–30 d) for confined growth of the PbBr2 sublattice within the MOF pores. Finally, the obtained PbBr2@MOF superlattice was thoroughly washed with DMF ten times to eliminate any isolated PbBr2 residue. The crystals were then characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.

#### **Synthesis of NiBr2@MOF superlattices**

The activated MOF crystals (5 mg), NiBr2 (10 mg) and diglyme (3 ml) were added into a 10 × 8 mm Pyrex tube. The reaction mixture was degassed by 3 freeze–pump–thaw cycles, after which the tube was flame-sealed and then placed in an oven heated to 120 °C for 1 month, resulting in a colour change of the crystals. The resulting NiBr2@MOF superlattice was thoroughly washed with DMF ten times to remove any isolated NiBr2 residue. Finally, the crystals were characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.

#### **Synthesis of CdI2@MOF superlattice**

The activated MOF crystals (5 mg) were dispersed in a 4 ml 10–15 mM CdI2 solution in a DMF/methanol mixture (1:2 v/v) in a 20-ml vial. The vial was then placed in a stable, undisturbed position to allow sufficient time (10–15 days) for confined growth of the CdI2 sublattice within the MOF pores. Finally, the obtained CdI2@MOF superlattices were thoroughly washed with DMF ten times to eliminate any isolated CdI2 residue. The crystals were then characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.

#### **Synthesis of amine-modified PbI2@MOF superlattices**

The PbI2@MOF crystals (50 mg) were placed in a glass vial containing amine (1 ml) and DMF (1 ml) and the mixture was soaked at 25 °C for various durations. For the synthesis of (*S*-NEA or *R*-NEA)/PbI2@PCN-606, the modification process required 10 days to complete under the same conditions owing to its larger molecular volume. After soaking, the amine-modified superlattices were collected by centrifuging the reaction mixture at 8,000 rpm for 5 min. The collected crystals were then redispersed in DMF and subjected to a centrifugation and redispersion process three times. This was followed by a similar washing procedure in ethanol three times to obtain amine-modified PbI2@MOF.

#### **Low-dose electron microscopy**

Low-dose (HR-TEM)[43](#page-8-0)[,44](#page-8-1) images were captured using an aberrationcorrected Themis ETEM 60-300 electron microscope operating at 300 kV and a Gatan double-tilt cryo-transfer holder. Specimen searching, zone axis alignment and prefocusing were meticulously performed at ×13,000 magnification under a dose rate of 0.01*e* Å−2 s−1. Subsequently, HR-TEM images were acquired at ×55,000 magnification using a Gatan K3 direct-detection camera in electron-counting mode with the dose fractionation function and under a dose rate of 1*e* Å−2 s−1. The advanced low-dose electron microscopy technique reported before facilitated the direct high-resolution imaging of PCN-700 and PCN-606 while preserving their structural integrit[y43](#page-8-0),[45.](#page-8-2) To achieve dose-efficient phase-contrast HR-TEM imaging and recover lost structural information owing to beam-induced motion and contrast reversal arising from the contrast transfer function of the objective lens (that is, a spherical aberration coefficient of 1 μm and defocus values of −200 nm and −484 nm were applied for PCN-700 and PCN-606, respectively), detailed image acquisition and processing methods were conducted according to a previous stud[y45](#page-8-2). The QSTEM software simulated the projected electrostatic potential, with point-spread-function width corresponding to the information transfer ([http://www.qstem.org\)](http://www.qstem.org).

#### **Single-crystal X-ray diffraction**

Single-crystal XRD data were collected on a Bruker D8 Venture with Cu Kα (*λ* = 1.54178 Å) at 173 K, or collected at the BL17B macromolecular crystallography beamline at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility at 100 K. We have collected several datasets for all metal-halide@MOF superlattices, and the best dataset was used for structure solution and refinement. The empirical absorption correction was applied by using the SADABS program[46](#page-8-3). The structures were solved by OLEX2 (version 1.5) in conjunction with SHELX (version 2018) to solve and refine the crystal structures. DFIX, SADI, FLAT, DANG, DELU, RIGU and SIMU restrains were used to obtain reasonable parameters. In these

compounds, all the non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically. Consequently, the unassigned electron density in these structures was accounted for using the SQUEEZE routine of PLATON. The structures were then refined again using the data generated. Crystal data and details of the data collection are given in Supplementary Tables 3–30. Disorder of the linkers and the metal halides gave rise to several A- and B-level alerts from checkCIF. Responses for the checkCIF alerts for the 28 crystal structures were illustrated in cif files.

#### **Cyclic voltammetry**

Cyclic voltammetry curves were measured in a standard three-electrode electrochemical cell, with a platinum plate as the counter electrode and saturated Hg/Hg2Cl2 as the reference electrode. The electrolyte consisted of 0.1 M tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate (*n*Bu4NPF6) in CH3CN. To prepare the working electrode, PbI2@MOF (2 mg) was sonicated in a mixture of ethanol (1 ml) and Nafion (10 μl) to form a slurry, and 10 μl of this slurry was applied to a pretreated glassy carbon electrode. Scan rate, 100 mV s−1, temperature, 25 °C.

#### **Photocurrent measurements**

Photocurrent tests were conducted in a standard three-electrode system, using PbI2@MOF-coated ITO as the working electrode, a platinum plate as the counter electrode, and saturated Hg/Hg2Cl2 as a reference electrode. A 300-W xenon lamp (LX-300F) with full spectrum was used as the light source; 0.1 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution was used as the electrolyte. To prepare the working electrodes, PbI2@MOF (2 mg) was dispersed in a mixture of 10 µl Nafion and 1.0 ml ethanol, and 200 µl of the suspension was drop-cast onto the surface of a 4-cm2 ITO plate. After drying, the photo-responsive signals of the samples were measured under chopped light with a bias potential of −0.1 V.

#### **Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements**

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was performed in a standard three-electrode system with the PbI2@MOF-coated glassy carbon (diameter *Φ* = 3 cm) as the working electrode, a platinum plate as counter electrode and saturated Hg/Hg2Cl2 as a reference electrode; 0.5 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution was used as the electrolyte. The PbI2@ MOF (2 mg) was dispersed into a mixed solution with 10 µl Nafion and 1.0 ml ethanol, and the working electrode was prepared by dropping the suspension (20 µl) onto the surface of the glassy carbon electrode. The working electrode was dried, and the electrolytes was bubbled with N2 for at least 30 min. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy data were recorded with frequency range of 0.1–105  Hz at a bias potential of 0.3 V (versus reversible hydrogen electrode) for describing Nyquist plots.

#### **Mott–Schottky plot measurements**

The Mott–Schottky plot measurements were performed in a standard three-electrode system with the PbI2@MOF-coated glassy carbon (*Φ* = 3 cm) as the working electrode, a platinum plate as counter electrode and saturated Hg/Hg2Cl2 as a reference electrode; 0.2 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution (pH = 6.8) was used as the electrolyte. The PbI2@ MOF (2 mg) was dispersed into a mixed solution with 10 µl Nafion and 1.0 ml ethanol, and the working electrode was prepared by dropping the suspension (20 µl) onto the surface of the glassy carbon electrode. The working electrode was dried, and the electrolytes was bubbled with N2 for at least 30 min.

#### **Ultrafast transient absorption spectroscopic characterizations**

The ultrafast transient absorption measurements were performed on a modified ExciPro system (CDP) in combination with an ultrafast amplified laser system (Coherent), using a femtosecond time-resolved pump–probe configuration under ambient conditions. The pump pulses (centre wavelength at 320 nm for this study; pulse energy of about 100 nJ at the sample cell, corresponding to a pump fluence of about 140 μJ cm−2) were delivered by an optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS-800-fs), which was excited by a titanium:sapphire regenerative amplifier (Legend Elite-1K-HE; centre wavelength 800 nm, pulse duration 35 fs, pulse energy 3 mJ) seeded with a mode-locked titanium:sapphire laser system (Micra 5) and pumped with a 1-kHz neodymium-doped yttrium-lithium-fluoride laser (Evolution 30). The probe pulses (pulse energy of about 0.4 µJ at the sample cell) were provided by a stable white-light continuum (450–650 nm for this study) that was generated by focusing the 800-nm beam (split from the regenerative amplifier by a portion of 10%) on a sapphire plate. The linear chirp of the white-light continuum spectrum was compensated with the aid of the cross-phase modulation signals recorded on a liquid hexane sample using ExiPro 2.6 software. The white-light continuum pulse was free of pre-pulsing and after-pulsing, as verified by our routine pulse characterizations. The pump and probe beams were collinearly polarized. The instrument response function was determined to be about 100 fs by cross-correlating the pump and probe pulses at the sample cell. The samples well dispersed in acetonitrile were contained in a 0.7-ml quartz cuvette under continuous magnetic stirring, ensuring that the photoexcited volume of the sample was kept fresh during the course of the transient absorption measurements.

#### **Density functional theory calculation**

All the structures (including the MOF, PbI2@MOF, *S*-MBA and *S*-MBA/ PbI2@MOF) were optimized through density functional theory calculations in the CP2K package[47.](#page-8-4) The PB[E48](#page-8-5) functional and a double zeta (DZVP-MOLOPT)[49](#page-8-6) basis set were used. In addition, van der Waals corrections were included by using DFT-D3 method[50](#page-8-7). On the basis of the optimized structures, the highest occupied molecular orbital and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital energy levels, as well as the binding energies, were estimated.

#### Stability

The thermal stability of superlattices was investigated through thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Solvent molecules were eliminated within the temperature range of 80 to 120 °C, while the superlattices maintained their structural integrity up to 450 °C (Supplementary Fig. 29). Due to the strong Zr-O bond and Pb-O bond in Zr6 nodes, the chemical stability of four PbI2@MOFs were verified by PXRD patterns and N2 sorption isotherms on samples after exposure to various harsh chemical conditions, including treatment in strong acid (1 M HCl), strong base (0.1 M NaOH) for a week, boiling water for one month (Supplementary Figs. 22-25). PXRD studies indicated no significant alterations in the diffraction patterns, while the BET surface areas of the treated samples were either comparable to or slightly lower than those of the prototype samples, further confirming the stability and permanent porosity of these superlattices.

Simultaneously, the atomic ratios of Pb2+/Zr4+ for the processed samples, as determined by ICP-OES (Supplementary Fig. 27), closely align with those of the pristine are close to that of the pristine PbI2@PCN-700 (~1.21), PbI2@NU-1000 (~0.74), PbI2@PCN-606 (~1.77) and PbI2@PCN-700 (~1.5). This alignment serves as lateral evidence of little to no decomposition of the PbI2 guest and underscores the high stability of the 3D Zr-MOF-based superlattices. Correspondingly, the Pb2+ concentration in each of the residual mother liquors, remaining under 5 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 28). Lastly, the FT-IR spectra of the treated superlattices exhibit no noticeable differences compared to the untreated ones (Supplementary Fig. 26). In summary, all these comparable experimental data robustly demonstrate the positive interaction between in-situ formed PbI2 sublattices and the intrinsic defects of unsaturated Zr6-nodes. This interaction results in the formation of porous, stable, single-crystalline superlattices, effectively preventing the degradation and leakage of PbI2 guests under diverse harsh conditions.

Additionally, we prepared two MOF-templated host-guest superstructures, 0D NU-600⊃PbI2 and 1D PCN-700⊃PbI2. SC-XRD reveals that the PbI2 units, stabilized by the supramolecular interactions with the channel surfaces, decompose readily in DMF to produce the pristine MOF single crystals (Extended Data Figs. 6-7). This stability behaviour is quite different from that of the superlattice structures.

#### ICP-OES data

Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis was performed to determine the Pb2+content (increased from 7.1% to 29.4%) and calculate the ratio of the Pb2+/Zr4+ (rose from 0.3 to 1.9) for PbI2@PCN-606, providing insights into the progressive growth process of PbI2 guest over time (Supplementary Fig. 17). In the final state of PbI2@PCN-606, the experimental Pb2+/Zr4+ratio (~1.9) was found to be lower than the theoretically calculated value (~2.9) based on single-crystal data. This discrepancy may be attributed to the presence of disorder and the restricted capacity for PbI2 occupancy within the MOF scaffolds.

#### Physical properties

The Raman spectrum of these porous multidimensional PbI2@MOF superlattices has now been tested. For comparison, the template-free PbI2 nanorods, prepared in DMF/MeOH solution (Extended Data Figs. 8), displayed the well-known Raman spectrum with peaks at Eg, A1g, and A2U (Supplementary Fig. S39). In contrast, all PbI2@MOF superlattices showed two prominent Raman bands around the Eg and A2u, vibration modes, with notable peak broadening and shifts. This broadening can be attributed to the distorted cuboid PbI2 clusters<sup>51</sup>. The severe peak broadening makes it challenging to deconvolute these scattering peaks and accurately assign their origins, as well as to understand their correlation with electron-phonon interactions as dimensionality evolves in the PbI2 superlattices. Nevertheless, we do observe dimensionality-dependent Raman scattering, which warrants further investigation in future studies.

Transient photocurrent measurements and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were conducted to further explore the structure-photoelectric activity relationship for different PbI2@MOF superlattices and intermediates.

As expected, the 2D PbI2@PCN-606 exhibits the highest photocurrent response, surpassing that of the intermediates PbI2@PCN-606 (I-IV), half-filled 2D PbI2@PCN-609, 1D PbI2@PCN-700, and 0D PbI2@NU-1000 superlattices. This indicates that the dimensionality of the PbI2 sublattice, with its atomic level of heterostructures, significantly influences the photoelectric response. The ElS curves also reveal that the radius of the semicircular Nyquist plot for the 2D PbI2@PCN-606 nanosheet (115 Ω) is much smaller than those of the intermediates PbI2@PCN-606 (I-IV), halffilled 2D PbI2@PCN-609, 1D PbI2@PCN-700, 0D PbI2@NU-1000 superlattices, indicating the

lowest charge-transfer resistance.

Based on the photocurrent response and the charge-transfer resistance results (Supplementary Figs. 43-46), it can be inferred that the electron-hole separation and transference efficiency of the 2D PbI2@PCN-606 are superior to those of the half-filled 2D PbI2@PCN-609, 1D PbI2@PCN-700, 0D PbI2@NU-1000 superlattices and the intermediates PbI2@PCN-606 (I-IV). This superiority is attributed to an increased presence of new excitons and interface channels originating from the PbI2 sublattice, effectively mitigating undesired volume charge recombination. Additionally, the cyclic voltammetry curves also demonstrates that the PbI2@MOF superlattices show stronger redox activity compared to the MOF templates.

#### Physical mechanism of PL emission

In general, the confined growth of PbI2 in different MOF templates results in variations in configurations, sizes, and dimensionalities of PbI2 sublattices, thereby creating PbI2@MOF superlattices with different band gap structures. Compared to rodlike PbI2 crystals (Extended Data Fig. 8), the unique coordination geometry of each MOF templates leads to PbI2 sublattices with different sizes and dimensionality (0D to 2D) and varying degree of lattice distortion (Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Figs. 58-60). Upon introducing selected (chiral) amine molecules, amine-modified PbI2@MOF superlattices show significantly enhanced perovskite-like luminescence (PL, circularly polarized light (CPL)) emission, indicating an electronic band structure closely resembling that of two-dimensional layered perovskites. However, we note the bandgap of 2D S-MBA/PbI2@PCN-606 is about 1.75 eV, much smaller than that of the layered perovskite with a band gap of around 2.5 eV. This reduced bandgap is likely to due to significant lattice distortion caused by the strong confinement of the MOF templates<sup>35</sup>, which leads in greatly narrowed bandgaps (theoretical analyses in Supplementary Fig. 61) along with severely broadened emission peak and Raman scattering peak.

Our studies further showed that the absorption edge of resulting amine/PbI2@MOF superlattices gradually shifts from 1.75 eV to 2.56 eV as the dimensionality of the building units decreases from 2D to quasi-2D, 1D and finally 0D. The CD, PL and CPL studies showed a similar trend with consistent peak positions, indicating the such transitions are originated from band edge transitions,

and the associated peak shifts can be largely attributed to additional quantum confinement effect of band edge transitions.

To understand the nature of PL in amine-modified PbI2@MOF superlattices, we conducted systematic PL lifetime, absorption edge analysis, temperature- and power-dependent PL studies. The studies together consistently show that the emission is from excitons, not defect states.

1) PL lifetime studies showed sub-nanosecond lifetimes (Supplementary Fig. 55b), which are characteristic of exciton emission in lead halide perovskites. In contrast, defect emission typically exhibits much longer lifetimes.

2) The absorption edge of the amine/PbI2@MOF displayed a consistent peak position, and the measured optical density right above the absorption edge is as large as that far above the absorption edge. This suggests band edge absorption rather than defect state absorption, as the latter usually features smaller absorption cross-sections and isolated absorption bands. Additionally, a similar trend in observed PL, CD and CPL emission with consistent peak positions, further supporting the band edge transition nature (rather than defect emission).

3) Temperature-dependent PL spectra of S-MBA/PbI₂@PCN-606, measured from 78 K to 300 K under above-bandgap excitation (Supplementary Fig. 53a), showed a decrease in emission intensity and a slight increase in full width at half maximum (FWHM) with rising temperature. This is typical of exciton emission: reduced radiative recombination and increased thermal broadening. The emission peak position remains largely unchanged due to temperature effects on thermal expansion and electron-phonon interactions, which are influenced by strong confinement and lattice distortion in the superlattice. In contrast, defect emission shows peak width (FWHM) and emission intensity that are less affected by temperature.

4) Power-dependent PL spectra at 78 K revealed that peak intensities follow a power law with a power index of around 1 at low excitation power (Supplementary Figs. 51 and 52). Moreover, the peak position and FWHM remain unchanged with varying excitation power, confirming the nature of exciton emission. The linear dependence on excitation intensity demonstrate that the emission does not arise from permanent defects<sup>52</sup> and the power-law dependence of 1<slope<2 implies that excitons are the nature of optical transitions in the materials<sup>53</sup>. The saturation of emission intensity at high excitation power is likely due to the limited density of states in the confined

structures. The stability of the emission peak position and FWHM regardless of excitation power suggests that laser heating effects are negligible.

Overall, the above results show that the exciton emission of amine/PbI₂@MOF and the confined PbI₂ sublattice structures modifies thermal-induced lattice constant changes and electron-phonon coupling strength while also limiting the density of states. This leads to a temperature-independent emission peak position and an excitation power-independent emission peak position and FWHM. However, the amine/PbI₂@MOF structure is complex, and this preliminary spectroscopy study requires further investigation to uncover more optical phenomena.

We believe that the dimensionality-dependent bandgap and PL shifts would be ascribed to the convoluted effect of the quantum confinement effect33,34 and the lattice distortion induced the bandgap narrowing effect<sup>35</sup> .

#### 2. Synthesis of MOF templates54-57 and template-free PbI2 nanorods

Synthesis of NU-1000. ZrOCl2·8H2O (388 mg, 1.20 mmol) and benzoic acid (10.8 g, 88.43 mmol) were mixed in N,N-diethylformamide (DEF, 32 mL) in a 100 mL medium bottle and ultrasonically dissolved. The clear solution was incubated in an oven at 100 °C for 1 h. After cooling down to

room temperature H4TBAPy (160 mg, 0.24 mmol) was added and the mixture was sonicated for 20 min. The yellow suspension was placed in a preheated oven at 100 °C for 2 d. Additionally, a small amount of trifluoroacetic acid was introduced as a modulator into the reaction mixture prior to heating in order to enhance crystallinity. NU-1000 single crystals were solvent-exchanged to absolute ethanol by changing the supernatant liquid to fresh ethanol three times over the course of three days.

Synthesis of PCN-700. A mixture of ZrCl4 (9 mg), H2Me2-BPDC (9 mg), trifluoroacetic acid (0.08 ml) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 1.5 mL) was sealed in a 20 mL vial with a screw cap and the vial was heated at 120 <sup>o</sup>C for 24 h. Colorless crystals were collected, washed with DMF and acetone, and dried in air. (8 mg, 65%)

Synthesis of PCN-606. A mixture of ZrCl4 (20 mg), H4TPCB (10 mg), benzoic acid (500 mg), and DMF (3 mL) was sealed in a 20 mL vial with a screw cap and the vial was heated at 120 °C for 48 h. Yellow crystals were collected, washed with DMF and acetone, and dried in air. (11 mg, 71%)

Synthesis of PCN-609. A mixture of ZrCl4 (20 mg), H4CBTB (10 mg), benzoic acid (430 mg), and DMF (3 mL) was sealed in a 20 mL vial with a screw cap and the vial was heated at 120 °C for 48 h. Yellow crystals were collected, washed with DMF and acetone, and dried in air. (12 mg, 67%)

Synthesis of NU-600. A mixture of ZrCl4 (10 mg), H4TCPB-Br2 (8 mg), DEF (2 mL), and acetic acid (0.8 mL) was sealed in a 20 mL vial and ultrasonically dissolved. The clear mixture was sealed and heated at 120 °C for 24 h. After cooling down to room temperature, cubic crystals were collected, washed with DMF and acetone, and dried in air. (12 mg, 65%)

Synthesis of rodlike PbI2 crystal. Firstly, the PbI2 powder (20 mg) was dissolved in DMF (1 mL) in a 10 mL vial. Then, the solution was added into a 0.5 mL methanol drop by drop for 20 minutes. Finally, the rodlike crystals can be found in the bottle of vial. The crystals were then characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.

#### Synthesis of MOF⊃PbI2 superstructure

The activated MOF crystals (5 mg) were added into a 20 mL vial Pyrex tube with PbI2 (20 mg) and DMF (4 mL), slowly adding the methanol (2 mL) drop by drop for 20 minutes. The vial was

then placed in a stable, undisturbed position to allow sufficient time (8-10 d) for confined growth of host-guest structures of MOF⊃PbI2. The crystals were then characterized by single-crystal Xray diffraction.
