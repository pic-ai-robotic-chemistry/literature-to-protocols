# **Direct photo-oxidation of methane to methanol over a mono-iron hydroxyl site**

**Bing An <sup>1</sup> , Zhe Li  2,3, Zi Wang1 , Xiangdi Zeng1 , Xue Han1 , Yongqiang Cheng4, Alena M. Sheveleva  1,5, Zhongyue Zhang6, Floriana Tuna 1,5, Eric J. L. McInnes  1,5, Mark. D. Frogley <sup>7</sup> , Anibal J. Ramirez-Cuesta  4, Louise S. Natrajan1 , Cheng Wang  <sup>2</sup> , Wenbin Lin  <sup>3</sup> , Sihai Yang <sup>1</sup>** ✉ **and Martin Schröder <sup>1</sup>** ✉

**Natural gas, consisting mainly of methane (CH4), has a relatively low energy density at ambient conditions (~36 kJ l−<sup>1</sup> ). Partial oxidation of CH4 to methanol (CH3OH) lifts the energy density to ~17 MJ l−<sup>1</sup> and drives the production of numerous chemicals. In nature, this is achieved by methane monooxygenase with di-iron sites, which is extremely challenging to mimic in artificial systems due to the high dissociation energy of the C–H bond in CH4 (439 kJ mol−<sup>1</sup> ) and facile over-oxidation of CH3OH to CO and CO2. Here we report the direct photo-oxidation of CH4 over mono-iron hydroxyl sites immobilized within a metal–organic framework, PMOF-RuFe(OH). Under ambient and flow conditions in the presence of H2O and O2, CH4 is converted to CH3OH with 100% selectivity and a time yield of 8.81 ± 0.34 mmol gcat−<sup>1</sup>  h−<sup>1</sup> (versus 5.05 mmol gcat−<sup>1</sup>  h−<sup>1</sup> for methane monooxygenase). By using operando spectroscopic and modelling techniques, we find that confined mono-iron hydroxyl sites bind CH4 by forming an [Fe–OH···CH4] intermediate, thus lowering the barrier for C–H bond activation. The confinement of mono-iron hydroxyl sites in a porous matrix demonstrates a strategy for C–H bond activation in CH4 to drive the direct photosynthesis of CH3OH.**

The activation of the C–H bond in methane (CH4) is a major goal in catalysi[s1–](#page-5-0)[3](#page-5-1) . Homogeneous catalysts based upon transition metal complexes and polyoxometalates can activate C–H bonds in CH4. These systems rely heavily on highly corrosive reagents, such as sulfuric acid[4](#page-5-2) and trifluoroacetic acid[5](#page-5-3) , to stabilize the reacted CH4 and thus have substantial environmental impacts. Moreover, high pressure (usually >20 bar) is required for sufficient CH4 to dissolve in solution. Inspired by methane monooxygenase in nature, iron- or copper-exchanged zeolites have been applied to promote the activation of the C–H bond in CH4 (refs. [6–](#page-5-4)[9](#page-5-5) ). However, these processes operate at high temperature (>800 °C) and/or high pressure (>30 bar) and are thus highly energy intensive. Although the use of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) can drive the desired reaction under milder condition[s10,](#page-5-6)[11](#page-5-7), its high cost prohibits large-scale application. On the other hand, O2 is a cheap, readily available oxidant, but often leads to over-oxidation of CH4 to CO2 as the main product[12](#page-5-8).

Photocatalysts based on BiVO4, WO3 or supported noble metals (for example, Au, Ag, Pt, Pd) on TiO2 and ZnO can promote CH4 activation at room temperatur[e13–](#page-5-9)[18.](#page-5-10) Recently, photo-oxidation of CH4 to CH3OH has been achieved over FeO*x*/TiO2 using H2O2 as oxidant with a CH3OH selectivity of 90% and a time yield of 0.36mmol gcat<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> (ref. [17\)](#page-5-11). Porous metal–organic framework (MOF) materials incorporating immobilized catalytically active centres can act as thermocatalysts for CH4 activation, although high temperature and pressure remain critical to promote the activation of C–H bond[s19](#page-5-12)[–21.](#page-5-13) To date, light-driven partial oxidation of CH4 over MOF catalysts has not been achieved. Here we describe the efficient direct photo-oxidation of CH4 to CH3OH under ambient conditions over mono-iron hydroxyl sites confined in a MOF using H2O and O2 as oxidants. By judicious design of our catalytic system, an exceptional Fe turnover number (up to 2,269±88), catalytic stability (>120h) and an unprecedented time yield of 8.81±0.34mmol gcat<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> (or 18,908±730mmolmolFe<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> ) for the production of CH3OH from CH4 can be achieved.

#### **Synthesis and characterization**

Photocatalytic systems integrating multiple components can underpin synergistically the observed catalytic performance. UiO-67 was chosen as a functional platform to integrate the three crucial components of our photocatalyst: the photosensitizer (PS) [RuII(bpy)2(bpydc)] (L), (bpy: 2,2′-bipyridine; H2bpydc: 2,2′-bipyridine-5,5′-dicarboxylic acid) to absorb light; a multielectron redox-active polyvanadotungstate [PW9V3O40]6<sup>−</sup> (PW9V3) to activate O2; a mono-iron hydroxyl site to activate CH4 (Fig. [1a](#page-1-0) and Supplementary Fig. 1). The PS and PW9V3 were incorporated into porous UiO-67 in a one-pot synthesis to give PMOF-Ru, and the iron sites were then introduced by post-synthetic metalation with FeCl3•6H2O to afford PMOF-RuFe(Cl), ((PW9V3)0.1@Zr6 (μ3-O)6.1(μ3-OH)1.9(H2O)0.24(bpydc)3.7[(bpydc)FeCl2(THF)2]1.9(L)0.28). The structure of PMOF-RuFe(Cl) was established by a combination of thermogravimetric and elemental analyses, by NMR, infrared (IR) and XAS (X-ray absorption) spectroscopies, and by consideration of charge balance (Supplementary Figs. 2–4 and Supplementary Table 1). Powder X-ray diffraction patterns and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirm the retention of UiO-type structure and morphology in PMOF-RuFe(Cl) (Fig. [2a](#page-2-0) and Supplementary Fig. 5), and binding of FeIII to bpydc2<sup>−</sup> was

The photocatalytic performance of these catalysts was investigated first in batch mode in water with CH4/O2 (v/v=1/1, 1 atm) as feed gas and irradiation with visible light (*λ*=400–780nm) at 25 °C (Supplementary Fig. 17). #### **Mono-iron hydroxyl sites for CH4 activation**

We next sought to identify the binding sites for CH4 molecules. Isotherms for CH4 uptake in both PMOF-RuFe(OH) and MOF-RuFe(OH) (that is, in the absence of PW9V3) display strong adsorption of CH4, as evidenced by the initial steep rise and marked hysteretic desorption (residue of 0.5 CH4/Fe). This behaviour is not observed in PMOF-Ru (Fig. [4a](#page-4-0)), suggesting that the mono-FeIII species acts as a binding site for CH4. INS spectra coupled with DFT calculations enabled the direct visualization of the binding dynamics of CH4 in this system (Fig. [4b,](#page-4-0) Supplementary Text and Supplementary Figs. 30–32). At low loadings of CH4 in PMOF-RuFe(OH) (0.22 and 0.45 CH4/Fe), the intensity at <40meV increases due to the libration/vibration of adsorbed CH4 molecules and their recoil. The difference spectra of bare and CH4-loaded PMOF-RuFe(OH) reveal three major peaks. The broad peak I centred at 12meV corresponds to the restricted/hindered motion of CH4 on the adsorption site and is reminiscent of the libration/vibration in solid CH4 (ref. [42](#page-6-8)). Peaks II (50–55meV) and III (65–70meV) are assigned to changes in INS intensities of the MOF on adsorption of CH4, specifically in the (i) vibration of the –OH group with H moving perpendicular and parallel to the [(bpy)Fe-OOH] plane, (ii) wagging modes of H2O located out of and in the [(bpy)Fe-OOH] plane, (iii) wagging modes of H2O located in the [(bpy)Fe-OOH] plane and vibration of OH with H moving perpendicular to O-H within the [(bpy)Fe-OOH] plane and (iv) rocking modes of the two axial H2O ligands in the [(bpy)Fe(OH)(H2O)3]2<sup>+</sup> fragment (Fig. [4b,](#page-4-0) Supplementary Fig. 32). At a higher loading of 1.1 CH4/Fe, a notable increase in intensity of peak I is observed, but not of peaks II or III, indicating that the additional CH4 has a much weaker or zero interaction with the MOF structure. Interestingly, peaks I–III are all retained on evacuating the CH4-loaded PMOF-RuFe(OH) sample under dynamic vacuum at ambient temperature, which is consistent with retention and strong binding of CH4 within the pores. DFT calculations show that the interaction between [(bpy)Fe(OH)(H2O)3]2<sup>+</sup> and CH4 in PMOF-RuFe(OH) is primarily based on hydrogen bonds (Fe–OH···CH4=2.39Å) (Supplementary Fig. 33). EPR spectroscopic spin-trapping experiments combined with DFT calculations afford further insights into the C–H activation process. The EPR spectrum of irradiated CH4-loaded PMOF-RuFe(OH) with 5,5-dimethy-1-pyrroline *N*-oxide (DMPO) in water shows a six-line signal typical of the DMPO-CH3 radical adduct, suggesting the photo-induced formation of methyl radicals (Fig. [4c](#page-4-0), Supplementary Fig. 35a and Supplementary Table 12). A minor component, DMPO-OH, was also observed, presumably due to splitting of water. This is consistent with the observation that the catalytic system can be quenched by radical scavengers (Supplementary Fig. 36). In contrast, no radical adduct was observed in the absence of catalysts or if the reaction was carried out over PMOF-Ru (Fig. [4c](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Fig. 35b). This suggests that CH4 adsorbed at the iron–hydroxyl sites attains a lower energy state with a reduced activation barrier to the formation of •CH3 radicals (Fig. [4d](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Text). Interestingly, DFT calculations suggest that the radical is not completely 'free' but is stabilized by the iron–hydroxyl sites to form Fe(OH2)···•CH3 interactions [HH2O··· CCH3=2.02Å], thus importantly inhibiting C–C coupling reactions (Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38 and Supplementary Tables 13–16). EPR trapping studies undertaken as a function of O2 also show signals for •DMPO-OH (Fig. [4c](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Fig. 39), implying that •OH is the reactive oxygen intermediate rather than 1 O2, O2 •− or •OOH; this is also demonstrated by the corresponding quenching experiments (Supplementary Table 17).

#### **Mechanism studies**

We propose that the photocatalytic cycle is initiated by photoinduced electron transfer from the excited-state PS\* of [Ru(bpy)2 (bpydc)]\* to produce reduced PW9V3 IV/V, as confirmed by the in situ EPR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 40, blue line), photoluminescent (Supplementary Figs. 41–43 and Supplementary Table 18) and electrochemical studies (Supplementary Fig. 44). The photocurrent response and electrochemical impedance spectroscopic studies confirm a notable enhanced efficiency for charge separation in PMOF-RuFe(OH) compared with partially functionalized systems (Supplementary Fig. 45). The electron-rich PW9V3 IV/V then drives the photoreduction of O2 to H2O2 via proton-coupled (from H2O) electron transfer (from reduced PW9VIV/V3), and H2O2 can be detected after illumination of PMOF-Ru for 5h in the presence of O2. In contrast, no H2O2 was detected in the reaction over PMOF-RuFe(OH) owing to the rapid conversion of H2O2 formed in situ to •OH radicals promoted by the {Fe-OH} moiety under illumination[44](#page-6-10)[,45](#page-6-11) (Supplementary Fig. 46), which is entirely consistent with the positive shift of the onset potential for reduction of H2O2 with PMOF-RuFe(OH) (Supplementary Fig. 47a). Also, addition of H2O2 enhances the production of CH3OH under irradiation (Supplementary Table 17, entry 6). The formation of •OH radical is also confirmed by a fluorescence spectroscopic study using terephthalic acid as a probe (Supplementary Fig. 47b). Thus, C–H activation proceeds via a pathway where the adsorbed CH4 interacts with the mono-iron hydroxyl moiety and is oxidized readily to •CH3 radical, which is stabilized by the iron site (Supplementary Fig. 48). The H/D kinetic isotope effect (KIE, *k*H/*k*D) using CH4 and CD4 gave a value for KIE (kCH3OH/kCD3OD) of 3.0±0.4, which represents a typical value for the primary kinetic isotope effect and suggests that the C–H bond dissociation is the rate-determining step (Supplementary Figs. 49 and 50). CH3OH is then formed by coupling of •CH3 and •OH radicals and extracted from the MOF by water. The high CH3OH selectivity is attributed to its rapid desorption owing to the relatively weak adsorption within the MOF matrix in the presence of water (Supplementary Figs. 51–54). Also, light irradiation effectively quenches •OOH intermediates to avoid over-oxidation of CH3OH (Supplementary Table 17, entries 7–10). To achieve optimal production of CH3OH, all components (that is, MOF, photosensitizer, polyvanadotungstate and mono-iron hydroxyl species) within the cycle must function cooperatively. The UiO-67 framework provides a stable platform to achieve this synergy to afford a higher activity compared with soluble methane monooxygenase incorporating di-iron centres (Supplementary Fig. 55)[7](#page-5-20) .

#### **Continuous-flow catalysis**

Building on the observed high activity of PMOF-RuFe(OH), we have developed a continuous-flow photo-oxidation process, where a flow of CH4/O2-saturated water (15mlh<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ) is passed through a packed layer of PMOF-RuFe(OH) catalyst under irradiation (Fig. [1c\)](#page-1-0). Importantly, the reacted CH4 over PMOF-RuFe(OH) includes both the water-dissolved CH4 and part of the gaseous CH4 passing through the branch of the dropping funnel (marked with a red line in Fig. [1c](#page-1-0)). It is the latter that breaks the limitation of CH4 solubility because CH4 can be directly adsorbed by the MOF, a feature that is distinct from conventional non-porous photocatalysts. Thus, this creates a dynamic gas/solid/liquid interface to maximize the contact between CH4, O2, H2O and the catalyst. Using this simple set-up, CH3OH was obtained as the only product in water with 100% selectivity and an accumulated Fe-TON of 2,269±88 with high catalyst stability over 120h (Fig. [3c\)](#page-3-0). This allows an unprecedented CH3OH time yield of 8.81±0.34mmol gcat<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> (or 18,908±730mmolmolFe<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> ) to be achieved under entirely ambient conditions. PMOF-RuFe(OH) outperforms methane monooxygenase (*Methylococcus capsulatus* (Bath), 5.05mmol gcat<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> ) and state-of-the-art photocatalysts, such as q-BiVO4 (0.97mmol gcat<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> ), Pd/ZnO (3.04mmol gcat<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> ) and FeOOH/m-WO3 (0.21mmol gcat<sup>−</sup>1h−<sup>1</sup> [\)7](#page-5-20)[,14,](#page-5-18)[18](#page-5-10)[,34.](#page-6-12) An economic analysis of the synthesis of CH3OH via this direct photochemical approach using CH4 and via the state-of-the-art industrial process using syngas (CO/CO2/H2) over commercial Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 catalyst has been performed, demonstrating the great promise of PMOF-RuFe(OH) in practical applications (Supplementary Notes and Supplementary Fig. 56).

#### **Outlook**

CH4 is a very plentiful carbon-based energy source, but it has a substantial environmental impact as a greenhouse gas. Its direct photoconversion to CH3OH, a liquid fuel and carbon feedstock, is an important alternative route to harness this carbon feedstock. However, this is a highly challenging process to achieve since over-oxidation to CO2 is commonly observed. The confinement of mono-iron hydroxyl sites within a porous metal–organic framework matrix, PMOF-RuFe(OH), linked to a photoactive receptor, represents an effective strategy for the activation of C–H bonds in CH4 to drive the direct photosynthesis of CH3OH without the use of H2O2. Future effort will be devoted to assessing practical photo-oxidation of CH4 to CH3OH with high catalytic stability and high turnover.

#### **Methods**

**Safety considerations.** Although all the reactions were carried out at ambient temperature, mixtures of CH4 and O2 can be explosive. Terefore, all glassware and apparatus should be pressure proof with full appropriate containment and protective screening.

**Catalytic preparation.** The photosensitizer, [Ru(bpy)2(H2bpydc)]Cl2 and [(*n*-C4H9)4N]6[α-1,2,3-PW9V3O40] (TBA-PW9V3) were synthesized according to the literature methods[46,](#page-7-0)[47](#page-7-1). ZrCl4 (10mg, 43μmol), H2bpydc (9mg, 37μmol), [Ru(bpy)2(H2bpydc)]Cl2 (2mg, 4μmol) and glacial acetic acid (82μl, 1.44mmol) in the presence of TBA-PW9V3 (2mg) were dissolved in 1.5ml DMF and sealed in a 5ml vial. The vial was heated at 100 °C for 72h, and, after cooling to room temperature, the solid product was collected and sequentially washed several times with DMF and THF and dried at 50 °C under vacuum overnight. An orange powder, PMOF-Ru, was obtained in a yield of 52%. Inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) analysis confirmed the Zr/Ru ratio of 21.4, indicating 0.28 Ru per {Zr6} cluster, respectively.

For post-synthetic metalation, FeCl3•6H2O (60mg) was dissolved in 10ml THF and the solution added to a suspension of MOF (60mg) in THF (10ml). The resulting mixture was sonicated for 0.5h. After being stirred at room temperature overnight, the solid sample, PMOF-RuFe(Cl), was collected by centrifugation and washed with THF 4–5 times to obtain a colourless supernatant. The dried sample was then treated under visible-light irradiation in water for 2h, and PMOF-RuFe(OH) was generated. The Fe loading was determined by ICP-OES analysis to be 2.6wt%, corresponding to a Fe/Zr ratio of 0.31, and 1.9 Fe per {Zr6} cluster.

**Photocatalytic oxidation of CH4 in batch mode.** Photocatalytic oxidation of CH4 was carried out in a transparent reaction vessel with a magnetic stirrer. A 10mg portion of catalyst and 3ml H2O were added into a 50ml Schlenk tube equipped with a cross-shaped magnetic stirrer bar, and the tube was sealed and evacuated by three successive freeze–pump–thaw cycles. Two balloons filled with CH4 and O2 (v/v=1/1, 1 atm) were incorporated into the system through the necks of the tube, and the system was placed 10 cm in front of a Xe lamp with a 400nm cut-off filter (*λ*=400–780nm). The reaction was stirred at 1,000 rpm for 20h, after which the gas in the headspace of the tube was analysed both by gas chromatography (GC) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector and a Bruker Matrix MG5 FTIR spectrometer (resolution, 0.5 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ). 1,4-Dioxane (100μl) was added to the liquid solution as internal standard for GC analysis (column: HP-5, flame ionization detector). The organic phase was extracted with ethyl acetate and analysed by GC combined with 1 H NMR spectroscopy and GC-MS; the aqueous phase was analysed by HPLC.

**Photocatalytic oxidation of CH4 in flow mode.** The reaction was performed with a designed fixed-bed reactor. A 10.0mg portion of solid catalyst was packed onto the surface of a glass frit and placed underneath a dropping funnel filled with water. After the system was evacuated and sealed, the gas mixture of 1:1 CH4:O2 was introduced into the reactor from the top (2mlmin<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ). The rate of flow of water (~15mlh<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> ) through the catalyst bed was controlled by a valve, and the system was irradiated using a Xe lamp (*λ*=400–780nm) to promote reaction. The product was collected via the outlet into a round-bottomed flask and analysed for each 8h run.

**Inelastic neutron scattering.** The INS scattering experiments were performed according to our reported method[48](#page-7-2) at Spallation Neutron Source, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, USA. The loading of CH4 into the cylindrical sample container was performed volumetrically at room temperature, and the temperature was reduced to 5K for data collection. After reaching adsorption saturation, the cell was evacuated and cooled for INS collection to detect the presence of strongly bound CH4. The detailed procedure for the experiment is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 30. Background spectra for the bare MOF were subtracted to obtain the difference spectra. The modelling of the INS spectra is shown in Supplementary Methods.

**Electron paramagnetic resonance.** Continuous wave (CW) EPR measurements were carried out at X-band (*~*9.5GHz) on a Bruker Elexsys 500 spectrometer equipped with an Oxford Instruments temperature control system. EPR data were collected with modulation amplitude 10G, microwave frequency ~9.85GHz and microwave power ~6.325mW. The magnetic field was calibrated using 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl with *g*=2.0028. Theoretical modelling of EPR spectra was performed with the EasySpin (v.5.2.24) package[49](#page-7-3) embedded in MATLAB software v.2013a. Spin-trapping experiments were carried out at X-band (9.85GHz) and room temperature using DMPO as the spin trap, and a commercial Bruker EMX Micro EPR spectrometer. A modulation amplitude of 2G was used with

 a microwave power of ~2.0mW after testing for saturation of spectral lines. EPR measurements of the MOF catalysts were carried out as follows: the MOF sample was prepared by loading 25mg into a J. Young quartz tube (outer diameter, 4mm; inner diameter, 2.8mm). The sample was activated under dynamic vacuum (<10<sup>−</sup>5mbar) for 12h at 80 °C. Measurements were then conducted on samples as a function of irradiation and various gas loadings of CH4, O2 and CH4+O2.

DMPO was used as the spin-trapping reagent in these experiments. DMPO is a nitrone spin trap, which is largely used for trapping short-lived radicals with the generation of a more stable free radical, which is readily detected by EPR spectroscopy. DMPO has a β-hydrogen and yields adduct with hyperfine splitting due to both the β-hydrogen and the nitroxide nitroge[n50.](#page-7-4) DMPO was dissolved in deoxygenated water and then added into the photocatalytic system in the presence of CH4, O2 or CH4+O2. All the reagents and tubes were deoxygenated using Ar before use, and 0.5ml of the mixed solution was filtered and injected into a deoxygenated EPR tube. In situ EPR spectra were collected under visible light.

#### General Experimental Methods

<sup>1</sup>H NMR spectra were recorded on a B400 Bruker Avance III and B500 Bruker Avance II+ spectrometers and referenced to the proton resonance resulting from incomplete deuteration of D2O*d*<sup>2</sup> (δ = 4.79), CDCl<sup>3</sup> (δ = 7.26) and DMSO-*d*<sup>6</sup> (δ = 2.50). Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR) were measured with a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5-IR spectrometer in attenuated total-reflection (ATR) sampling mode across the range 4000-400 cm-1 . Elemental analyses for Zr, Ru, P, W, V, and Fe were performed on a Thermo iCap 6300 inductively-coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) and analyses for C, H and N were carried out on a CE-440 elemental analyzer (EAI Company). Mass spectrometry was performed on a Bruker Esquire electrospray iontrap (ESI) spectrometer with samples dissolved in the appropriate solvent, and data are reported as m/z. Thermal gravimetric analyses (TGA) were performed under a flow of air (100 mL/min) with a heating rate of 2 °C/min using a TA SDT-600 thermogravimetric analyzer (TA Company). Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded on a Philips X'pert X-ray diffractometer (40 kV and 30 mA) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). UV-Vis spectra were measured with Hitachi U-3010 spectrophotometer.

Adsorption isotherms for N2 were recorded at 77 K on a 3Flex or Tristar gas sorption analyzer (Micromeritics Instruments Corporation) to analyze the porosity of the catalysts. The samples were prepared by solvent exchange and outgassed at 80 <sup>o</sup>C under vacuum for 12 h. The specific surface area was calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method in the range of P/P<sup>0</sup> = 0.05-0.3. Micro- and meso-porous surface areas, pore volumes, and mean pore diameters were evaluated by the Horvath-Kawazoe (HK) method and the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method from the adsorption branches of the isotherms.

The morphology and size of MOF crystals were measured by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on a Quanta FEG 650 microscope. The ratios of Zr/Ru/Fe/W/V in the sample were quantified by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) using multiple regions over a sample on a Bruker XTrace instrument. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were performed on Phillips Analytical FEI Tecnai F30 electron microscope operating at an electron acceleration voltage of 300 kV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out with a Qtac-100 LEISS-XPS spectrometer with a hemispherical electron energy analyzer and a home-made reaction chamber. Monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV, anode operating at 300 W) was used as the excitation source. The error in the measured binding energies was ±0.2 eV. The binding energies in all spectra were calibrated according to C 1s peak at 284.8 eV, and XPS binding energies were taken from the NIST XPS database (https://srdata.nist.gov/xps/main\_search\_menu.aspx). Quantum efficiency measurements were performed using the standard ferrioxalate actinometric method.<sup>1</sup>

*In situ* synchrotron FTIR microspectroscopy experiments were carried out using the B22: Multimode InfraRed Imaging and Microspectroscopy (MIRIAM) beamline at the Diamond Light Source. Spectra were collected (512 scans) in the range 4000–650 cm−1 with 4 cm−1 resolution and infrared spot size at the sample of approximately 15 × 15 *μ*m.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and photocurrent measurements were conducted in a standard threeelectrode glass cell connected with a CHI760e electrochemical workstation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of samples were measured on an electrochemical workstation (Zahner Zennium). Emission spectra were measured on an Edinburgh Instrument FP920 Phosphorescence. Time-domain lifetimes were measured using the Time-Correlated Single Photon Counting (TCSPC) method on a Lifetime Spectrometer equipped with 450 W steady state xenon lamp, a 405 nm ps pulsed diode laser and a Hamamatsu R928P red-sensitive photomultiplier in peltier, air cooled housing. Kinetic data were acquired following 405 nm pulsed excitation using time correlated single photon counting and lifetimes determined by applying a tail fit exponential function and the goodness of fit judged by minimising Chi2 and residuals squared.

#### Synthetic Procedure for UiO-MOF Catalysts

ZrCl<sup>4</sup> (10.0 mg, 43 mol), H2bpydc (9.0 mg, 37 mol ), [Ru(bpy)2(H2bpydc)]Cl<sup>2</sup> (2.0 mg, 4 mol), and glacial acetic acid (82 μL, 1.44 mmol) were dissolved in 1.5 mL DMF and sealed in a 5 mL vial. The vial was heated at 100 °C for 72 h, and, after cooling to room temperature, the solid product, MOF-Ru, was collected and sequentially washed several times with DMF and THF and dried at 50 °C under vacuum overnight (yield: 51%). ICP-OES analysis confirmed the Zr/Ru ratio of 20 indicating 0.30 per {Zr6} cluster.

Post-synthetic metalation of mono-iron-hydroxyl species in MOF-Ru is similar with PMOF-RuFe(OH) to obtain MOF-RuFe(OH). The Fe-loading of MOF-RuFe(OH) was also determined by ICP-OES analysis to be 4.0 wt%, corresponding to a Fe/Zr ratio of 0.51, and 3.0 Fe per {Zr6} cluster.

#### Analysis of Products

The reactor was connected directly to a gas chromatographic (GC) analyser equipped with both thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and flame ionisation detector (FID) for analysis of the gaseous products. Before analysis, the gas in the reactor was used to sweep the GC lines 3 times. The amount of CH<sup>4</sup> was quantified by GC using an external standard with a Bruker Matrix MG5 FTIR spectrometer, calibrated using known concentrations of target gases. The conversion of CH<sup>4</sup> was calculated according to the following equation:

$$
CH_4 \text{ conversion} \% = \frac{mol_{\text{initial CH}_4} - mol_{\text{CH}_4 \text{after reaction}}}{mol_{\text{initial CH}_4}} \times 100\%
$$

The other gases (e.g., CO<sup>2</sup> on TCD, CxH<sup>y</sup> on FID) were quantified by the following equation by using quantified CH<sup>4</sup> as the standard.

$$
mol_{gas} = f(correction factor) \times Area_{gas} \times \frac{mol_{CH_4}}{Area_{CH_4}}
$$

After analysing the gaseous phase, the solid catalyst was separated from the liquor and the liquor analysed by GC-FID with 1,4-dioxane as an internal standard. The selectivity and productivity of CH3OH were calculated by the following equations:

*Methodal Selectivity* % 
$$
Methanol Selectivity % = \frac{mol_{produced methanol}}{mol_{initial CH_4} - mol_{CH_4 after reaction}} \times 100\%
$$

#### Isotopic Experiments

The isotopic experiments were performed with PMOF-RuFe(OH) using the general procedure for photocatalytic methane oxidation in batch mode except with isotopic sources. After the reaction, the products were collected and analyzed by GC-MS.

#### Leaching Test

For the leaching experiment, 10.0 mg of PMOF-RuFe(OH) catalyst in 3 mL of H2O was used. After reaction with irradiation for 20 h under 1 atm of CH<sup>4</sup> and O2, the solid catalyst was removed *via* centrifugation, and the supernatant filtered and split into 2 samples - one for GC analyses and one for a second reaction. The reactor was re-filled with CH<sup>4</sup> and O<sup>2</sup> gas for further reaction tests under irradiation. No additional product was observed in the second reaction.

#### Recycling Experiments

The best-performing catalyst, PMOF-RuFe(OH), was chosen for the recycling test for photocatalytic oxidation of CH4. After the general catalytic procedure, the headspace gas was analyzed by GC using a TCD, and the solid PMOF-RuFe(OH) was collected from the reaction mixture *via* centrifugation, and the supernatant analyzed by GC to quantify the yield of CH3OH. The recovered solid catalyst was washed for 3 times with H2O and added again to the reactor together with 3 mL of fresh H2O. The reactor was then re-filled with CH<sup>4</sup> and O<sup>2</sup> gases for a second reaction under irradiation. The catalyst was reused without any significant drop in activity for 10 consecutive runs.

#### Computational Setup

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 09 Program.<sup>4</sup> The structures of chemical species in solution-phase and gas-phase were fully optimized by using the B3LYP functional. 5,6 The 6-31+G (d) basis set was used for C, H, N, O and P, and the LanL2DZ (Lanl-2-double-zeta) was used for Fe, Ru, Zr, W and V. Solvation effects were accounted for by using the implicit solvation model, *i.e.,* SMD.<sup>7</sup> The model contains a solvent molecule in a cavity surrounded by a continuous polarizable dielectric medium, which responds to the distributed solute charges according to its dielectric constant. The solvent was set as water. The structures were optimised using the method described above. For the calculation of solvation free energies, both electrostatic and nonelectrostatic (dispersion, cavitation) energy contributions were considered. No explicit water was included in the catalyst models. The corresponding single point energy calculation was based on def2TZVP basis set for accuracy. The catalyst structure was modeled using [(bpy)Fe(OH)(H2O)3] 2+ , the {Zr6} cluster with formate as a capping groups, [Ru(bpy)3] 2+ and PW9V3. As bpydc2 ligands are coordinated to Zr<sup>6</sup> clusters, the coordinated carboxyl groups can be regarded as neutral groups (similar to ester), and thus the [(bpy)FeIII(OH)(H2O)3] 2+ fragment was used instead of [(bpydc)Fe(OH)(H2O)3]. The bpy was fixed and optimized, and CH<sup>4</sup> together with associated oxygen coordinated to the iron center were free to move.

Vibrational frequencies and polarization vectors were calculated using CP2K (http://www.cp2k.org)<sup>8</sup> , based on the mixed Gaussian and plane-wave scheme<sup>9</sup> and the Quickstep module<sup>10</sup>. The calculation used molecularly optimized Double-Zeta-Valence plus Polarization (DZVP) basis set<sup>11</sup>, Goedecker-Teter-Hutter pseudopotentials<sup>12</sup>, and the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange correlation functional<sup>13</sup>. The plane-wave energy cutoff was 400 Ry. The DFT-D3 level correction for dispersion interactions, as implemented by Grimme et al<sup>14</sup>, was applied, with a cutoff distance of 15 Å. The calculation was performed on Gamma point only, with no symmetry constraint. A cubic unit cell with a lattice constant of ~26.9 Å was used for the calculation for each system. Structural disorder and various possible local coordinates are represented within the unit cell, but their long-range effects are suppressed due to the periodic boundary conditions. Structural optimization was performed using the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shannon (BFGS) optimizer, until the maximum force is below 0.00045 Ry/Bohr (0.011 eV/Å). The finite displacement method was used for the phonon calculation, with incremental displacement of 0.01 Bohr (0.0053 Å). The INS spectrum was simulated using the OClimax software.<sup>15</sup>

Catalyst Model and Calculation of Values of p*K*<sup>a</sup>

To determine the protonation states of [(bpy)Fe(OH)x(H2O)4-x] n+ under the neutral pH conditions of the reaction, the p*K*<sup>a</sup> of the possible acidic sites, including coordinated water and hydroxyl groups, were calculated. The acidity constant (p*K*a) of a species X–H is defined as:

$$
pK_a(XH) = -\log K_a = \frac{\Delta G_{ps}}{2.303RT}
$$
 (eq. S1)

where Δ*G*ps is the Gibb's free energy of protonated X–H in the solution phase, and *K*<sup>a</sup> is the equilibrium constant<sup>16</sup> . Here, the Δ*G*ps is calculated from the thermodynamic cycle (Scheme S1).<sup>17</sup>

$$
XH(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta G_{pg}} X^{\cdot}(g) + H^{\cdot}(g)
$$
  

$$
\Delta G_s(XH) \downarrow \Delta G_s(X^{\cdot}) \downarrow \Delta G_s(H^{\cdot})
$$
  

$$
XH(aq) \xrightarrow{\Delta G_{ps}} X^{\cdot}(l) + H^{\cdot}(l)
$$

**Scheme S1.** Thermodynamic cycle used for calculating p*K*<sup>a</sup> of X-H.

Δ*G*pg and Δ*G*ps are the gas- and solution-phase free energy of deprotonation, respectively, and Δ*G*s(XH), Δ*G*s(X- ) and Δ*G*s(H<sup>+</sup> ) are the free energies of solvation for XH, Xand H<sup>+</sup> , respectively. From this cycle, Δ*G*ps can be derived as:

$$
\Delta G_{ps} = \Delta G_s(X^-) + \Delta G_s(H^+) + \Delta G_{pg} - \Delta G_s(XH) \tag{eq. S2}
$$

Thus, substituting *eq.S2* in *eq.S1*, the p*K*a of X-H can be obtained from *eq.S3*:

$$
pK_a(XH) = \frac{\Delta G_s(X^-) + \Delta G_s(H^+) + \Delta G_{pg} - \Delta G_s(XH)}{2.303RT}
$$
 (eq. S3)

The experimental value (-264.0 kcal mol-1 ) was used for the free energy of solvation of a proton Δ*G*s(H<sup>+</sup> ). <sup>18</sup> All values for *Gpg* (gas-phase free energy) were calculated by the CBS-QB3 method. 19,20 All values of Δ*G*<sup>s</sup> (free energy of dissolution) were calculated based on the M05-2X method combined with the SMD solvent model.21,22 Different methods were used here to ensure the accuracy and efficiency of calculation, in line with literature reports. 23,24

The p*K*<sup>a</sup> of all possible acidic sites of the model were calculated, and the results are summarised in Figure S11. The first deprotonation of [[(bpy)Fe(H2O)4] 3+ gives the complex with the hydroxyl group in the equatorial plane (p*K*<sup>a</sup> = 6.74) as opposed to the axial position (p*K*<sup>a</sup> = 7.74). As would be expected protonation of [[(bpy)Fe(H2O)4] 3+ gives 4+ cations with significant acidity, p*K*<sup>a</sup> = -17.97 and -18.97, while deprotonation of [(bpy)Fe(OH)(H2O)3] 2+ has values of p*K*<sup>a</sup> of 10.72 and 21.12. Under the reaction conditions of this study (pH = 7.0) the equilibrium structure [(bpy)Fe(OH)(H2O)3] 2+ was obtained as the catalyst model with one coordinated hydroxyl group and three water molecules on the iron site.

#### Calculation of the Activation of CH<sup>4</sup>

To further support the proposed mechanism of CH<sup>4</sup> activation, the profile of energy change from CH<sup>4</sup> to ·CH<sup>3</sup> radical was calculated. The Gibbs energy was calculated using B3LYP/6-31G\* to optimise the structure and obtain thermal correction for the Gibbs free energy. The thermal correction contains the correction factor of entropy caused by the changes of vibrational/translational/rotational degrees of freedom. Figure 4D gives the corresponding energy changes and the intermediates during the activation process of CH4. Adsorption of CH<sup>4</sup> onto [(bpy)Fe(OH)(H2O)3] 2+ gives a stabilization energy of -10.6 kJ mol-1 , which thus has a pre-activation effect on CH4. On oxidation of the Fe center, along with the calculated potentials, the barrier is 81.0 kJ mol-1 (0.84 V potential vs NHE) and the potential of photosensitizer of 87.8 kJ mol-1 (0.91 V potential vs NHE) was coupled into this step to give an overall energy change of -7.0 kJ mol-1 , which is thermodynamically favorable. The oxidative intermediate [Fe-OH∙∙∙CH4] <sup>+</sup> will then go through a transition state leading to the formation of the •CH<sup>3</sup> radical with a barrier of only 17.4 kJ mol-1 , the energy for which is delivered by the photosensitizer. After crossing the transition state, the energy drops by 10.6 kJ mol-1 to the final state. The overall energy change profile for CH<sup>4</sup> activation shows that the rate determine step is the electron injection from PS\* to the iron centre.

#### Justification of choice of methods in DFT calculations

The DFT calculation was employed (i) to optimise the intermediate structure and calculate the Gibbs free energies associated with the activation of methane and (ii) to calculate the pK<sup>a</sup> value in various structural models to identify the local structure of Fe site. Each of the calculations is different in nature for a system of such complexity, and the most appropriate method (based upon literature survey) was therefore chosen for each calculation rather than using one method throughout the study. Such methodology, using different DFT methods for calculations, has been widely reported previously in the literature.26,27

#### Inelastic Neutron Scattering (INS)

Direct visualization of the interaction between adsorbed CH<sup>4</sup> and the active sites is crucial to understanding the molecular details of adsorption and activation of CH<sup>4</sup> into CH3OH. INS is a powerful neutron spectroscopy technique to investigate the dynamics (particularly for H-containing compounds, such as hydrocarbons) of host-guest interactions because it has several unique advantages:

 INS spectroscopy is ultra-sensitive to the vibrations of hydrogen atoms, and hydrogen is ten times more visible than other elements due to its high neutron cross-section.

 The technique is not subject to any optical selection rules. All vibrations are active and, in principle, measurable.

 INS observations are not restricted to the centre of the Brillouin zone (gamma point) as is the case for optical techniques.

 INS spectra can be readily and accurately modelled: the intensities are proportional to the concentration of elements in the sample and their cross-sections, and the measured INS intensities relate straightforwardly to the associated displacements of the scattering atom. Treatment of background correction is also straightforward.

 Neutrons penetrate deeply into materials and pass readily through the walls of metal containers making neutrons ideal to measure bulk properties of the material.

 INS spectrometers cover the whole range of the molecular vibrational spectrum, 0-500 meV (0-4000 cm-1 ).

 INS data can be collected at low temperature (at 5 K in this case), where the thermal motion of the MOF material and the adsorbed CH<sup>4</sup> molecules is significantly reduced.

 Calculation of the INS spectra by DFT vibrational analysis can be readily achieved, and DFT calculations relate directly to the INS spectra, and, in the case of solid-state calculations, there are no approximations other than the use of DFT eigenvectors and eigenvalues to determine the spectral intensities.

NOTE: Generally, the characteristic absorption band of C–H bond occurs in three regions: 3010– 2700 cm-1 , 1475–1300 cm-1 and 1000–650 cm-1 . Since 1000–650 cm-1 falls into the fingerprint region, there are many influencing factors that are too complex to give an unequivocal identification. Thus, this study focused on the absorption band in the other two regions.

On loading of CH<sup>4</sup> into PMOF-RuFe(OH), the characteristic C-H stretching and bending modes at 3017, 1412 and 1305 cm-1 appear immediately. Additionally, the absence of vibrational modes at 1000-1200 cm-1 that could be ascribed to C−O stretching vibration from a Fe-OH…CH<sup>4</sup> moiety or to a C−H symmetric rocking of OCH<sup>3</sup> (formed *via* direct cleavage of CH<sup>4</sup> to form Fe-OCH3), indicates that the adsorption of CH<sup>4</sup> is not through a direct C···O interaction. **NOTE:** The process of electron transfer by luminescence quenching has been measured. The Ru(bpy)3-derived chromophore, [Ru(bpy)2(H2bpydc)] 2+ (PS, H2L) can be excited to the <sup>1</sup>MLCT excited state under irradiation, which efficiently transfers to the <sup>3</sup>MLCT state through intersystem crossing. The long-lived <sup>3</sup>MLCT state decays to the ground-state, which is accompanied with phosphorescence.<sup>52</sup>

The excited RuII -polypyridyl chromophores (PS\* ) in MOF-Ru can be efficiently quenched by PW9V<sup>3</sup> and/or Fe-species as electron acceptors, but not quenched by CH<sup>4</sup> as electron donors. This indicates that the PS\* was initially oxidatively quenched to generate PS<sup>+</sup> (Figure S41). PW9V<sup>3</sup> and FeCl<sup>3</sup> were added incrementally to a suspended MOF-Ru solution in H2O (containing 5 µM of PS) to afford the different molar ratios of PW9V3/Fe and PS. A luminescence spectrum was collected for each molar ratio by a fluorimeter with an excitation wavelength at 465 nm. Such oxidative quenching was supported by fitting obtained intensities at 610 nm to the concentration using the Stern-Völmer equation:

$$
\frac{I_0}{I} = 1 + K_{SV}C
$$

where *K*SV is the Stern-Völmer constant and *I*0/*I* is the ratio of luminescence intensity of PS at 610 nm in the absence and presence of PW9V<sup>3</sup> and Fe3+. The fitting of this equation confirmed that *I0/I* has a linear relationship with respect to the concentration of PW9V<sup>3</sup> (*CPW9V3*) with R<sup>2</sup> = 0.998 and *K*SV = 0.4865 ± 0.0081, and Fe3+ (*CFe 3+*) with R<sup>2</sup> = 0.998 and *K*SV = 0.3752 ± 0.0109, indicating a more efficient quenching by PW9V3.

The oxidative quenching mechanism is further supported by time-resolved photoluminescence measurements (Figure S42).

$$
\frac{\tau_0}{\tau} = 1 + K_{SV-d} \tau_0 C
$$

where *K*SV-d is the dynamic Stern-Völmer constant and τ0/τ is the ratio of luminescence decay intensity of PS after the addition of different amounts of PW9V3 and Fe3+ measured at the 610 nm emission peak (with 405 nm excitation). The Ru–polypyridyl <sup>3</sup>MLCT emissions exhibited linear quenching with different amounts of PW9V<sup>3</sup> (*CPW9V3*) with R<sup>2</sup> = 0.961 and *K*SV-d = (30.1 ± 3.8) × 10-5 µM-1 ∙ µs-1 and Fe3+ (*CFe 3+*) with R<sup>2</sup> = 0.992 and *K*SV-d = (7.8 ± 0.3) × 10-5 µM-1 ∙ µs-1 . This indicates that both static quenching (formation of quencher/Ru(bpy)<sup>3</sup> 2+ ion pairs) and dynamic quenching (eg. electron/charge transfers) occurs in this system.

#### **Supplementary Notes**

#### **Cost analysis of catalysts for CH3OH production**

We performed a simple calculation on the cost of the catalyst to gain a sense of its cost-effectiveness. Clearly, this cost analysis will be subject to change as external conditions, costs and supply chains vary and develop.

The total cost for the synthesis of PMOF-RuFe(OH) is ~\$4200/kg due mainly to the costs involving the preparation of the organic ligands, H2bpydc and the photosensitizer. The current industrial route for the synthesis of CH3OH from syngas (CO/CO2/H2) relies upon relatively high pressures (50-100 bar) and high temperature (220-240 <sup>o</sup>C) using a ternary Cu/ZnO/Al2O<sup>3</sup> (CZA) catalyst which was first adopted by ICI in 1966. The CZA catalyst needs to be activated at high temperature under a flow of H2, and thus, the total cost for activated catalyst is ~\$240/kg, less than for PMOF-RuFe(OH). However, CZA catalysts undergo relatively fast deactivation even in the absence of poisons, and more than one third of the activity, MTY (mass-time-yield, mol∙kgcat -1 ∙h -1 ), is lost over the first 1000 h (Figure S56).<sup>107</sup> Assuming that (i) the CZA catalyst has a selectivity for CH3OH of 55.7% with an average MTYCZA for CH3OH production of ~3.1 mol∙kgcat -1 ∙h -1 from syngas, 108,109 and (ii) the cost of both methane and syngas is \$0.03/m<sup>3</sup> , the Δcost (the difference in cost) (\$/kgCH3OH) for CH3OH synthesis using PMOF-RuFe(OH) from CH<sup>4</sup> against that of commercial Cu/ZnO/Al2O<sup>3</sup> catalyst from syngas can be calculated using the following equation:

$$
\Delta \text{cost}(\text{$f/kg_{CH3OH}$})
$$
\n
$$
= \frac{1 kg}{0.032 kg/mol} \times \left[\frac{\text{$4200/kg}}{\text{$MTY_{PMOF-RuFe(OH)} (mol/kg_{cat}/h) \times lifetime (h)}}\right]
$$
\n
$$
+ \frac{\text{$0.03/m^3 \times 0.0224 m^3/mol}}{100\%}\right]
$$
\n
$$
- \frac{\text{$240/kg$}}{\text{$MTY_{CZA}(mol/kg_{cat}/h) \times lifetime (h) \times 55.7\%}} \times \frac{\text{$0.03/m^3 \times 0.0224 m^3/mol}}{55.7\%}\right] = \frac{1.3125 \times 10^5}{MTY_{PMOF-RuFe(OH)} \times t} \times \frac{7500}{3.1 \times t} - 0.0167
$$

As shown in Figure S56, only when the MTY is up to 10.7 mol∙kgcat -1 ∙h -1 , is PMOF-RuFe(OH) competitive against the commercial catalysts for CH3OH synthesis. CZA performs at high pressure, while catalysis of oxidation of CH<sup>4</sup> to CH3OH under the atmospheric condition has a competitive advantage, including the cost of reactor and safety issues. For example, one stainless steel autoclave reactor with a Teflon vessel and quartz window used for high-pressure photo-reaction costs at least \$2500 depending on different pressure-proof capacities, although costs will of course vary according to different suppliers. However, the simple glasses kit used for atmospheric photoreaction in our flow or batch system costs less than \$120, 20x cheaper. Thus, the activity of PMOF-RuFe(OH) in flow mode has reached 8.81 mol∙kgcat -1 ∙h -1 which is only 18% less than the target MTY. Considering the mild conditions, the simple equipment and a 100% CH3OH selectivity, PMOF-RuFe(OH) appears to be a viable candidate and has potential for further development leading to commercialisation.
