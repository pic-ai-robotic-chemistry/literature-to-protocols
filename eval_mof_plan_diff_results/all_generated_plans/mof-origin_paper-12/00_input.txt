# Crystalline coordination framework endowed with dynamic gate-opening behaviour by being downsized to a thin film

Shun Sakaida1 , Kazuya Otsubo1,2\*, Osami Sakata3, Chulho Song4†, Akihiko Fujiwara4†, Masaki Takata4,5† and Hiroshi Kitagawa1,2,6,7\*

The fabrication of porous coordination frameworks in thin-film forms has been investigated intensively with a view to using their structural response to external stimuli and guests for potential nanotechnological applications, for example as membranes for gas separation. Here we report a coordination framework that exhibits a dynamic guest-sorption behaviour in a nanometre-sized thin-film form (16 nm thick), yet shows no guest uptake in the bulk. Highly oriented crystalline thin films of this coordination framework—which consists of interdigitated two-dimensional layers of {Fe(py)2[Pt(CN)4]} (py, pyridine)—were fabricated through liquid-phase layer-by-layer synthesis. The resulting thin film exhibited a clear guest uptake with a structural transformation of the gate-opening type as characterized by in situ X-ray diffraction. Increasing the film's thickness markedly suppressed this behaviour. We envisage that such a crystal-downsizing effect may be observed with other coordination frameworks, and may be of use to develop functional materials, for example, for switching or sensing devices.

S tructural characteristics of surfaces play an important role in the functional response of living systems to external stimuli such as light, temperature, electric fields and pH. Some examples of such stimuli-responsive surfaces include the nanostructures in the super-hydrophobic lotus leaf, the optical effects of the photonic crystal structure on beetle coloration and the stimuli-activated gating of ion channels located on the cell surfac[e1](#page-5-0)–<sup>5</sup> . To understand how the functional properties or processes of chemical reactions correlate with unique surface or interface structures that differ from the internal (bulk) regions, surface chemistry that utilizes several different approaches has been advanced successfully over the past decade. Taking inspiration from biological systems, the development of biomimetic materials, surfaces based on organic polymers and mechanically interlocked molecules with stimuli-responsive properties has attracted significant attention6[–](#page-5-0)<sup>9</sup> .

Among the promising candidates for intelligent nanomaterials on surfaces, coordination framework materials, typically referred to as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), and porous coordination polymers have been researched intensively[10](#page-5-0)–15. The variety of molecular building components—metal ions and organic-linker ligands —provides not only structural diversity (topology) and robustness, but also unique physical properties derived from their microporosity, such as gas sorption, electrical and proton conduction and catalytic properties<sup>16</sup>–[19.](#page-5-0) Whereas inorganic porous materials comprise metal–oxygen bonds, MOFs are held together by coordination bonds between metal ions and organic linkers that are relatively weaker than covalent bonds and reversible, which often results in a high degree of structural flexibility. In some cases, this flexibility means that structural transformations can be triggered by external stimuli, such as the sorption of a specific guest[20.](#page-6-0) One example of the guest-induced switching property is the 'gate-opening phenomenon': from a specific vapour pressure of guest molecules (the gateopening pressure), the sorption of those guest molecules promotes the rearrangement of the crystal structure of the host framework from a 'closed form' (which does not take up guests) to an 'open form' (which does)<sup>21</sup>–[24.](#page-6-0) This rearrangement is typically a sharp structural transition, and such gate-opening behaviour is often observed in relatively flexible frameworks. For rigid threedimensional (3D) frameworks ([Fig. 1a](#page-1-0)), the structural response is relatively insensitive to adsorbate loading, whereas characteristically a dynamic first-order structural transformation with hysteresis occurs at the gate-opening pressure in structurally flexible MOFs, such as 2D interdigitated frameworks and interpenetrated frameworks [\(Fig. 1b\)](#page-1-0).

To develop an efficient use of MOFs for future nanotechnological applications, including catalysis, sensing and separation by gaspermeable membranes, research on the fabrication, characterization and application of MOF thin films has expanded rapidly in recent years25–[29](#page-6-0). Among the MOF thin-film fabrication techniques proposed, three are well established bottom-up approaches for fabricating nanometre-sized MOF thin films: the layer-by-layer (LbL) method, a combination of the Langmuir–Blodgett (LB) and LbL

(LB–LbL) methods, and the modular assembly method<sup>30</sup>–[38](#page-6-0). The LbL method is a step-by-step approach in a liquid phase using structural components and self-assembled monolayer (SAM)-functionalized substrates, in which the substrates are alternately immersed into solutions of metal ions and organic ligands of the target MOFs[30](#page-6-0)–35. The LB–LbL and modular assembly methods are relatively new approaches for producing crystalline oriented MOF thin films. In the LB–LbL method, a 2D-layered MOF sheet is fabricated first on an aqueous solution via compression of a solution surface that contains metal ions and ligands, similar to an LB method. The step-by-step growth of the target MOF thin film with an intended thickness is then achieved by a repeated transfer of the 2D-layered MOF sheet onto the substrate (LbL)[36,37](#page-6-0). In contrast, in the modular assembly method, 2D MOF flakes are first synthesized in a solvent by solvothermal synthesis and used as structural modules of the MOF thin film. These pre-synthesized flakes are placed on a water surface, where they assemble into a sheet that can then be transferred onto a substrate. The process can be repeated several times to form a MOF thin film on the substrat[e38](#page-6-0). The development of these MOF thin-film fabrication techniques allows for the control not only of film thickness but also of the crystalline orientation, which can be confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) along both the parallel (in-plane) and perpendicular (out-of-plane) directions relative to the substrate surface. Although MOF thin films are expected to be more flexible than MOFs in the bulk state, the guest-induced switching properties of thin films have been investigated only rarely. To date, in situ XRD monitoring of structural changes in flexible 3D MOF thin films under water vapour has been studied[39,](#page-6-0) and showed lattice shrinkage along one direction (the perpendicular direction with regards to the substrate surface); however, no information was obtained concerning changes in the direction parallel to the substrate surface. Dynamic structural transformations, such as gate opening from a closed form to an open form, have not yet been observed directly for MOF thin films.

Here we show the guest uptake of a gate-opening type in a highly oriented crystalline thin film of {Fe(py)2[Pt(CN)4]} (1) (py, pyridine), a MOF that consists of interdigitated 2D layers. In the bulk state (particle size 135 nm), it exhibits no significant guest uptake. However, for a nanometre-sized crystalline thin film of the same MOF (crystal size, approximately 22 × 22 × 16 nm3 estimated from XRD observation), an obvious response to the vapours of guests, accompanied by a dynamic structural transformation, was observed by in situ surface XRD analysis. There are several reports on crystal-downsizing effects in MOF materials, in which changes in the guest-adsorption behaviour (kinetics), crystal structure and magnetic property are discussed40–[45.](#page-6-0) To the best of our knowledge however, such a change in the dynamic guest uptake by downsizing from the bulk to a thin film has not been observed previously (Fig. 1c).

Although there are many reports on the fabrication and characterization of MOF thin films, it remains challenging to create these films with a high crystallinity and well-controlled growth directions. In addition, sometimes their low electron density and thickness cause difficulties in characterization using XRD, which can provide direct structural evidence for MOF thin films. Hence, synchrotron surface XRD measurements on the obtained film-1-30L were performed to confirm its fabrication and investigate its crystallinity, including its orientation on the substrate surface. Figure 3 presents the synchrotron XRD patterns of film-1-30L both in the parallel direction (in-plane scan, grazing-incidence 2θ scan mode (Fig. 3a)) and in the perpendicular direction (out-of-plane scan, θ–2θ mode (Fig. 3b)) relative to the substrate surface (see the insets of Fig. 3a,b). As clearly shown in Fig. 3, the diffraction patterns observed for the two experimental geometries are quite different. Comparing the obtained diffraction patterns with the simulated XRD patterns calculated from the single-crystal X-ray structure of bulk-1, the diffraction peaks observed in the in-plane geometry could be indexed mainly as h0l, which reveals the periodicity of the 2D layer, whereas only 0k0 peaks, which correspond to the interlayer spacing mediated by the pyridine ligand, were observed in the out-of-plane geometry. However, two peaks assignable to the 111 and 311 diffraction peaks were also observed (Fig. 3a), which would not be observed in a perfect crystalline oriented thin film. These peaks did not originate from the bare substrate components (Cr or Au). To explain the observation of these diffraction peaks, we also performed rocking-curve (θ scan mode) and azimuthal-angle-dependence (φ scan mode) measurements at the 020 Bragg peak (Supplementary Fig. 13). In the rockingcurve measurement, a very weak single peak at θ of approximately 5.9° with a full-width at half-maximum of 4.5° was observed, which suggests that each 2D layer is stacked with an average tilting angle of ∼4.5°. Therefore, the observed 111 and 311 peaks originate from the canted nature of film-1-30L on the substrate. The result of the azimuthal-angle-dependence measurement clearly indicates the uniformity of the obtained thin film. From the 020 (out-of-plane XRD) and 001 (in-plane XRD) peaks, using Scherrer's equation the average thickness and crystal domain (island) size were calculated to be approximately 16 and 22 nm, respectively. From the atomic force microscopy (AFM) image results, several island structures of this thin film (thickness, 20–40 nm; side, <30 nm and 70–100 nm) were observed (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Specific sorption properties that arise from a thin-film state. As demonstrated above, the synchrotron XRD measurements provide clear evidence that the obtained film-1-30L is crystalline and exhibits a preferentially oriented nature on the substrate surface. The 2D interdigitated structure is expected to enable a guest-responsive structural change in the vertical direction, although bulk-1 shows no significant guest uptake. To investigate the characteristic response to the guest of film-1-30L, we then performed in situ XRD experiments under ethanol vapour at room temperature. In situ in-plane and out-of-plane XRD patterns under different ethanol relative pressure (P/P0) conditions are presented in [Fig. 4.](#page-4-0) No significant change in the in-plane diffraction pattern was observed, which indicates that the 2D layer remains unchanged before and after the introduction of ethanol [\(Fig. 4a](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Fig. 15). However, in [Fig. 4b](#page-4-0) for the out-of-plane geometry, both the 020 and 040 peaks are clearly shifted to the lower 2θ region under the presence of ethanol vapour (2θ = 11.51° for 020, where b = 15.44 Å at P/P<sup>0</sup> = 0.93), and the peaks are shifted back to the original position when the ethanol vapour is removed (2θ = 11.69° for 020, where b = 15.19 Å at P/P<sup>0</sup> = 0). These changes in the diffraction profiles are completely different to those of bulk-1 (Supplementary Fig. 4 inset) and indicate that film-1-30L transformed abruptly from the closed form to the open form, accompanied by the anisotropic expansion of the interlayer spacing induced by the ethanol vapour. We performed further in situ XRD measurements using other solvent vapours. Similar reversible peak shifts were also observed using relatively small molecules, such as water, methanol and acetonitrile, which indicates that such behaviour depends on the molecular size of the adsorbates (Supplementary Figs 16 and 17). The results obtained in this study show a switch in the guest-uptake property, accompanied by an anisotropic structural transformation from the closed form to the open one in a nanometre-sized thin film, which implies the existence of the crystal-downsizing effect in this system. To understand precisely this phenomenon, we further investigated the dependency of the thin-film size on the gate-opening behaviour. In this system, it is difficult to control the domain size of the thin film along the in-plane direction parallel to the substrate; however, the domain size along the out-of-plane direction (that is, the thickness) can be controlled using a simple LbL protocol. Three thin films of different thicknesses (film-1-60L, film-1-120L and film-1-150L) were fabricated successfully by varying the number of LbL cycles. The obtained thin films also exhibited crystallinity and a preferentially oriented nature, in a similar way to film-1-30L (Supplementary Figs 27–31). Although the domain sizes of each thin film along the in-plane direction parallel to the substrate were almost constant (19 nm for film-1-30L, 22 nm for film-1-60L, 22 nm for film-1-120L and 23 nm for film-1-150L), the average thickness of the thin film increased successively with the number of LbL cycles (16 nm for film-1-30L, 30 nm for film-1-60L, 45 nm for film-1-120L and 49 nm for film-1-150L (Supplementary Figs 32 and 33). From AFM observations, the island size along the parallel direction relative to the substrate seems to become larger and the interfaces among adjacent domains seem to become more unclear with increasing numbers of LbL cycles (thickness, 50–75 nm; side, >120 nm for film-1-60L (Supplementary Fig. 30)). Structural transformation of the gate-opening type observed in film-1-30L was suppressed by the increase of the thickness of thin film, which clearly demonstrates the existence of the effect of crystal downsizing on 1 (Supplementary Fig. 34).

#### Discussion

In this work, we discovered the gate-opening switching behaviour of 2D interdigitated MOF 1 realized in a nanometre-sized thin film. In addition, this dynamic structural response to the guests is not observed in the bulk-crystal state, and when the crystal size decreases down to the nanometre size, the thin-film state exhibits a selective guest uptake accompanied by anisotropic expansion along the vertical direction to the substrate surface, which is completely different from that of the bulk state. Moreover, the results of the LbL cycle dependency indicate that this type of gate-opening behaviour in the thin film arises from downsizing the film thickness. The origin of such a change in response to the guests (and the transition in an energetically stabilized guest-adsorbed phase) is not yet clear; however, in the thin-film state the diffusion length for guest molecules is decreased compared with that of the bulk crystal. The change in island morphology seen by AFM for the thicker thin films might also hinder the diffusion of guests. In the bulk state, we suggest that the guest penetration within the framework is prevented (or limited to the surface area) by the strong π–π stacking between pyridine ligands (see [Fig. 2\)](#page-2-0). However, the surface area is significantly increased on decreasing the crystal size down to the nanometre scale, where the ligand π–π interaction is not as effective as in the bulk because of the reduced number of ligands at the outermost surface. This situation would contribute to the lowering of the potential barrier height for a gate-opening-type guest uptake, which results in a specific response to the guests and adsorption kinetics in thin films that differs from those of bulk crystals. These findings provide insight towards the understanding of the mechanism that dominates the switchable sorption properties or host–guest interactions of MOF thin films, which may help their development for future applications, such as chemoswitching or sensing applications, gas separation, catalytic devices and integrated systems of functionality (for example, an innovative fuel cell).

#### Methods

#### Preparation of the {Fe(py)2[Pt(CN)4]} single crystal (bulk-1).

Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6H2O (196 mg, 0.5 mmol), K2[Pt(CN)4] (189 mg, 0.5 mmol) and citric acid (451 mg, 2.5 mmol) were mixed in 60 ml of water. Then, 1,3-diaminopropane (1.0 ml, 12 mmol) was added to the solution to yield a yellow transparent solution of Fe(NH3)2[Pt(CN)4]. An excess amount of pyridine (5.0 ml) was slowly diffused into H2O that contained this yellow transparent solution (10 ml) using an H-shaped glass tube at room temperature. Block-shaped single crystals of 1 were obtained after three weeks (Supplementary Fig. 1). Elemental analysis (% calculated, % found for C14H10N6FePt: C (32.77, 32.54), H (1.96, 1.91), N (16.38, 16.32)).

Single-crystal X-ray crystallography. X-ray crystal structure analysis of bulk-1 was performed using a Bruker SMART APEX II CCD detector with graphitemonochromated Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å) at 298 K. The details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Preparation of the {Fe(py)2[Pt(CN)4]} thin film (film-1). A Au/Cr/Si substrate (20 × 15 × 0.5 mm) was soaked in an ethanol solution of 4-mercaptopyridine overnight. The substrate was rinsed with ethanol and dried by N2 flow, then soaked alternately in the ethanol solutions of the building units Fe(BF4)2·6H2O (10 mM) and [(C4H9)4N]2[Pt(CN)4] (10 mM) at room temperature for the total of the intended cycles (30–150 cycles). Both solutions contained an excess amount of pyridine (100 mM). Every soaking process was followed by an ethanolwash treatment.

Synchrotron XRD measurements. Synchrotron XRD experiments were performed at room temperature with a multi-axis diffractometer with a scintillation detector installed on the BL13XU beamline of SPring-8, Japan. Helium gas was always supplied through the cell during the measurement. The relative vapour pressure for in situ XRD and grazing-incidence XRD experiments was controlled by tuning the ratio of vapour/helium using a BEL-Flow apparatus (MicrotracBEL Corp.).

Data availablity. X-ray crystallographic data for bulk-1 have been deposited in the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (CCDC) under deposition number CCDC 1405911, and can be obtained free of charge via [www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/data\\_request/cif](http://www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/data_request/cif).

### **1. Materials and Methods**

**General.** All the reagents and solvents were purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Industries Ltd., TCI Co., Ltd., Alfa Aesar Co, and Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. and used without further purification. The substrates were prepared by evaporating a 5-nm chromium buffer followed by 100 nm of gold onto polished silicon wafers (Au/Cr/Si, purchased from Nilaco Corp.). The typical substrate size was 20 × 15 × 0.5 mm<sup>3</sup> . H2-annealing was performed on the substrate before the thin-film preparation. Infrared reflection absorption spectra (IRRAS) were collected with a Thermo Nicolet NEXUS 670 FT-IR spectrometer and Thermo Scientific Spectra-Tech Collector diffuse reflectance accessory (incident angle = 80°). Raman spectra were recorded with a JASCO NRS-1000 using a microscope. A CVI Melles Griot 25 LHP 925 He–Ne laser provided the exciting line (632.8 nm). Thermogravimetric analysis was performed with a TG-DTA 2000SA (NETZSCH Japan) at a heating rate of 5 K per min in a constant flow of N2. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using an ESCA-3400 spectrometer (Shimadzu Corp.) equipped with Mg *K*α radiation as the exciting source (1253.6 eV). The binding energies were calibrated with the Au 4*f*7/2 signal (84.0 eV). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were obtained in tapping mode using NanoScope IIIa equipment (Bruker) with a Si cantilever. Adsorption isotherms for ethanol (EtOH), acetonitrile (MeCN) and benzene of the thin film sample were obtained with a BELQCM apparatus (MicrotracBEL Corp.). A sample fabricated on an Au electrode (5 mm *ϕ*) on a quartz tip (14 mm *ϕ*) was used. The vapour pressure in He gas was controlled by mass flow controller (MFC), bubbler, and condenser installed on a BEL-Flow apparatus (MicrotracBEL Corp.).

**Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (XRD) of bulk-1.** X-ray crystal structure analysis of **bulk-1** was performed using a Bruker SMART APEX II CCD detector with Mo *K*α radiation (rotating anode, λ = 0.71073 Å) at 298 K. The structure was determined using direct methods (SIR2004)<sup>51</sup> and refined by full-matrix least-squares refinement on *F*<sup>2</sup> (SHELXL-2013) <sup>52</sup> using the CrystalStructure software package53. Crystallographic data for **bulk-1** (C14H10FeN6Pt): Mr = 513.21, crystal dimensions 0.20 × 0.20 × 0.05 mm<sup>3</sup> , orthorhombic, space group *C*mmm (no. 65), *a* = 7.5075(8) Å, *b* = 15.2605(16) Å, *c* = 7.4436(8) Å, *V* = 852.80(16) Å<sup>3</sup> , *Z* = 2, *d*calc = 1.999 g/cm<sup>3</sup> , *F*(000) = 480.00, µ = 9.047 mm–1, *T* = 298(2) K. A total of 4,737 reflections were collected, of which 587 reflections were unique (*R*int = 0.0176). The refinement of 41

parameters led to the final *R*<sup>1</sup> = 0.0072 (*I* > 2σ(*I*)), *wR*<sup>2</sup> = 0.0173 (all data), goodness of fit (GOF) = 1.014, and residual electron density max/min = 0.243/–0.455 eÅ–3. CCDC 1405911 contains further information and details of crystallographic data for **bulk-1**. These data can be obtained free of charge via www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/data\_request/cif.

**Synchrotron X-ray powder diffraction (XRPD) measurements.** XRPD measurements of **bulk-1** were performed using a large imaging plate Debye–Scherrer camera installed on the BL02B2 beamline of the SPring-8, Japan. The incident X-ray was monochromated to 8 keV (λ = 1.550 Å) with a Si(111) double crystal monochromator. The samples were sealed in the glass capillaries, and all the XRPD patterns were obtained with a 0.01° step at rt.

**Sorption experiments on bulk-1.** The sorption experiments for water (H2O), methanol (MeOH), ethanol (EtOH), acetonitrile (MeCN), 2-propanol, chloroform, benzene (298 K), N2 (77 K) and CO2 (195 K) were performed using a BELSORP-max apparatus (MicrotracBEL Corp.). The as-synthesised **bulk-1** (approximately 110 mg) was dried under high vacuum (< 10–2 Pa) at 343 K for 12 hours before each measurement.

**Preparation of film-1-30L.** The starting material, [(C4H9)4N]2[Pt(CN)4], was obtained from the cation-exchange reaction of K2[Pt(CN)4] using [(C4H9)4N](ClO4) in methanol. Fe(BF4)2·6H2O, and py were used as purchased. First, the Au/Cr/Si substrate was soaked in an ethanol (EtOH) solution of 4-mercaptopyridine overnight. After this treatment, the substrate was washed with EtOH and dried by N2 flow. Next, the substrate was alternately soaked in two types of EtOH solutions of Fe(BF4)2·6H2O (10 mM) and [(C4H9)4N]2[Pt(CN)4] (10 mM) containing excess amount of py (100 mM), in this order for 3 min. at rt for a total of 30 cycles. In this work, we also varied the cycle numbers up to 150. By repeating this simple LbL protocol, thin films of **1** with various thicknesses (**film-1-60L**, **film-1-120L**, and **film-1-150L**) were successfully obtained. The substrate was washed with EtOH for 1 min. after each soaking.

**Surface XRD measurements.** The preliminary surface XRD measurements on obtained thin film samples were performed using a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer with Cu *K*α radiation (rotating anode, λ = 1.5418 Å). For the variable temperature measurements (Supplementary Fig. S9), a dome-type high temperature attachment (DHS-900, Anton Parr) was also used to control the temperature.

**Synchrotron surface XRD measurements.** Synchrotron XRD measurements on the obtained thin film samples were performed using a multi-axis diffractometer (Kohzu-Seiki TDT-17) with a NaI scintillation detector installed on the BL13XU beamline of SPring-8, Japan. The incident X-ray was monochromated to 8 keV (λ = 1.550 Å) with a Si111 double-crystal monochromator. In-plane and out-of-plane XRD patterns were obtained with a 0.1° step. Out-of-plane XRD measurement was performed in a typical θ–2θ scattering geometry. Rocking-curve (θ-scan mode) and azimuthal-angle-dependence (φ-scan mode) measurements were performed at the 020 peak position in the out-of-plane geometry (Supplementary Fig. S13) with 0.1° and 1° steps, respectively. For the in-plane XRD (grazing-incidence (GI) XRD mode), strong diffraction can be observed on the condition that the X-ray incident angle (α) to the sample is below a critical angle. Thus, an incident-angle dependence measurement at the 001 peak position was performed before the in-plane XRD measurement, and the diffraction pattern was collected at α = 0.15°. A Soller slit (Huber 3030-I, 0.4°) was placed between the sample and detector to improve the signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio of the diffraction. Both in-plane and out-of-plane XRD measurements were performed under constant He flow. The XRD profile fittings shown in Fig. 3 were performed using the Topas program54. The simulated XRD patterns shown in Fig. 3 were obtained using the Mercury software package<sup>55</sup> based on the perfectly oriented crystal model of **bulk-1**. For the *in situ* XRD measurements under vapours of several solvents, the vapour pressure in He gas was controlled by MFC, bubbler, and condenser installed on a BEL-Flow apparatus (MicrotracBEL Corp.).

**Molecular mechanics calculations.** Ideally guest-adsorbed model structures were obtained from molecular mechanics calculations installed on HyperChem Pro ver. 8.0.10 (HyperCube Inc.). The basic framework structure of **1** was generated from its single crystal X-ray structure and used as its expanded structure (3 × 1 × 3 supercell) without symmetry restriction. The lattice constants were set to the results of the *in situ* synchrotron surface XRD measurements under the vapour pressure of *P*/*P*<sup>0</sup> = 0.93. For geometry optimisation (MM+)<sup>56</sup> of the guest molecules, the framework structures were fixed, and the PolakRibiere optimiser was used (RMS Gradient: 0.001 kcal/ Å mol).

**Supplementary Figure 18 | Sorption isotherms for EtOH (circles), MeCN (triangles) and benzene (squares) of film-1-30L obtained from QCM measurements at 298 K.** For EtOH and MeCN, clear uptakes were observed. Although clear structural transformations with hysteresis are observed in *in situ* XRD measurements, their adsorption behaviours with slight hysteresis are not so clear compared with ideal gate-opening-type adsorption behaviour. This would be due to the difference in the substrates. For QCM measurements, thin film samples fabricated on commercially available Au electrode on a quartz tip without any annealing procedures were used. Because of the setup limitation for QCM equipment, Au on QCM tip has relatively rough surface compared with the Au/Cr/Si substrate for XRD measurements. Supplementary Figs. S19 and S20 show out-of-plane and in-plane XRD diffraction profiles of **film-1-30L** fabricated on QCM tip. For out-of-plane geometry, clear 020 and 040 diffraction peaks of **1** were observed, whereas any diffraction peaks were not detected in in-plane geometry. This means that average crystal domain size along in-plane (parallel to the QCM tip) direction is too small to be detected, or thin film does not have clear periodic order along in-plane direction, which are derived from the rough surface nature of Au on QCM tip. Therefore, it is suggested that the sorption isotherms obtained from QCM observe the summation of the guest uptakes of the thin film with a variety of domain sizes, resulting in the ambiguous sorption behaviour with slight hysteresis.
