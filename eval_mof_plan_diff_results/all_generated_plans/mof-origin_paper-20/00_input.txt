Metal–sulfur active sites play a central role in catalytic processes such as hydrogenation and dehydrogenation, yet the majority of active sites in these compounds reside on the surfaces and edges of catalyst particles, limiting overall efciency. Here we present a strategy to embed metal–sulfur active sites into metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) by converting bridging or terminal chloride ligands into hydroxide and subsequently into sulfde groups through post-synthetic modifcation. We apply this method to two representative MOF families: one featuring one-dimensional metal–chloride chains and another containing discrete multinuclear metal clusters. Crystallographic and spectroscopic analyses confrm structural integrity and sulfde incorporation, and the transformation is monitored by in situ total scattering methods. The sulfded MOFs display enhanced catalytic activity in the selective hydrogenation of nitroarenes using molecular hydrogen. Density functional theory calculations indicate that sulfur incorporation promotes homolytic metal–ligand bond cleavage and facilitates H2 activation. This work establishes an approach to construct MOFs featuring accessible metal–sulfde sites.

Highly polarizable components, such as sulfur-containing groups, in catalytic processes have emerged as a forefront area of research due to their unique ability to modulate chemical reactivity through electronic coupling. In this context, metal–sulfide compounds are of particular interest as they leverage the electronic flexibility of sulfur centres[1](#page-7-0)[–5](#page-8-0) , resulting in a more covalent nature of M–S bonds compared with their oxygen analogues, enhancing the charge-transfer properties that play important roles in catalytic processe[s1](#page-7-0),[6–](#page-8-1)[9](#page-8-2) . Nature's ingenious use of sulfur-containing species, such as Fe4S4, Fe–V–S, Fe–Mo–S clusters and Cu–S moieties, in the active sites of enzymes for critical redox reactions has afforded excellent reactivity within biological systems for transformations such as hydrogenation and dehydrogenation[8](#page-8-3),[10–](#page-8-4)[15](#page-8-5). Despite their efficacy, however, the fragility and complexity of enzyme systems often limit their scalability and industrial application[16](#page-8-6).

In contrast to enzymatic sulfur-containing species, transition metal–sulfur compounds, exemplified by MoS2 (refs. [17](#page-8-7)[–20](#page-8-8)) and CoS*<sup>x</sup>*

This necessitates sophisticated defect engineering to maximize exposure of active sites. It has been demonstrated that increasing the surface areas of these systems improves the catalytic performance as the active sites are more easily accessible, ensuring efficient mass transport[24](#page-8-13)–[27.](#page-8-14)

Beyond these well-known materials, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) represent a burgeoning class of materials with notable implications for catalysis. The structural robustness and high internal surface areas of MOFs make them ideal for serving as high-performing catalysts and hosting catalytic substrates[28–](#page-8-15)[30](#page-8-16). In fact, encapsulating enzymes within MOF pores can substantially enhance both stability and catalytic efficiency relative to those of the free enzym[e31–](#page-8-17)[34](#page-8-18). Furthermore, MOFs featuring open metal sites as reactive centres have been identified as promising candidates in catalysis, offering well-organized and protected reactive centres in a solid-state framework[30,](#page-8-16)[35](#page-8-19)[–37.](#page-8-20) However, the synthesis of MOFs incorporating highly polarizable components, such as sulfide bridges, metal–sulfur-based clusters or thiolate linkers, remains largely underexplored, with only a few known examples[38–](#page-8-21)[45.](#page-8-22) The primary challenges hindering progress in this area include the instability of metal–sulfur clusters, the comparatively lower crystallinity than metal–oxide-based MOFs resulting from the irreversible nature of forming highly polarized bonds, the poorly understood effects of templating agents on sulfur-containing structures, and the overarching need for novel synthetic methodologies to achieve desired topologie[s38.](#page-8-21)

Post-synthetic modification[46](#page-9-3) has emerged as a promising strategy to modify bridging linkers and/or transform metal clusters, such as metal–oxide clusters, into their sulfur counterparts by leveraging the inherent topology of parent MOFs, the extensive database of known structures and the simplified synthetic conditions required (Fig. [1a\)](#page-2-0). This approach not only mitigates the challenges associated with direct synthesis but also opens new avenues for the development of advanced catalytic materials.

In this study, we selected two families of specific MOFs with potentially redox-active metal centres and accessible open metal sites: M2Cl2BBTA (M = Co, Ni; H2BBTA = 1*H*,5*H*-benzo(1,2-d:4,5-d′)bistriazole; Fig. [1b](#page-2-0)) [47](#page-9-4)[–49](#page-9-5) and M-MFU-4*l*-Cl (M*x*Zn5−*x*Cl4(BTDD)3; *x* = 4 for M = Co and *x* = 2.5 for M = Ni; H2BTDD = bis(1*H*-1,2,3-triazolo[4,5-b][4′,5′-i])dibenzo [1,4]dioxin; Fig. [1c](#page-2-0)) [50](#page-9-6)[–52](#page-9-7) as parent MOFs. We converted them to their hydroxide-bridged versions, M2(OH)2BBTA and M-MFU-4*l*-OH, and subsequently to their sulfided versions, M2(SH)2BBTA and M-MFU-4*l*-SH, and elucidated their crystal structures using a combination of singlecrystal X-ray diffraction and electron diffraction techniques. In situ pair distribution function (PDF) analysis revealed real-time insights into the sulfidation process, enabling dynamic tracking of structural transformations during modification. We evaluated the catalytic performance of the sulfided MOFs with a model reaction using chemoselective hydrogenation of nitroarenes—the reduction of aryl –NO2 to –NH2. The sulfided MOFs showed markedly enhanced hydrogenation reactivity compared with their parent MOFs, which contain terminal or bridging chloride and hydroxyl groups. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations clarified the mechanism, showing sulfide ligands promote the homolytic cleavage of metal–ligand (M–X) bonds, leading to more efficient H2 activation. These integrated experimental and computational investigations reveal key reactivity trends and emphasize the crucial role sulfur incorporation plays in modulating the catalytic properties of MOFs.

#### **Results and discussion**

#### **Sulfidation of MOFs via post-synthetic methods**

For the post-synthetic modification, we selected two types of MOFs, M2Cl2BBTA and M-MFU-4*l*-Cl (M = Co, Ni), to investigate how isolated metal nodes or one-dimensional (1D) chains with adjacent centres affect the properties of hydrogenation reactions. Both MOFs have similar triazolate linkers as supporting ligands and comparable pore sizes (Fig. [1\)](#page-2-0). In enzymes, nitrogen-containing heterocyclic groups such as imidazoles have been proposed to extend the conjugation system for electron tunnelling with metal–sulfide clusters[53.](#page-9-0) Similarly, the triazolate groups in these MOFs can potentially act as *π*-acceptors to enhance the electronic polarizability of the metal centre[54](#page-9-1)[–56.](#page-9-2) With these two types of parent MOFs in hand, we then performed post-synthetic modifications to install metal–sulfur linkages.

In M2Cl2BBTA (M = Co, Ni), chloride serves as a bridging ligand between adjacent metal centres, providing a well-defined, substitutionally labile bridge that can be targeted easily to access other functionalities. During our initial attempts at replacing chloride in the presence of H2S, we observed slow and incomplete conversion to the SH variant (for example, ~50% Cl replaced by SH after 48 h). We presume this limited reactivity is due to the formation of the by-product HCl, which is a strong acid and drives the reaction backward. To circumvent this issue, we elected to treat the chloride-based MOFs with KOH to transform the bridging chloride anions into bridging hydroxyl groups, with the MOF products denoted as M2(OH)2BBTA. Replacement of the chloride was confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 12), while the integrity of the frameworks was confirmed by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) and N2 sorption isotherms collected at 77 K (Supplementary Fig. 6). We reasoned that bridging hydroxyl groups would make the subsequent sulfidation reaction more feasible as water would be the leaving group. Indeed, reacting M2(OH)2BBTA with in situ-generated H2S converted the lightly coloured MOF powders to dark brown within minutes, with ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra showing broad absorption bands in the visible-light range (Supplementary Fig. 56) and the *ν*S–H bands appears on the infrared spectra (Supplementary Fig. 57), signalling the formation of the SH-bridging analogues M2(SH)2BBTA (Fig. [2\)](#page-3-0). XPS (Supplementary Tables 6 and 9) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopic (EDS) analysis (Supplementary Figs. 21 and 23) revealed the anticipated 1:1 ratio between metal and sulfur, which suggests complete replacement of the hydroxyls with sulfhydryls. Importantly, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirmed that these post-synthetic modifications did not affect the particle size or morphology (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 22). We note that the Co:S ratio can exceed 1:1 when excess H2S is used (Supplementary Fig. 21), which can be attributed to the formation of polysulfides in the MOF cavities.

For the M2X2BBTA series (M = Co, Ni; X = Cl, OH, SH), single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) or electron diffraction experiments enabled us to determine the solid-state structures of these MOFs. All structures were solved under the *R*-*3*m space group with similar unit cell parameters (Supplementary Table 1), indicating that the parent MOFs' topology was preserved through these transformations. The simulated PXRD patterns closely match those of the experimental data (Fig. [3a](#page-4-0) and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2), which confirms the phase purity of the bulk samples. Examination of the coordination sphere revealed that all metal ions are octahedrally coordinated and surrounded by three nitrogen atoms from different triazolate linkers. Two additional sites are occupied by X atoms from bridging groups, and the final site by terminal water molecules, except in the SH version. Notably, in the SH version, we discovered a disordered S5 chain bridging two cobalt atoms across two distinct 1D metal chains. This corresponds well with the high Co:S ratios observed when excess H2S was used in the synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 21). The sulfur contents of the sulfided samples were also confirmed by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (Supplementary Table 18). The Co–N bond distances shortened from approximately 2.1 Å to 2.0 Å and 1.9 Å as the bridging groups changed from –Cl to –OH to –SH, respectively. Specifically, these unique in situ total scattering experiments followed the local rearrangement during the dechlorination of Co2Cl2BBTA to Co2(OH)2BBTA and the subsequent sulfidation of Co2(OH)2BBTA to Co2(SH)2BBTA.

#### **XPS analysis of sulfur incorporation**

Following the successful conversion of MOFs to their sulfided versions, confirmed by X-ray techniques, XPS spectroscopy was used to further identify the oxidation state of the sulfided MOFs in the post-synthetically modified samples. For the M2X2BBTA series, the results indicate that after the treatments with KOH, there is no chloride left in the samples (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9, 11 and 12 and Supplementary Tables 4, 5, 7 and 8). After the H2S treatment, a peak of binding energy (BE) at ~163 eV (162.7 and 162.6 eV for Co2(SH)2BBTA and Ni2(SH)2BBTA, respectively) starts appearing on the XPS spectra (Fig. [3e](#page-4-0), Supplementary Figs. 10 and 13 and Supplementary Tables 6 and 9), which falls into the range of –SH functional groups with sulfur in the −2 oxidation state. The M2Cl2BBTA MOFs showed minimal conversion (that is, close to that of the background reaction without the inclusion of MOFs; Supplementary Figs. 29 and 32), and the conversion rate improved slightly for both M2(OH)2BBTA samples with ~9% and 18% conversion for M = Co and Ni, respectively. In contrast, M2(SH)2BBTA exhibited a notable improvement in the conversion rate to ~37% for nickel and ~100% for cobalt. Interestingly, for the M-MFU-4*l*-X series, M = Co showed relatively low conversion when X = Cl (~4%), X = OH (~1%) and even when X = SH (~23%). In contrast, the Ni-MFU-4*l*-X analogues demonstrated relatively higher conversion rates (~8% for X = Cl and ~20% for X = OH), and Ni-MFU-4*l*-SH achieved >99% conversion under these conditions. Cycling studies revealed that both Co2(SH)2BBTA and Ni-MFU-4*l*-SH showed consistent conversion rates of >99% under the same conditions. However, the TOF for Ni-MFU-4*l*-SH at the 5% catalyst loading was measured to be 1.7 ± 0.1 h−1) under analogous conditions.

#### **Mechanistic elucidation with density functional theory calculations**

To elucidate the experimentally observed reactivity trends in nitroarene hydrogenation, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Starting with extended crystals constrained by periodic boundary conditions, we constructed finite-sized cluster models by truncating the framework around a metal node (Extended Data Fig. 1). Using these cluster models, we propose a reaction pathway that begins with the coordination of methanol solvent molecule(s) to the metal centres, followed by M–X bond cleavage to generate an open metal site, H2 adsorption and subsequent H–H bond dissociation, ultimately forming the active species **E** (Fig. [5a\)](#page-6-0) responsible for reduction catalysis[63,](#page-9-13)[64.](#page-9-14)

Experimentally, we found that methanol is critical for the catalysis. To probe its role, we systematically explored methanol coordination effects (Supplementary Fig. 42) and observed distinct solvation behaviours across different MOFs. For example, the Co(II) centre in MFU-4*l* is predicted to remain tetrahedral (unsolvated), whereas the Ni(II) centre should adopt an octahedral geometry (solvated). Moreover, we propose a methanol-assisted pathway for H2 dissociation and the formation of active species **E** (Fig. [5a](#page-6-0), Extended Data Fig. 2 and Supplementary Information, sections H.5–H.5) consistent with the hydrogenation catalysis observed in cobalt single-atom system[s64](#page-9-14).

The formation of open metal sites was investigated by exploring both homolytic (C1) and heterolytic (C2) M–X bond cleavage routes (Fig. [5a](#page-6-0) and Extended Data Fig. 2). Computational results show that the SH ligand facilitates homolytic cleavage, with the MFU-4*l* framework demonstrating a preference for the homolytic route over the heterolytic one (Δ*G*(C1–C2) = −4.0 kcal mol−1; Fig. [5b\)](#page-6-0). In contrast, Cl and OH ligands favour the heterolytic pathway, with Δ*G*(C1–C2) values of 30.7 and 15.1 kcal mol−1, respectively.

Following open metal site generation, H2 adsorption and subsequent methanol-mediated dissociation leads to the formation of active species **E**. For the Ni-MFU-4*l*-X series, the SH group enables the most facile generation of **E** (Δ*G*423K(**E**) = 53.0 kcal mol−1; Fig. [5b](#page-6-0)), whereas Cl and OH groups require higher energy with Δ*G*423K(**E**) values of 73.3 and 75.2 kcal mol−1, respectively. This reactivity trend across X groups persists for Co-MFU-4*l* systems, with SH remaining the most reactive ligand (Fig. [5c\(1\)](#page-6-0)). However, Co-MFU-4*l*-SH (Δ*G*423K(**E**) = 61.0 kcal mol−1) is predicted to exhibit lower reactivity than its nickel analogue (Δ*G*423K(**E**) = 53.0 kcal mol−1), consistent with experimental observations.

A similar mechanism was invoked in the 1D BBTA system (Extended Data Fig. 2), which also shows preference for the SH group over Cl or OH for both cobalt and nickel variants (Supplementary Fig. 54). Interestingly, within the BBTA framework, our calculations predict that the Co–SH catalyst generates species **E** more favourably than the Ni–SH analogue, with Δ*G*423K(**E**) values of 62.4 and 67.6 kcal mol−1, respectively. These results again corroborate the experimentally observed higher conversion rates for Co–SH compared with Ni–SH in the 1D BBTA framework.

To better understand the reactivity trends of various X ligands (X = Cl, OH, SH) bound to metal centres M (= Co, Ni), we performed energy decomposition analysis (EDA) based on absolutely localized molecular orbitals (ALMOs)[65](#page-9-15),[66.](#page-9-16) This approach enabled a detailed analysis of the contributions to MII–X bonding in MFU-4*l*-Ni–X systems, as detailed in Supplementary Information, section H.10 and Extended Data Table 1. The total electronic contribution (Δ*E*tot) for binding X**·** to a formal M(I) site showed increasing M–X interaction strength from SH to OH to Cl, with binding energies (in kcal mol−1) of −72.0 (SH), −83.2 (OH) and −94.4 (Cl). This trend was dominated by the charge-transfer term (Δ*E*CT), which quantifies energy lowering from orbital mixing between X**·** and M(I) fragments, showing increasingly favourable Δ*E*CT values (in kcal mol−1) of −59.9 (SH), −85.9 (OH) and −92.7 (Cl). These results indicate reduced electron density sharing between the metal centre and SH ligand compared with OH or Cl, resulting in a more labile MII–SH bond with enhanced propensity for generating open metal sites. Consequently, the ease of homolytic MII–X bond cleavage and open metal site generation decreases in the order: SH > OH > Cl.

We further explored the differential reactivity of CoII–SH and NiII–SH active sites in BBTA and MFU-4*l* frameworks by analysing bonding interactions between open metal sites (MI ; M = Co, Ni) and H2 molecules. Using ALMO-EDA analysis, we found that coordination geometry plays a decisive role in determining catalytic performance (Supplementary Information, section H.10 and Extended Data Table 2). In the pseudo-octahedral environment of BBTA, Co(I) (*d*<sup>8</sup> ) with two singly occupied *eg* orbitals experiences lower Pauli repulsion (13.5 kcal mol−1) than Ni(I) (*d*<sup>9</sup> ) with one doubly and one singly occupied *eg* orbital (51.8 kcal mol−1), resulting in stronger H2 adsorption at Co(I) centres (−5.8 kcal mol−1 versus −3.7 kcal mol−1 for Ni(I)). Conversely, in the pseudo-tetrahedral MFU-4*l* framework, this trend reverses due to inverted crystal field splitting, with Ni(I) showing lower Pauli repulsion (32.0 kcal mol−1 versus 39.4 kcal/mol) and stronger H2 binding (−20.9 kcal mol−1 versus −14.9 kcal mol−1) than Co(I). These findings rationalize the observed reactivity patterns: cobalt performs better in octahedral BBTA environments, whereas nickel demonstrates superior activity in tetrahedral MFU-4*l* frameworks.

#### **Conclusion**

We developed a stepwise post-synthetic strategy for synthesizing MOFs with highly polarizable SH groups by converting M–Cl species to M–OH and subsequently to M–SH (M = Co, Ni), overcoming challenges in the direct synthesis of metal–sulfur MOFs. This method was successfully applied to both 1D M2(SH)2BBTA and 3D M-MFU-4*l*-SH series, with SCXRD and PDF analyses confirming quantitative conversion. Catalytic studies on 4-nitrophenol hydrogenation demonstrated that incorporating sulfur-based ligands substantially enhances reactivity compared to chloride- and hydroxide-based analogues. DFT calculations further showed that the lability of the metal–sulfur bond plays a crucial role in the M–X bond cleavage pathway, enhancing hydrogen activation and offering valuable insights for future MOF-based catalyst design. This approach establishes a versatile method for integrating polarizable sulfur components into porous materials, broadening opportunities for high-performance catalyst development.

### **Methods**

#### **Materials and general methods**

All reagents were obtained from commercial suppliers and were utilized as received without additional purification.

The powder samples of Co2Cl2BBTA and Ni2Cl2BBTA were synthesized by methods reported in the literature[49](#page-9-5).

#### **Synthesis of the powder samples of M2Cl2BBTA**

First, 7 ml of a DMF solution of H2BBTA (1*H*,5*H*-benzo(1,2-d:4,5-d′) bistriazole, 30 mg, 0.19 mmol) and 7 ml of a methanol solution of MCl2·6H2O (M = Co, Ni; 100 mg, 0.42 mmol) were combined in an 8-dram vial. Concentrated HCl (12 M) was then added (0.1 ml for cobalt and 3 ml for nickel). The mixture was incubated at 65 °C for 72 h. The resulting powder was washed three times with DMF and three times with methanol. To remove any residual DMF within the cavity, Soxhlet extraction with methanol was conducted twice, each for 36 h. Activation was conducted under dynamic vacuum at 120 °C for 24 h before adsorption measurements.

#### **Synthesis of the powder samples of M2(OH)2BBTA**

M2Cl2BBTA (100 mg) is dispersed in 100 ml KOH (1 M) aqueous solution under nitrogen for 12 h. The product was washed with water and methanol three times and dried under a nitrogen flow. Activation was conducted under dynamic vacuum at 120 °C for 24 h before adsorption measurements. An alternative method[67](#page-11-0) for the preparation of Co2(OH)2BBTA was also tested using NH3 as a base to assess the potential formation of metal hydroxides in this system. No substantial differences were observed in subsequent experiments or catalytic results.

#### **Synthesis of the powder samples of Co2(SH)2BBTA**

Co2(OH)2BBTA (30 mg) was dispersed in 4 ml of methanol in a 4-dram vial containing a stir bar. The mixture was sealed and exposed to in situ-generated H2S, produced from 19 mg of NaHS and 0.5 ml of concentrated H3PO4, for 5 h. After exposure, the product was washed three times with methanol and dried under a flow of nitrogen. The sample was then activated at 100 °C for 24 h prior to conducting adsorption measurements.

#### **Synthesis of the powder samples of Ni2(SH)2BBTA**

Ni2(OH)2BBTA (30 mg) was dispersed in 4 ml of methanol in a 4-dram vial containing a stir bar. The mixture was sealed and exposed to in situ-generated H2S, produced from 19 mg of NaHS and 0.5 ml of concentrated H3PO4, for 12 h. After exposure, the product was washed three times with methanol and dried under a flow of nitrogen. The sample was then activated at 100 °C for 24 h prior to conducting adsorption measurements.

#### **Synthesis of the single-crystal samples of Co2Cl2BBTA**

A synthesis mixture was prepared by combining 4 ml of a solution of H2BBTA (20 mg, 0.125 mmol) in diethylformamide, 4 ml of a methanol solution of CoCl2·6H2O (60 mg, 0.252 mmol) and 0.2 ml of 38% HCl in a 6-dram vial. This vial was then placed in a preheated oven at 70 °C. After 3 days, needle-like pink single crystals had formed.

#### **Post-synthetic conversion of the single-crystal samples of Co2Cl2BBTA to Co2(OH)2BBTA**

A single-crystal sample of Co2Cl2BBTA (~5 mg) was treated with 4 ml KOH (0.1 M) for 4 h at room temperature and then washed with deionized water three times. The morphology of the crystals remains the same but their colour becomes darker.

#### **Post-synthetic conversion of the single-crystal samples of Co2(OH)2BBTA to Co2(SH)2BBTA**

A single-crystal sample of Co2(OH)2BBTA (~3 mg) was dispersed in 2 ml methanol and treated with in situ-generated H2S (generated by mixing 20 mg NaHS with 0.5 ml concentrated H3PO4) for 4 h at room temperature and then washed with methanol 3 times. The morphology of the crystals remains the same but their colour becomes darker.

#### **Synthesis of powder samples of M-MFU-4***l***-SH**

M-MFU-4*l*-OH (30 mg) was dispersed in 4 ml methanol. The mixture was sealed and exposed to in situ-generated H2S, produced from 50 mg of NaHS and 0.5 ml of concentrated H3PO4, for 12 h. Subsequently, the brown sample was washed with water three times. The resulting powder sample was dried under a nitrogen flow and activated under 120 °C before adsorption measurements.

#### **Post-synthetic conversion of the single-crystal samples of M-MFU-4***l***-OH to M-MFU-4***l***-SH**

Single-crystal samples of M-MFU-4*l*-OH (∼2 mg) were dispersed in 4 ml methanol. The mixture was sealed and exposed to in situ-generated H2S, produced from 50 mg of NaHS and 0.5 ml of concentrated H3PO4, for 12 h. The morphology of the crystals remained the same but their colour changed to dark brown.

SCXRD data of Co2Cl2BBTA, Co2(OH)2BBTA, Co2(SH)2BBTA, Co-MFU-4l-Cl, Co-MFU-4l-OH, Co-MFU-4l-SH, Ni-MFU-4l-Cl, Ni-MFU-4l-OH, and Ni-MFU-4l-SH were collected at 100 K, using a Rigaku Cu-Synergy/DW-Synergy diffractometer, equipped with a shutterless electronic-noise free Hybrid Photon Counting detector, a Cryostream 80-500 K cooler (Cryosystems), a Cu Kα (*λ* = 1.54184 Å) microfocus source with a beam size of ~110 μm and a four-circle kappa geometry goniometer. The single crystals were mounted on MicroMesh (MiTeGen) with paratone oil. ED data for Ni2Cl2BBTA, Ni2(OH)2BBTA and Ni2(SH)2BBTA were collected at 293 K under vacuum using MicroED, also known as the XtaLAB Synergy-ED system. The instrument is equipped with a Rigaku HyPix-ED direct electron detector and controlled by the Rigaku Oxford Diffraction CrysAlisPro software package. The XtaLAB Synergy-ED system utilizes a 200-kV electron gun, featuring a lanthanum hexaboride crystal as the electron emitter for optimized electron optics. The structures were determined by intrinsic phasing (SHELXT 2018/2) and refined by full-matrix least-squares refinement (SHELXL-2018/3) using the Olex2 software packages. The disordered non-coordinated solvents were removed using the solvent marks option in Olex2 software. The refinement results are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Crystallographic data crystal structures in CIF format have been deposited in the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (CCDC) under the deposition numbers shown in Supplementary Table 1.

PXRD measurements of MOFs were conducted at room temperature using a STOE-STADIMP powder diffractometer. This set-up included an asymmetric curved germanium monochromator utilizing Cu Kα1 radiation (*λ* = 1.54056 Å) and a 1D silicon strip detector (MYTHEN2 1K from DECTRIS). The copper X-ray tube, set in a line focus configuration, was operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. Samples of activated powder were placed between two Kapton foils and analysed in transmission mode within a rotating holder. Data collection ranged from 2*θ* = 1°–40° over 6-min intervals. Prior to data acquisition, instrument calibration was verified using a NIST silicon standard (640d). XPS measurements were performed at the Keck-II/NUANCE facility at Northwestern University using a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250 Xi, which uses Al Kα radiation (photon energy, 1,486.6 eV) and includes an electron flood gun. Data analysis was conducted using Thermo Scientific Avantage Data System software. All spectra were calibrated relative to the C 1*s* peak at 284.8 eV.

N2 adsorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2420. The BET surface area was calculated from the N2 sorption data within the relative pressure range of *P*/*P*0 = 0.05–0.2.

Elemental analysis was performed using a Varian Vista-MDX ICP-OES spectrometer equipped with a charge-coupled device detector and argon plasma operating over the 175–785 nm wavelength range. Approximately 5 mg of each preactivated sample was digested in 2 ml of concentrated HNO3 using a Biotage SPX microwave reactor (software version 2.3, build 6250) at 200 °C for 30 min. After digestion, the whole resulting solution was diluted to 40 ml with Millipore water prior to analysis. Hydrogenation catalysis were carried out in Parr reactors equipped with Teflon liners, which were precleaned using aqua regia. The reactors were initially purged three times with argon at 7 bar, followed by three purges with hydrogen at 13.7 bar. Post-reaction, the products and catalysts were separated using a centrifuge. The catalysts were then washed with appropriate solvents prior to reuse.

#### **PDF analysis**

In situ X-ray scattering data, suitable for XRD and PDF analysis, were collected at beamline 28-ID-1 of the National Synchrotron Light Source II at Brookhaven National Laboratory. Samples were loaded in either borosilicate (for dichlorination experiments) or polyimide capillaries (outer diameter, ~1.1 mm) and assembled within a flow-cell apparatus that either allows for a constant flow of a solution over the sampl[e58](#page-9-9) or in a gas-flow furnace that allows for heating in a desired gas environmen[t68](#page-11-1). X-ray scattering images were collected using high-energy X-rays (*λ* = 0.1653 Å) at sample-to-detector distances optimized for PDF and XRD analysis.

Data were collected for ex situ samples and for in situ dechlorination and sulfidation of a Co-BBTA sample. For the dechlorination experiment the sample was loaded in a borosilicate capillary and a 1 M KOH solution was directed over the sample while data were collected at the PDF distance for 10 s per scan. The dechlorination was also studied in an ex situ approach using a more dilute KOH solution (0.01 M) where the reaction was quenched at certain intervals to visualize the changes over a a longer time resolution. The in situ sulfidation using H2S as sulfiding agent was conducted using NaHS (10 mg) as a precursor, which was primed with diluted H2SO4 (100 ml); evolving H2S gas was directed over the sample using nitrogn as carrier gas, while data were collected at the PDF distance.

The sample-to-detector distance and instrument geometry were optimized within GSAS-II based on the scattering collected for a CeO2 standar[d69.](#page-11-2) Images were reduced to 1D intensity data as a function of scattering angle. The scattering contribution from the sample environment was subtracted and the data converted into PDFs, *G*(r), within xPDFsuite (*Q*max = 21.48 Å−1) [70.](#page-11-3)

#### **Computational methods**

**Periodic DFT calculations.** To perform DFT calculations on cluster models, the geometries of all studied MOFs were optimized using periodic boundary conditions with the PBESol-D3(0) density functiona[l71](#page-11-4) in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP, v.6.4.0)[72.](#page-12-0) Dispersion interactions were accounted for using Grimme's D3 method[73.](#page-12-1) Valence–core interactions were described by the projector-augmented wave method[74,](#page-12-2)[75.](#page-12-3) The valence electrons included in the calculations for each atom type were: C (*4*), H (*1*), N (*5*), O (*6*), S (*6*), Cl (*7*), Co (*9*), Ni (*10*), Zn (*12*). A 520-eV plane-wave energy cut-off was used, and geometries were relaxed until atomic forces were below 0.01 eV Å−1. Brillouin zone sampling was performed using a *Γ*-point only 1 × 1 × 1 *k*-mesh. These optimized structures were used as the basis for finite-sized cluster model calculations.

Cluster calculations were performed using DFT as implemented in Gaussian 16 (rev. B.01[\)76](#page-12-4). Geometry optimizations and frequency calculations were carried out at the TPSSh-D3(BJ)/basis-I level of theor[y77](#page-12-5)[,78,](#page-12-6) where basis-I denotes def2-SVP for non-metals and def2-TZVP with an Stuttgart-Dresden (SDD) effective core potential for cobalt, nickel and zinc[79,](#page-12-7)[80](#page-12-8). An 'ultrafine' grid was utilized for numerical integration. The natures of all stationary points were verified by calculating quasi-harmonic vibrational frequencies. Entropic terms were obtained using the rigid-rotor-harmonic-oscillator approximation, with the free-rotor approximation applied for vibrational modes below 100 cm−1 (ref. [81\)](#page-12-9).

**Thermodynamic corrections.** To account for the change of standardstate concentration from 1 atm pressure in the gas phase to 1 M in the solution phase, an entropy correction term of 1.89 kcal mol−1 was added to the absolute free energies of all computed species. For methanol solvent, the pure liquid (24.72 mol l−1) was used as the standard state. The correction to the Gibbs free energy due to the change in the standard state was calculated as follow[s84](#page-12-12)[,85:](#page-12-13)

$$
G^\circ=G^{\text{latm}}+\text{RT}\ln(C^\circ/C^{\text{latm}})
$$

Here, *G*° denotes the Gibbs free energy relative to the appropriate standard state in solution phase, *G*1atm refers to the ideal gas standard Gibbs free energy at 298.15 K under a standard state of 1 atm, *C*° represents the concentration in the standard reference state (specifically, 24.72 M for methanol), while *C*1atm is the concentration of an ideal gas at standard pressure (1 atm), given by *C*1atm = 1/*V*m = *P*/*RT*. The correction term for each methanol molecule in the equation above is equal to 2.7 kcal mol−1. To emulate experimental reaction conditions, the partial pressures of H2 and methanol (*p*H2 and *p*MeOH) were considered to be 19.34 and 8.78 atm at 423.15 K, respectively, and the *G* correction term was calculated accordingly[86](#page-12-14). These thermodynamic parameter calculations were facilitated by the use of the Shermo cod[e87](#page-12-15).

## **Section B. Synthetic Protocols and Characterization**

#### **Synthesis of powder samples of Zn-MFU-4***l***-Cl**

The preparation of Zn-MFU-4*l*-Cl was performed according to the previously reported procedure. 1 In a 100-dram scintillation vial H2BTDD (100 mg, 0.36 mmol), ZnCl<sup>2</sup> (1.0 g, 7.3 mmol) and DMF (60 mL) were heated for 18 h at 140 °C and cooled down to room temperature. The precipitate was collected by centrifuged and washed with 20 mL DMF for 3 times and followed by 20 mL MeOH for 3 times, and 20 mL CH2Cl2 for 3 times. Then the solid was dried for 24 h at 180 °C under vacuum. Yield brown microcrystalline powder.

#### **Transmetalation of Zn-MFU-4***l***-Cl to Co-MFU-4***l***-Cl**

The transmetallation of Zn-MFU-4*l*-Cl to Co-MFU-4*l* was performed according to the previously reported procedure. <sup>1</sup> Anhydrous cobalt (II) chloride (0.5 g, 2.1 mmol) was dissolved in DMF (16 mL) and MFU-4*l* (100 mg, 0.08 mmol) was added to the solution. The reaction mixture was stirred for 20 h at 140 °C in a 20-dram vail. The dark-green or dark-blue precipitate was centrifuged, washed with DMF, methanol and dichloromethane.

## **Transmetalation of Zn-MFU-4***l***-Cl to Ni-MFU-4***l***-Cl**

The transmetallation of Zn-MFU-4*l*-Cl to Ni-MFU-4*l* was performed according to the previously reported procedure. <sup>1</sup> Nickel(II) chloride (0.6 g, 2.6 mmol) was dissolved in N,Ndimethylacetamide (16 mL) and MFU-4*l* (100 mg, 0.08 mmol) was added to the solution. The reaction mixture was heated for 20 h at 60 °C in a 100-dram scintillation vial. N,Ndimethylacetamide was exchanged with fresh N,N-dimethylacetamide and then added Nickel(II) chloride (0.4 g) and heated for 20 h at 60 °C. The green precipitate was centrifuged, washed with N,N-dimethylacetamide, methanol and dichloromethane.

#### **Synthesis of powder samples of M-MFU-4***l***-OH**

**M-MFU-4***l***-Cl** (50 mg) was treated with 50 mL of a 1 M aqueous solution of 4-ethylmorpholine for 12 hours. Subsequently, the samples were washed with water three times. The resulted powder sample was dried under N<sup>2</sup> flow and activated under 120 ℃ before adsorption measurements.

#### **Post-synthetic conversion of the single-crystal samples of M-MFU-4***l***-Cl to M-MFU-4***l***-OH.**

Single crystal samples of **M-MFU-4***l***-Cl** (approximately 2 mg) were treated with 4 mL of a 1 M aqueous solution of 4-ethylmorpholine for 12 hours. Subsequently, the samples were washed with water and MeOH three times. The morphology of the crystals remained unchanged, resulting in a pale green color for M = Ni and pale pink for M = Co, respectively.

## **Section D. Sorption Properties**

The theoretical pore volume (PVcalc) of the MOFs were calculated by the equation shown below:

$$
PV_{calc} = \frac{n}{\rho}
$$

Where n is the void rate that calculated by Mercury software with probe size as 1.8 Å and grid spacing as 0.3 Å; ρ is the theoretical density of the MOFs calculated from cif files. The theoretical N<sup>2</sup> sorption amounts, *A*calc, were calculated with the equation as below:

$$
A_{calc} = \frac{PV_{calc} \cdot \rho_{N_2,l}}{M_{N_2}} \cdot V_{molar}
$$

Where 2, is the density of liquid N<sup>2</sup> at 77 K, <sup>2</sup> is the molar mass of N2 and is the molar volume of a gas.

## **Section G. Hydrogenation Catalysis of 4-Nitrophenol**

The experiment was conducted using a Parr reactor. Initially, 20 mg of 4-nitrophenol was dissolved in 6 mL of methanol (MeOH) within a 25 mL Teflon liner. The MOFs were added in an amount corresponding to 10% based on the metal sites. The reactor system was purged with argon at 10 bar pressure three times, followed by purging with hydrogen gas at 13.8 bar pressure three times. The final pressure of hydrogen gas was kept at 13.8 bar at room temperature. Subsequently, the mixture was heated to 150°C (No significant changes in the mass of the MOFs were observed at the reaction temperature, as confirmed by TGA analysis (Supplementary Figure 55).) for 4.25 hours and then allowed to cool to room temperature. The product was separated using a centrifuge, and the conversion rate was monitored via NMR spectroscopy. The turnover frequency measurements were formed with 1.5 mol% Co2(SH)2BBTA and 5 mol% Ni-MFU-4*l*-SH.

#### **H.10. Energy decomposition analysis (EDA)**

In the ALMO-EDA framework (ALMO = absolutely localized molecular orbitals; EDA = energy decomposition analysis), 4,5 the total electronic contribution (Δ*ETot*) for binding X(•) to a formal M(I) site, forming an M(II)–X site, comprises three fundamental components (Extended data Table 1). 6 The frozen term (Δ*EFrz*) represents the energy change when bringing two fragments together without altering their electron distributions. The polarization term (Δ*EPol*) accounts for the energy reduction when the electron distribution of each fragment adjusts to the other's presence, without electron sharing. Lastly, the charge transfer term (Δ*ECT*) quantifies the additional energy lowering from orbital mixing between the two fragments.

To probe the differential reactivity of CoII–SH and NiII–SH active sites in BBTA and MFU-4*l* frameworks we specifically probed the "frozen" energy (Δ*EFrz*) between the catalyst (species **C**) and approaching H2, decomposing this interaction energy term into three components (Extended data Table 2): 7,8 the electrostatic term Δ*EElec* for interactions between fragment charge distributions, Δ*EPauli* accounting for repulsion between overlapping filled orbitals, and the dispersion interaction term Δ*EDisp*.
