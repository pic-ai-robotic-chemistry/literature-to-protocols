ABSTRACT: Addressing the spatial organization of high-performance single-molecule magnets (SMMs) and achieving stimuli-responsive switching of their magnetic bistability are pivotal challenges in molecular memory technologies, paving the way for advanced optomagnetic devices. Herein, we utilize the photosensitive ligand 4,4′-bipyridine (BPy) as a linker to incorporate typical pentagonal-bipyramidal SMMs as nodes into a two-dimensional metal-organic framework (MOF), formulated as {[Dy1.5(OPh)2Cl(BPy)3(THF)1.5]- [(BPh4)1.5]·0.5THF}*<sup>n</sup>* (1). The precise synthesis facilitates axial coordination of PhO<sup>−</sup> and equatorial alignment of BPy, enforcing perpendicular orientations of the principal magnetic axes of Dy3+ ions across all Kagomé layers. Compound 1 exhibits photochromic behavior upon exposure to ultraviolet irradiation at room temperature, driven by a photoinduced electron transfer process that generates radicals. The resulting 1uv displays overall faster

Notably, the coercive field decreases dramatically from 4500 Oe for 1 to 1300 Oe for 1uv at 2 K, while the hysteresis loop opening temperature decreases from 20 K for 1 to 14 K for 1uv. These photoinduced changes are due to the formation of photogenerated radicals and alterations in crystal packing. This work achieves an MOF that integrate high-performance SMM behavior with magnetic coercive photomodulation, providing a design paradigm for engineering advanced SMM-MOFs with tailored photomagnetic switching.

# ■ **INTRODUCTION**

Single-molecule magnets (SMMs)�discrete molecules that relax magnetization slowly and exhibit magnetic hysteresis as well as quantum effect at low temperatures�are prime candidates for high-density information storage and quantum computing[.1](#page-7-0)<sup>−</sup>[8](#page-7-0) Recently, lanthanide SMMs have witnessed remarkable progress, including energy barriers over 1000 K, and magnetic hysteresis above liquid nitrogen temperature.[9](#page-7-0)<sup>−</sup>[16](#page-8-0) These advances are predominantly driven by two molecular platforms: sandwich-type complexes with conjugated aromatic ligands and pentagonal/hexagonal bipyramidal coordination systems, where strategic ligand field engineering enables strong axial magnetic anisotropy. To realize SMM-based memory applications, two critical issues require resolution: individual molecule spin-state detection and scalable integration strategies that preserve magnetic functionality in densely packed architectures. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) offer distinct advantages in this context, as their periodic structures can enable precise spatial organization of SMM building blocks, tunable inter-spin distances and maintained magnetic isolation, thereby facilitating the creation of dense, addressable spin arrays.[17,18](#page-8-0) The reported SMM-MOF strategies mainly involve either direct incorporation of SMMs at MOF nodes or encapsulation of SMMs within MOF channels.[18](#page-8-0)<sup>−</sup>[27](#page-8-0) Nevertheless, the development of MOFs incorporating SMMs with energy barriers approaching 1000 K remains unexplored, due to the difficulty in maintaining the chemical integrity of prefabricated SMMs during framework assembly. More critically, achieving a unified alignment of magnetic anisotropy across metal ions within an SMM-MOF presents a significant hurdle.

The precise control of magnetic states via external stimuli, such as electric potential, pressure, solvent, temperature, or light, broadens the scope of practical applications for SMMs.[27](#page-8-0)−[31](#page-8-0) In particular, photo-switchable SMMs enable the use of light as a highly effective stimulus, offering high spatial and temporal resolution. To date, only a small number of Ln-SMMs exhibiting photo-responsive magnetism have been reported[.32](#page-8-0)<sup>−</sup>[49](#page-9-0) Despite this progress, significant challenges remain, as these complexes face multiple substantial limitations: (1) Light-induced ligand reactions, such as ring-opening/closing, cis/trans isomerization, and cycloaddition, often occur far from the first coordination

Here, we present an effective strategy for constructing twodimensional MOFs (2D MOFs) that combine high-performance SMM behavior with an efficient photomagnetic response. By employing an axial strong-field phenoxide ligand and equatorial photosensitive 4,4′-bipyridine (BPy) ligand, we isolated three 2D MOFs {[RE1.5(OPh)2Cl(BPy)3(THF)1.5]- [(BPh4)1.5]·0.5THF}*<sup>n</sup>* (RE = Dy (1), Y (2) and Gd (3)). Compound 1 features a pentagonal-bipyramidal Dy-based SMM at its nodes, displaying an energy barrier for magnetization reversal of 1048(17)/822(46) K and a coercive field of 4500 Oe at 2 K. Interestingly, the magnetic principal axes of Dy3+ ions are well-aligned in a perpendicular orientation across all layers. Furthermore, this SMM-MOF shows photochromic behavior at room temperature under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, accompanied by a pronounced UV-light-actuated photomagnetic effect and photomodulation of magnetic coercivity.

The key challenge in the synthesis lies in the effective coordination of the PhPy ligand, as its presence is essential for the successful formation of 1−3. We attempted to grow single crystals of the intermediate after the addition of PhPy, ultimately succeeding by slightly modifying the reaction ratio. The resulting structure [Dy(OPh)2(PhPy)5](BPh4)·5Benzene (4) [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S3 and [Tables](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S2 and S3), combined with the previously reported structures [Dy(O*<sup>t</sup>* Bu)2(PhPy)4]<sup>+</sup> and [Dy(O*<sup>t</sup>* Bu)2(PhPy)5]<sup>+</sup> demonstrates that the intermediate is crucial for the subsequent substitution by BPy, likely due to a lowered activation energy.[51,52](#page-9-0)

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction reveals that 1 crystallizes in the monoclinic space group *C*2/*c* ([Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S4). The asymmetric unit consists of one and a half crystallographically independent Dy3+ ions, two PhO<sup>−</sup>, one Cl<sup>−</sup>, three BPy, along with THF molecules and BPh4 <sup>−</sup> counterions [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S4). Both Dy3+ ions (Dy1 and Dy2) adopt a similar pentagonal-bipyramidal configuration, with axial coordination by PhO<sup>−</sup>/Cl<sup>−</sup> ligands (albeit disordered) and equatorial coordination by four BPy ligands and one THF molecule [\(Figures](#page-1-0) 1a, b and [S5\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf). Thus, they can be fully described as [Dy(OPh)2(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> (1a) and [Dy(OPh)- Cl(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> (1b) species, with a ratio of 1:2 (vide infra). The bond lengths of Dy−O(PhO) range from 2.034(13) to 2.12(4) Å, while the Dy−Cl bond lengths span 2.499(14) to 2.533(16) Å, consistent with those reported for [Dy- (OPh)2(THF)5]+, [Dy(OPh)2(Py)5]<sup>+</sup> and [Dy(OPh)Cl- (THF)5]+ ([Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S5).[50](#page-9-0) In comparison, the equatorial Dy− O(THF) bond lengths (2.337(13)−2.393(15) Å) and Dy−N(BPy) bond lengths (2.501(10)−2.553(9) Å) are significantly longer. The axial O(PhO)−Dy−O(PhO) and O(PhO)−Dy−Cl angles range from 172.7(11)° to 178.3(3)°, while all adjacent equatorial bond angles fall within 70.1(4) to 74.7(4)°, closely approximating to the ideal values for a pentagonal-bipyramidal geometry. This geometry (*D*5h) can also be confirmed by the SHAPE values (1.010, 0.733 and 0.605) [\(Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S8).[53](#page-9-0)

Each Dy(III) ion coordinates to four BPy ligands, and each BPy ligand bridges two Dy(III) ions, forming a twisted Kagomé layer ([Figure](#page-1-0) 1c). Each triangle within the layer is equilateral, consisting of Dy3(BPy)3 units with internal angles of 60°. Meanwhile, each hexagon adopts an irregular, shield-like shape, composed of Dy6(BPy)6 units with internal angles of 158° and

82°. Such Archimedean tessellation is labeled as (3.6)<sup>2</sup> [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) [S6](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf)). The shortest Dy···Dy distance within the layer is about 12.1 Å.

The Kagomélayers stack in an AB mode, where triangular and hexagonal motifs exhibit a staggered conformation across neighboring layers [\(Figure](#page-1-0) 1d,e). The centroids of the hexagons in one layer align almost vertically above those of the hexagonsin the subjacent layer (offset = 2.3 Å). Consequently, this architecture preserves hexagonal channels. The charge-balancing BPh4 <sup>−</sup> anions are embedded between the Kagomélayers, with two-thirds located in triangular cavities of adjacent layers ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S7 and S8). The remaining third of anions occupy the large hexagonal channels but are highly disordered relative to the symmetry elements due to their weak interactions with the framework. Detailed analysis of interlayer forces at known locations shows that each benzene ring of BPh4 <sup>−</sup> and certain hydrogen atoms on BPy/PhO<sup>−</sup> generate C−H···*π* interactions, with distances of 2.4−2.9 Å (hydrogen atom to ring centroid) ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S9). The interlayer spacing is 11.0 Å.

The Y(III) analogue 2 and Gd(III) analogue 3 possess crystal cell parameters very similar to those of 1 [\(Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S10). However, 2 displays X-ray photochromism when exposed to Cu K*α* radiation for extended periods due to its weak diffraction [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) [S10\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf). Meanwhile, 3, which suffers from severe polycrystallinity, also undergoes a subtle color change upon prolonged exposure to Mo K*α* radiation. Importantly, their unit cell parameters remain unchanged after the color change. Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that the dried crystals exhibit a layered stacking structure, which explains the strong polycrystallinity ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S11).

**Photochromic Properties.** In an inert atmosphere, the yellowish powder of 1 undergoes a rapid color change to deep blue-violet (denoted as 1uv) upon 365 nm (6 W) ultraviolet irradiation ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S12). This deep blue-violet sample exhibits exceptional stability, maintaining its state for months when stored in a glove box in the dark at ambient temperature. Exposure to O2 quickly reverts the powder to the yellow form (denoted as 1O2), whereas visible light and high temperatures fail to trigger this oxidation process. 2 and 3 also display reversible photochromic behavior, with the corresponding states labeled 2uv, 2O2, 3uv and 3O2, respectively.

This photochromic transition is also observed in single crystals of 1, 2 and 3 ([Figures](#page-2-0) 2 and [S13](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf)). Taking 1 as an example, a remarkable rapid response to UV light in air is observed in single-crystal X-ray diffraction experiments. The light-yellow crystals of 1, coated with perfluoropolyether, quickly turn deep blue-violet within seconds under 365 nm UV light (6 W) in air. The crystals revert to yellow within seconds after UV irradiation ceases in air. The unit cell parameters of 1, 1uv, and 1O2 remain nearly unchanged ([Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) [S4](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf)). Similar behavior is observed for 2, 2uv, 2O2, 3, 3uv, and 3O2 [\(Table](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S11).

To explore the photochromic behavior, solid-state ultravioletvisible (UV−vis) spectra were measured. The pristine sample of 1 displays a strong absorption peak near 265 nm, primarily attributed to the *π*−*π*\* transition of the BPy ligand ([Figure](#page-2-0) 2a). Additionally, two weak peaks around 780 nm correspond to the f−f electronic transitions of Dy3+ ions.[54,55](#page-9-0) Upon irradiation with 365 nm UV light (6 W), two new electron absorption bands emerge at around 375 and 570 nm. These bands are assigned to the electronic transitions of the BPy anion radical, indicating the single-electron reduction of BPy.[56](#page-9-0)<sup>−</sup>[58](#page-9-0) The peak intensity increases with prolonged irradiation time, reaching saturation after 25 min. When 1uv exposed to O2, the UV−vis absorption peaks at around 375 and 570 nm nearly disappear within 30 min. However, 1uv does not fully revert to the original state of 1 ([Figure](#page-2-0) 2b). Crystallographic cell parameters, powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), and infrared (IR) spectroscopy confirm the structural integrity throughout the coloring−decoloring process (Table S4, [Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S14 and S15). The photochromic activation−deactivation of 2 and 3 are also validated by UV− vis spectra and PXRD ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S14, S16 and S17).

It is noteworthy that 1−3 also undergo slow discoloration under visible light, indicating broader photochromic activity. Exposure of 1 to ambient light for 3 daysresults in a color change to light blue, accompanied by a weak electronic absorption band at 570 nm ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S18). A similar discoloration and electronic absorption band are observed upon irradiation with 520 nm green light (7 W) ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S19).

Solid-state electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy performed in an argon atmosphere confirms the presence of radicals. A sharp, strong signal for 1uv is detected at a frequency of 9.86 GHz with *g* = 2.0000, while no such signal is observed for 1 and 1O2 [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S20). For the diamagnetic Y(III)-based 2, a very weak radical signal at a *g*-factor of 2.0040 is observed, resulting from a minimal quantity of light-induced contamination ([Figure](#page-2-0) 2c). After irradiation, this EPR signal for 2uv is significantly enhanced, suggesting the generation of radicals and electron transfer to the BPy ligand. Notably, the EPR signal of 2uv exposed to O2 reveals the presence of superoxide anion radicals[.59,60](#page-9-0) Following full decolorization, pump/fill cycles, which effectively exclude interference from superoxide anion radicals, render EPR signals for 2O2 nearly absent, demonstrating that the radicals are almost entirely quenched by O2. For 3, 3uv and 3O2, the EPR signals are nearly identical, with a *g*-value of 1.9800 [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S21). This is due to the pronounced signal from Gd(III), resulting from its half-filled f <sup>7</sup> electron configuration, which obscures the sharper radical signals.

The Raman spectra were further studied [\(Figure](#page-2-0) 2d). Before irradiation, the band at 1616 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> for 1 corresponds to the ring stretching vibration of BPy, while the band at 1298 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> represents the C−C stretching between the pyridine rings. Additionally, the peaks at 1000 and 1021 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> are attributed to the ring breathing modes of the pyridine and benzene rings.[57](#page-9-0),[61,62](#page-9-0) After irradiation, the vibrational band originally positioned at 1616 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> upshifts to 1628 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> for 1uv, accompanied by the emergence of a prominent band at 1517 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> , indicating charge transfer to BPy, which affects the stretching vibration of the pyridine ring. The 1353 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> band exhibits a 55 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> upshift relative to the 1298 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> band for 1, attributed to the formation of a quinone-like structure and the reduction of BPy aromaticity.[61](#page-9-0),[62](#page-9-0) Meanwhile, the band at 1000 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> is apparently diminished for 1uv, while a new band at 1035 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> emerges as a strong feature, consistent with observationsin reported viologen compounds and bipyridine radicals.[58](#page-9-0),[61,62](#page-9-0) After further oxidation, 1 and 1O2 exhibit nearly identical peak positions. However, the peak at 1021 cm<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> does not fully recover, suggesting partial structural reversibility, as corroborated by the UV−vis spectra. Similar Raman spectral changes are observed for the Y(III) and Gd(III) analogues [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S22 and [S23\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf).

**PET Mechanism.** Insights into the mechanism of photoinduced electron transfer (PET) are derived from density functional theory (DFT) calculations [\(Supporting](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) Informa[tion\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf). For computational modeling of 1, two extended crystal structures based on [Dy(OPh)2(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> and [Dy- (OPh)Cl(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> were selected as model systems (denoted as 1A and 1B, respectively). Inspection of the orbitals close to the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) indicates that the electron densities are predominantly localized on the BPy groups [\(Tables](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S12−S15). For the orbitals near the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), the densities are primarily concentrated on the PhO<sup>−</sup> and BPh4 <sup>−</sup> groups for 1A, and on the PhO<sup>−</sup>, BPh4 <sup>−</sup>, and Cl<sup>−</sup> groups for 1B. The calculated energy gaps between these HOMOs and LUMOs correspond to wavelength ranges of 141−618 nm for 1A, 146−515 nm for 1B, in excellent agreement with the experimentally observed photoresponse under UV−visible light irradiation. Among these transitions, those corresponding to the experimental wavelength of 365 nm mainly involve electron transfer from electron-rich PhO<sup>−</sup> and BPh4 <sup>−</sup> to electron-deficient BPy, as transitions from Cl<sup>−</sup> to BPy require higher energy. The potential electron transition pathways under 365 nm irradiation are depicted in Figures 3a and [S26](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf).

The single-crystal structures of 1, 1uv, and 1O2 were determined at 150 K using the same crystal ([Tables](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S4−S7). Although polycrystallinity and disorder in the crystal data complicate the analysis of minute bond changes on the picometer scale relevant to PET processes[,49](#page-9-0),[63](#page-9-0) a significant structural alteration is observed in a disordered phenyl ring of the BPh4 <sup>−</sup> anion. This particular phenyl ring approaches a neighboring PhO<sup>−</sup> ring, with the shortest C···C distance between the two rings decreasing from 3.491 Å in 1 to 2.294 Å in 1uv, and further to 1.896 Å in 1O2 (Figure 3b). Such variation offers strong evidence for enhanced intermolecular forces. Therefore, the PET process is suggested to proceed through the following mechanism: under 365 nm irradiation, photoinduced electron transfer from BPh4 <sup>−</sup> and PhO<sup>−</sup> to BPy ligands generates BPh4 • , PhO• radicals, and BPy•− radical anions. This irradiation leads to a reduction in the distance between one disordered phenyl ring of the BPh4 <sup>−</sup> anion and one neighboring PhO<sup>−</sup> ring, suggesting an interaction or potential pairing effect between BPh4 • and PhO• radicals. During the oxidation step, BPy•− transfers an electron to O2, reducing O2 to O2 •−. Meanwhile, the closer proximity of BPh4 • and PhO• radicals, combined with the disappearance of the EPR signal in 1O2, further suggests a localization or pairing effect of these radicals. Therefore, BPy can oscillate between two redox states, whereas BPh4 <sup>−</sup> cannot. With each of the five irradiation cycles, new electrons are supplied to sustain the continuous generation of photogenerated radicals. However, the gradual depletion of electron donors leads to a decline in radical generation efficiency, resulting in the observed fatigue behavior.

Therefore, the characteristic relaxation time (*τ*) at each temperature was extracted by globally fitting the frequencydependent *χ*′ and *χ*″ data to the generalized Debye model using the CC-FIT2 program ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S32−S35)[.64,65](#page-9-0) The obtained *α* value roughly increases as the temperature decreases ranging from 0.14 to 0.44 for 1 and 0.20 to 0.53 for 1uv ([Tables](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S16 and [S17\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf). The field-cooled (FC) and zero-field cooled (ZFC) magnetization curves measured at 1000 Oe reveal divergence for both 1 and 1uv, indicating a possible transition from the paramagnetic state to long-range ordering, a superparamagnetic state, or a spin glass state ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S37 and S38).[66](#page-9-0) Notably, 1 displays superior magnetic characteristics, with a higher irreversibility temperature (*T*IRREV = 9 K) compared to 1uv (*T*IRREV = 6 K). Furthermore, 1 shows a distinct ZFC peak maximum at 2000 Oe (corresponding to blocking temperature *T*<sup>b</sup> = 5 K), while this feature remains absent in 1uv, implying three plausible mechanisms in the latter: stronger quantum tunneling of magnetization (QTM), reduced blocking temperature, or a broader relaxation time distribution (as indicated by larger *α* values, Tables S16 and S17 and [Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S39 and S40).[5](#page-7-0)

Magnetic hysteresis measurements on polycrystalline 1 reveal hysteresis loops up to 20 K at a scan rate of 200 Oe s −1 , with a coercive field (*H*c) of 4500 Oe and remanent magnetization (*M*r) of ∼2.52 N*β* at 2 K (Figure 4d). In contrast, 1uv exhibits narrowed hysteresis loops with a *H*<sup>c</sup> of 1300 Oe at 2 K, decreasing to nearly zero at 14 K, at which *H*<sup>c</sup> for 1 remains at 1400 Oe ([Figure](#page-5-0) 4e). This also enables the coercive field to be photo-switched off within the 14−20 K temperature range ([Figure](#page-5-0) 4f). The reduction in coercive field for 1uv demonstrates irradiation-enhanced QTM, as confirmed by abrupt hysteresis collapse near zero field, low-temperature ac susceptibility tail, and the aforementioned FC-ZFC characteristics. In a recent study, Tong's group demonstrated optical control of the coercive field in a Dy-SMM.[46](#page-9-0) To our knowledge, these two studies are among the rare example of the practical realization of UV-light-actuated magnetic coercivity photomodulation in Ln-SMMs.

The magnetic properties of 1O2 were further explored. The *χ*M*T*−*T* curve shows a trend similar to 1 and 1uv, with *χ*M*T* values of 20.51 cm<sup>3</sup> K mol<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> at 300 K and 10.54 cm3 K mol<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> at 2 K ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S27). At 2 K and 70 kOe, the magnetization reaches 7.11 N*β* ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S41). The FC and ZFC magnetization curves diverge at 8 K at 1000 Oe [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S42), and 1O<sup>2</sup> exhibits double relaxation behavior analogous to 1 and 1uv ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S43). For 1O2, the coercive field and remanent magnetization at 2 K are approximately 1500 Oe and 2.57 N*β*, respectively [\(Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S44). This aligns with previous findings, as oxygen scavenges photogenerated free radicals, hindering the sample from returning to the original state.

To explore the generation of free radicals and their influence on magnetic exchange interactions, the dc magnetic susceptibility of diamagnetic Y(III)-based and isotropic Gd(III)-based compounds were investigated under identical conditions. For 2uv, the *χ*M*T* value at 300 K is 0.09 cm<sup>3</sup> K mol<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> , combined with a maximum magnetization of 0.12 N*β* at 2 K and 70 kOe, reflecting a relatively low radical yield of approximately 10% ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S45 and S46). Interestingly, this approximate value closely matches the number of phenyl ring from BPh4 <sup>−</sup> that approach PhO<sup>−</sup> during the structural transformation from 1 to 1uv. For 3 and 3uv, the nearly identical *χ*M*T*−*T* curves as well as *M*−*H* plots at various temperatures further support a low radical yield and slight change in exchange interactions after irradiation ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S47 and S48). Curie-Weiss analysis provides Weiss constants of *θ* = −2.19 K (3) and −2.29 K (3uv), corresponding to weak antiferromagnetic interactions (*J* = −0.10 K for 3 and −0.11 K for 3uv) [\(Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S49 and S50). Given the low radical yields in certain photochromic compounds and the extremely small Dy···Dy distance contraction (Δ*d* ≈ 0.005 Å for 1uv), it is expected that the magnetic interactions in 1 and 1uv differ only marginally ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S51).[67](#page-9-0)−[69](#page-9-0)

To elucidate the magnetic relaxation mechanism, we performed *ab initio* calculations using the CASSCF/RASSI-SO/SINGLE−ANISO approach implemented in the Open-Molcas program[.70](#page-9-0)<sup>−</sup>[72](#page-9-0) Despite the 2D nature of 1, the local coordination environment surrounding each Dy(III) ion plays a pivotal role in determining the crystal field splitting and energy barrier. Herein, the crystal structures [Dy- (OPh)2(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> (1a-Dy1) and [Dy(OPh)Cl- (BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> (denoted as 1b-Dy1 for Dy1, and 1b-Dy2 for Dy2) were selected as models ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S52−S54), retaining their complete coordination environments without optimization. For 1a-Dy1, 1b-Dy1 and 1b-Dy2, the ground-state doublet is predominately characterized by the *m*<sup>J</sup> = |±15/2> wave function, with the principal magnetic axis aligned along the pseudo-*C*<sup>5</sup> axis ([Tables](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S18−S23). The first excited state for all three models is almost pure *m*<sup>J</sup> = |±13/2> state, with the *gz* value closely aligned to the pseudo-*C*<sup>5</sup> axis. For 1b-Dy1 and 1b-Dy2, the second excited state exhibits a highly mixed wave function, with the largest component being 35 % |±3/2> for 1b-Dy1 and 43 % |±1/2> for 1b-Dy2, resulting in a *gz* value nearly perpendicular to the pseudo-*C*<sup>5</sup> axis. In contrast, this mixed state occurs only in the third excited state for 1a-Dy1, while the second excited state remains a pure m*<sup>J</sup>* = |±11/2> state. Therefore, the magnetic relaxation likely proceeds via the third excited state, with a predicted *U*eff of 1312 K for 1a-Dy1, and via the second excited state, with*U*eff of 760 K for 1b-Dy1 and 644 K for 1b-Dy2 ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S55−S57).

For 1uv, the computational models employed the same structural motifs: [Dy(OPh)2(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> (1c-Dy1) and [Dy(OPh)Cl(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> (denoted as 1d-Dy1 for Dy1 and 1d-Dy2 for Dy2), with two key approximations: exclusion of radical spin contribution and idealized charge state assignments ([Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S58−S60). Similar to 1, the relaxation of 1c-Dy1 occurs via the third excited state with *U*eff of 1325 K. The 1d-Dy1 and 1d-Dy2 proceed through the second excited state, with *U*eff of 857 and 652 K, respectively (Tables S24−S29 and [Figures](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S61− [S63\)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf). These results demonstrate that structurally analogous units in 1 and 1uv exhibit virtually identical magnetic relaxation pathways, along with similar energy level structures, consistent with their minimal structural variations.

The above experimental and computational results suggest several key points:

- (1) The unique architecture of this SMM-MOF facilitates an ordered arrangement of SMMs, with each SMM's principal magnetic easy axis oriented perpendicular to the layer plane. This perpendicular alignment is uniformly maintained across all stacked layers.
- (2) The Orbach relaxation at higher temperatures is contributed by [Dy(OPh)2(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> , while the process at lower temperatures is attributed to [Dy(OPh)- Cl(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> , consistent with reports for compounds possessing similar axial coordination environments.[50](#page-9-0) This observation reinforces the critical role of axial ligands in determining energy barriers of pentagonalbipyramidal SMMs. Similarly, while the reference complex [DyCl2(THF)5] <sup>+</sup> with the same axial ligands exhibits relaxation behavior below 10 K (*U*eff = 83 K),[73](#page-9-0) no corresponding feature is observed in 1. Quantitative single-crystal XRD refinement further limits any [DyCl2(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> content ≤ 8%. These results indicate that [DyCl2(BPy)4(THF)]<sup>+</sup> is either absent or present in undetectable trace amounts.
- (3) Compared to 1, 1uv displays accelerated relaxation dynamics throughout the entire temperature range, characterized by shorter relaxation times at identical temperatures within the 12−70 K range, a lower FC-ZFC divergence temperature (6 K for 1uv vs. 9 K for 1), reduced energy barriers (1000(9)/641(34) K for 1uv vs. 1048(17)/822(46) K for 1), a lower blocking hysteresis temperature (14 K for 1uv vs. 20 K for 1) and narrowed hysteresis loops (e.g., 1300 Oe for 1uv vs. 4500 Oe for 1 at 2 K).
- (4) For the changes in dynamics governed by the Orbach process, the coordination of photogenerated BPy•− and PhO• radicals to the Dy(III) center perturbs the primary coordination sphere (∼10% radical generation). A close inspection of the coordination environment reveals a reduction in axial linearity (O−Dy−O: −1.4°; O−Dy− Cl: −0.6-2.1°), and non-systematic variations in bond lengths ([Figure](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/suppl/10.1021/jacs.5c03704/suppl_file/ja5c03704_si_001.pdf) S64). Continuous Shape Measures (CShM) analysis reveals the Dy(III) center in 1uv

<span id="page-7-0"></span>For the significantly enhanced QTM at low temperatures under zero field after irradiation, several structural modifications are crucial. Although the radical-induced Dy···Dy distance contraction is minimal (0.005 Å), consistent with only slightly enhanced antiferromagnetic interactions in the Gd analogues, its impact at cryogenic temperatures could be considerable. Detailed analysis of the crystal packing reveals more pronounced intermolecular distance contraction, particularly as phenyl rings from BPh4 <sup>−</sup> approach PhO<sup>−</sup> during the structural transformation from 1 to 1uv. Closer packing likely enhances spin-phonon coupling and promotes QTM efficiency.[39](#page-8-0),[74,75](#page-9-0) Furthermore, 1uv exhibits increased average M−L bond length variation (0.0145 Å for 1 vs 0.0156 Å for 1uv), indicating enhanced ligand vibrations and reduced molecular rigidity. Such changes in local vibrational modes may facilitate stronger QTM effects, ultimately leading to the observed reduction in coercive fields.[76](#page-9-0)

# ■ **CONCLUSIONS**

We present an efficient approach to integrate pentagonalbipyramidal SMMs with high magnetic anisotropy into a 2D MOF using photoactive BPy linkers. The resulting SMM-MOF achieves a record energy barrier exceeding 1000 K and a coercive field of 4500 Oe at 2 K. It exhibits photochromic behavior at room temperature upon UV irradiation, accompanied by accelerated relaxation dynamics, as demonstrated by shorter relaxation times and narrowed hysteresis loops. These changes are likely due to photogenerated radicals that alter the crystal field, exchange interactions, and crystal packing. To our knowledge, this system represents a rare example of UV-lightactuated photomodulation of magnetic coercivity in Ln-SMMs, offering a new platform for the design of optically responsive magnetic materials with molecular-scale ordered arrays.

# **1. Materials and Instrumentation**

# **Materials**

All reactions were carried out under a dry and oxygen-free argon atmosphere by using Schlenk techniques or in a glovebox. All solvents were dried and degassed by standard technique. Anhydrous DyCl3, YCl<sup>3</sup> and GdCl<sup>3</sup> salts were prepared according to the literature procedure. 1

# **Instrumentation**

Elemental analyses (C, H and N) were performed on a Elementar vario EL cube elemental analyzer. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) measurements were recorded on a Bruker D8 ADVANCE diffractometer at room temperature. The solid-state UV−vis spectra were measured using polytetrafluoroethylene as a reference on a PerkinElmer Lambda950 spectrophotometer at room temperature. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) data were achieved in the X band (frequency of microwave, 9.86 GHz) at room temperature on a Bruker A300-9.5/12 EPR spectrometer with a 100 kHz magnetic field. The modulation amplitude was 2 Gauss, with a sweep time of 81.92 s and a conversion time of 80 ms. The Infrared (IR) spectra were recorded on a BRUKER ALPHA. Raman spectra were recorded on a Renishaw inVia Qontor Raman spectrometer using 785 nm laser sources at room temperature. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer using Al Kα as X-ray source. To compensate forsurface charging effects, all XPS spectra were referenced to the C 1*s* neutral carbon peak at 284.6 eV. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were obtained with a GeminiSEM 500. Direct-current (dc) magnetic susceptibilities were measured on a Quantum Design MPMS-squid VSM magnetometer. Diamagnetic corrections were made with Pascal's constants for all the constituent atoms and sample holder. Alternating-current (ac) magnetic susceptibility data were collected on the same instrument employing a 1.5 Oe oscillating field at frequencies up to 1000 Hz. Solid-state samples were used for all of the above measurements, and air-sensitive samples were sealed in argon/vacuum for measurement.

# **Density functional theory (DFT) calculations**

Two extended crystal structures, [(Dy6(OPh)12(BPy)18(THF)6)(BPh4)] 5+ (**1A**) and [(Dy6(OPh)6Cl6(BPy)18(THF)6)(BPh4)] 5+ (**1B**) were selected to analyze photochromic properties in the calculations. These models were derived from the crystal structures of **1pre** and **1**, based on the coordination environment of [Dy(OPh)2(BPy)4(THF)] + and [Dy(OPh)Cl(BPy)4(THF)] + , respectively. To facilitate the DFT calculations, and given the similar ionic radii and chemical properties of Y(III) and Dy(III), we used Y(III) in the calculations as a substitute for Dy(III). <sup>2</sup> Frontier molecular orbital analysis was performed at the Becke's three parameter hybrid method with the LYP correlation function (B3LYP) 3,4 level in gas phase with Grimme's D3 dispersion correction. <sup>5</sup> The def2-TZVP basis set for Y atom<sup>6</sup> and 6- 311G(d) basis set for other atoms <sup>7</sup> were utilized. The calculations were carried out using the GAUSSIAN 16. 8

# *Ab initio* **calculations**

*Ab initio* calculations with CASSCF/RASSI-SO/SINGLE\_ANISO type using the OpenMolcas program package have been performed on [Dy(PhO)2(BPy)4(THF)] + and [Dy(PhO)Cl(BPy)4(THF)] + , based on the crystal structures of **1** and **1uv** without any optimizations. The basis sets were chosen from the ANO-RCC library with the paramagnetic ion treated with VTZP quality, the coordinated O, N and Cl atoms with VDZP quality, and the other atoms with VDZ quality. <sup>9</sup> The state-averaged CASSCF orbitals of the sextets, quartets and doublets were optimized with 21, 224 and 490 states, respectively. 21 sextets, 128 quartets and 130 doublets were chosen to construct and diagonalize in spin-orbit (SO) coupling Hamiltonian with the RASSI module. These computed SO states were written into the SINGLE\_ANISO program to compute the g-tensors, crystal field parameters and magnetic energy levels for the doublets of the ground *J* = 15/2 multiplet of the <sup>6</sup>H term for Dy(III). The two electron integrals were Cholesky decomposed with a threshold of 1 × 10 −8 to account for the accuracy.

# **2. Synthesis**

# **Syntheses of complexes 1–4**

### Complex {[Dy1.5(OPh)2Cl(BPy)3(THF)1.5][(BPh4)1.5]·0.5THF}*<sup>n</sup>* (**1**)

Phenol (0.5 mmol, 47 mg) and NaH (0.5 mmol, 12 mg) were reacted in 12 mL THF for 30 min. Next, DyCl<sup>3</sup> (0.5 mmol, 134 mg) and sodium tetraphenylborate (NaBPh4) (0.5 mmol, 171 mg) were added to the above solution. After heating and reflux at 85 ℃ for 24 hours, the resultant suspension was filtered. 4-phenylpyridine (3 mmol, 466 mg) was then added to the filtrate. After reflux overnight, the mixture was filtered. 4,4'-bipyridine (1 mmol, 156 mg) was added to the resulting filtrate, and the reaction was carried out at reflux for 3 days. After filtration, slow diffusion of hexane into the filtrate at room temperature led to the formation of light-yellow crystals suitable for single crystal X-ray diffraction (ca. 40.2 % yield, based on Dy). Elemental analysis calcd (%) for **1** (C86H80B1.5N6ClDy1.5O4): C 66.34, H 5.18, N 5.40; found: C 66.31, H 5.39, N 5.55.

#### Complex {[Y1.5(OPh)2Cl(BPy)3(THF)1.5][(BPh4)1.5]·0.5THF}*<sup>n</sup>* (**2**)

The synthesis is the same as **1** with anhydrous DyCl<sup>3</sup> replaced by 0.5 mmol of anhydrous YCl3. Yield 50.1 %. Elemental analysis calcd (%) for **2** (C86H80B1.5N6ClY1.5O4): C 71.40, H 5.57, N 5.81; found: C 71.53, H 5.23, N 5.97.

### Complex {[Gd1.5(OPh)2Cl(BPy)3(THF)1.5][(BPh4)1.5]·0.5THF }*<sup>n</sup>* (**3**)

The synthesis is the same as **1** with anhydrous DyCl<sup>3</sup> replaced by 0.5 mmol of anhydrous GdCl3. Yield 46.6 %. Elemental analysis calcd (%) for **3** (C86H80B1.5N6ClGd1.5O4): C 66.68, H 5.20, N 5.43; found: C 66.94, H 5.15, N 5.92.

#### Complex [Dy(OPh)2(PhPy)5](BPh4)·5Benzene (**4**)

Phenol (1 mmol, 94 mg) and NaH (1 mmol, 24 mg) were reacted in 12 mL THF for 30 min. Next, DyCl<sup>3</sup> (0.5 mmol, 134 mg) and sodium tetraphenylborate (NaBPh4) (0.5 mmol, 171 mg) were added to the above solution. After heating and reflux at 85 ℃ for 24 hours, the resultant suspension was filtered. 4-phenylpyridine (3 mmol, 466 mg) was then added to the filtrate. After 24 hours of reflux, the solution was filtered and subsequently dried under vacuum. The resulting powder was dissolved in a small amount of benzene and allowed to stand for one week, yielding colorless block-shaped crystals.

#### **Cl – content determination**

The presence of chlorine (Cl) was detected by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Figure S1). Subsequently, the concentration of chloride ions (Cl – ) in samples **1**–**3** was quantitatively analyzed using a chloride ion-selective electrode (ISE) method. A series of standard solutions with different Cl – concentrations were prepared according to Table S1, and their corresponding potential values were measured. Based on the Nernst equation, a standard curve of potential (*E*) versus –lg*c*Cl – was fitted. Compounds **1**–**3** (20.0 mg each) were individually dissolved in 0.50 mL of DMF, mixed with 5.00 mL of 0.2 mol·L –1 KNO<sup>3</sup> solution, and then diluted to 50.00 mL to prepare the test solutions. The average potentials of compounds **1**–**3** were measured to be 187.0 mV, 186.0 mV, and 186.0 mV, respectively. After background subtraction, the standard curve was used to determine that the molecular formulas of compounds **1**, **2** and **3** contain 1.14, 1.15, and 1.19 Cl – ions, respectively.

# **3. X-ray Crystallography Data**

The single-crystal X-ray diffraction data of **1** were recorded on a Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer using MoKa radiation at 150 K. The structure was solved by SHELXT and refined by full-matrix least-squares method on *F* <sup>2</sup> with anisotropic thermal parameters for all nonhydrogen atoms. <sup>10</sup> Hydrogen atoms were located geometrically and refined isotropically. Some [BPh4] − anions and guest molecules in **1** were severely disordered and therefore removed by solvent mask in structural refinement. 1348 electrons were found in a volume of 8222 Å 3 in 1 void per unit cell. This is consistent with the presence of 4[BPh4] − and 16[THF] per unit cell which account for 1316 electrons per unit cell. The crystals are naturally polycrystalline (Figure S11), and despite numerous attempts to obtain a relatively better single crystal, the crystal data still exhibit severe disorder, necessitating the application of numerous constraints during structural refinement. Nevertheless, the structure shows a high degree of consistency with a higher-quality crystal, **1pre**, formulated as {[Dy1.5(OPh)3(BPy)3(THF)1.5][(BPh4)1.5]}*n*, which further supports the definition and analysis of the structure.

The single-crystal X-ray diffraction data for **1uv** and **1O<sup>2</sup>** were collected under identical conditions to those used for **1**. Specifically, after the diffraction data for the single crystal of **1** were collected at 150 K, the crystal was warmed to 300 K and irradiated with 365 nm UV light (6 W) under N<sup>2</sup> for 30 minutes. It was then cooled to 150 K under continuous irradiation, after which X-ray diffraction data for **1uv** were collected at 150 K. Subsequently, this crystal coated with perfluoropolyether was exposed to ambient air for 5~10 minutes and then cooled to 150 K, at which point the diffraction data for **1O<sup>2</sup>** were collected. Nearly identical constraints were applied during the refinement process. Similarly, some [BPh4] − anions and guest molecules in **1uv** and **1O<sup>2</sup>** were severely disordered and removed by solvent mask in structural refinement. In **1uv**, 1352 electrons were found in a volume of 8056 Å 3 in 1 void per unit cell. This is consistent with the presence of 4[BPh4] − and 16[THF] per unit cell which account for 1316 electrons per unit cell. In **1O2**, 2208 electrons were found in a volume of 8256 Å 3 in 1 void per unit cell. This is consistent with the presence of 4[BPh4] − and 16[THF] and 64[O2] per unit cell which account for 2212 electrons per unit cell.

Fast scans were performed on single crystals of **2**, **2uv**, and **2O<sup>2</sup>** utilizing CuKa radiation, while MoKa radiation was employed for **3**, **3uv** and **3O2**. The irradiation and oxidation procedures were conducted with reference to the methods described for **1**, **1uv**, and **1O2**.

The single-crystal X-ray diffraction data for **4** were collected utilizing MoKa radiation at 150 K.
