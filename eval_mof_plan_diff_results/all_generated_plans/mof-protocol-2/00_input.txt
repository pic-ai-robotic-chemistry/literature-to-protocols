## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post-processing Steps

The synthesis of MOF materials in this study was performed using three distinct methods: solvothermal, microwave-assisted, and mechanochemical. Each method follows an identical step sequence to ensure reproducibility and controlled crystal formation.

#### • Solvothermal Method  
1. Precursor Mixing/Dissolution  
 - Dissolve metal precursors (e.g., zinc nitrate, 0.1 M; ZrCl₄, 0.05 M) and organic linkers (e.g., terephthalic acid, 0.1 M) in DMF.  
 - Employ a Teflon-lined autoclave to ensure chemical resistance.  
2. Modulator or Structure-Directing Agent Addition  
 - Add acetic acid to control nucleation and crystal growth immediately after complete dissolution.  
3. Crystallization  
 - Heat the solution at 120°C for 24 h.  
4. Cooling and Collection  
 - Allow the reaction mixture to cool to room temperature; collect crystals by filtration.  
5. Washing and Solvent Exchange  
 - Wash the crystals sequentially with DMF and perform three ethanol cycles to remove unreacted species.  
6. Drying  
 - Dry at 80°C until complete solvent removal is achieved.  
7. Activation  
 - Activate under vacuum at 150°C for 12 h to remove residual guest molecules.  

#### • Microwave-Assisted Method  
1. Precursor Mixing/Dissolution  
 - Mix metal and linker in DMF; combine with modulator if applied.  
2. Modulator Addition  
 - Introduce acetic acid after initial mixing to enhance crystal orientation.  
3. Crystallization  
 - Subject to microwave irradiation at 150°C for 30 min.  
4. Cooling, Collection, Washing, and Drying  
 - Follow similar post-treatment steps as in the solvothermal method with washing in ethanol (three cycles) and drying at 60°C overnight.

#### • Mechanochemical Method  
1. Precursor Mixing  
 - Combine metal salts and organic linkers in a ball mill.  
2. Grinding (Crystallization)  
 - Mill at 30 Hz for 1 h.  
3. Post-treatment  
 - Wash with methanol in two cycles and dry at 50°C for 6 h.

A comparative analysis reveals that while the solvothermal method offers greater scalability and control over reaction conditions, the microwave-assisted approach provides faster crystallization. Conversely, mechanochemical synthesis is simpler and environmentally friendly though it may yield products with differing crystallinity.

## 5. Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

In the pre-use treatment of the MOF materials, a number of activation parameters were carefully defined and implemented to ensure an optimal balance between complete desolvation and maintenance of structural integrity.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
• Definition: The activation temperature is crucial for removing residual guest molecules and opening the MOF pores without compromising the framework.  
• Experimental Conditions: The protocol specifies a moderate thermal treatment within the range of approximately 120°C to 150°C. Although exact numbers vary slightly between samples, this temperature window was chosen to maximize pore opening while avoiding the risk of structural collapse.  
• Observations: It was noted that temperatures above this range tend to result in partial framework degradation, whereas too low a temperature may yield incomplete solvent removal.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
• Definition: A controlled low-pressure (vacuum) environment facilitates efficient solvent evacuation from the MOF pores.  
• Experimental Conditions: The activation process was performed under vacuum conditions, approximately at 10⁻³ bar.  
• Observations: Maintaining this low-pressure environment was found to significantly enhance the desolvation efficiency, ensuring that trapped guest molecules were thoroughly expelled.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas and Flow Rate  
• Definition: The use of an inert activation gas, such as nitrogen, aids in the continuous removal of residual solvents and prevents oxidative degradation.  
• Experimental Conditions: An inert gas (N₂) was employed with a controlled flow rate of around 100 mL/min during the activation stage.  
• Observations: The combined effect of a stable inert atmosphere and an optimized flow rate was observed to sustain framework integrity while promoting rapid and uniform solvent purge.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
• Definition: Sufficient activation duration is necessary to complete solvent removal without overexposing the MOF to thermal stress.  
• Experimental Conditions: Although detailed time frames are less frequently specified, protocols indicate that activation processes often extend for several hours—with 6 hours being commonly reported in related experiments—to achieve full pore opening.  
• Observations: Extended activation periods enhance porosity; however, caution is advised, as overly prolonged exposure may initiate partial decomposition.

Together, the controlled application of these parameters underpins a robust activation protocol that maximizes MOF performance in subsequent photocatalytic water splitting applications.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of the synthesized MOFs were evaluated using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). PXRD is essential for confirming the formation of the intended crystalline phase and assessing sample purity by comparing experimental patterns to simulated references.
- **Findings:** PXRD measurements, performed with a scan rate of 2°/min over a 2θ range of 5°–50°, revealed diffraction patterns that closely matched simulated patterns generated from single-crystal data. Calibration was conducted using a silicon standard to ensure accuracy.
- **Observations:** The absence of impurity peaks and the presence of sharp, well-defined reflections confirmed high crystallinity and phase purity across all MOF samples.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were determined by nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K, employing the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method for surface area and the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method for pore size and volume. These parameters are critical for evaluating the potential of MOFs in gas storage and catalysis.
- **Findings:** Prior to analysis, samples were degassed under vacuum at 120 °C for 12 hours. The BET surface area was measured at 1200–1500 m²/g, with a pore volume of 0.65 cm³/g and an average pore size of 2.1 nm, indicating high porosity and well-defined mesopores.
- **Observations:** The measured values are consistent with highly porous MOF structures. Higher synthesis temperatures correlated with increased surface area and pore volume.

#### 6.1.3 Thermal Stability
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was used to assess the thermal stability of the MOFs. TGA provides insight into the decomposition temperature and framework robustness under inert conditions.
- **Findings:** TGA was performed at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere, with sample masses of approximately 5 mg. The MOFs exhibited stability up to 350 °C, with significant weight loss and framework decomposition occurring above this threshold.
- **Observations:** The results confirm that the synthesized MOFs are thermally robust under the tested conditions.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to investigate the morphology and particle size distribution. SEM was conducted at 10 kV (5,000×–50,000×), and TEM at 200 kV.
- **Findings:** The MOF crystals displayed uniform morphology, with well-defined shapes such as octahedral or cubic forms. The average particle size was in the sub-micrometer range, with a narrow size distribution and minimal aggregation.
- **Observations:** Uniformity and high crystallinity were consistently observed, supporting controlled synthesis conditions.

#### 6.1.5 Chemical Composition and Bonding Environment
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR), and Raman spectroscopy were used to confirm elemental composition, oxidation states, and the integrity of organic linkers.
- **Findings:** XPS (using a monochromatic Al Kα source, 1486.6 eV) identified binding energies at 284.8 eV (C 1s), 531.2 eV (O 1s), and 399.8 eV (N 1s), consistent with the expected chemical structure. FTIR and Raman spectra showed characteristic peaks for carboxylate, aromatic, and metal–ligand bonds, confirming the presence and integrity of the linkers.
- **Observations:** The spectroscopic data verified the successful incorporation of metal centers and organic linkers without evidence of decomposition or unwanted side reactions.



### 6.2 Performance Results

#### 6.2.1 Gas Adsorption Capacity
- **Description:** Gas adsorption capacity quantifies the amount of gas a MOF can adsorb per unit mass under specified conditions, typically expressed in mmol/g. This metric is crucial for evaluating MOFs in gas storage and separation applications.
- **Experimental Data:** The synthesized MOFs demonstrated CO₂ uptake capacities of up to 3.5 mmol/g at 25 °C and 1 bar, as measured after equilibrium was reached within 1 hour. N₂ uptake under identical conditions was significantly lower, consistent with selective adsorption behavior.
- **Observations and Trends:** Mg-MOF-74 exhibited the highest CO₂ uptake, attributed to its open metal sites and high surface area. The results showed a direct correlation between BET surface area and adsorption capacity, with materials exceeding 1000 m²/g displaying superior uptake. Variations in modulator concentration and solvent composition during synthesis were found to enhance porosity and, consequently, gas uptake.

#### 6.2.2 Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity is defined as the preferential adsorption of one gas over another, calculated as the ratio of their adsorption capacities:  
  \[
  \text{Selectivity} = \frac{\text{Uptake of target gas}}{\text{Uptake of competing gas}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂/N₂ selectivity values reached up to 25, as determined by single-component adsorption isotherms and validated through breakthrough experiments and IAST calculations.
- **Observations and Trends:** High selectivity was observed in MOFs with functionalized linkers (e.g., UiO-66-NH₂), where amine groups enhanced CO₂ affinity. Selectivity remained stable over repeated cycles, indicating robust framework integrity.

#### 6.2.3 Catalytic Activity
- **Description:** Catalytic activity is assessed by the conversion yield and turnover frequency (TOF) in specific reactions, calculated as:  
  \[
  \text{Conversion (\%)} = \frac{\text{Amount of reactant converted}}{\text{Initial amount of reactant}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Conversion yields for representative catalytic reactions ranged from 85% to 95%, with reaction times of 2–6 hours at temperatures between 25 °C and 80 °C. TOF values were reported in line with high-efficiency benchmarks for MOF catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** Zr-based MOFs exhibited superior catalytic performance, attributed to their structural stability and accessible active sites. Catalytic activity was retained after multiple cycles, demonstrating reusability.

#### 6.2.4 Cycling Stability
- **Description:** Cycling stability measures the retention of performance metrics (e.g., adsorption capacity, catalytic activity) over repeated use. It is typically evaluated by monitoring the change in performance after multiple cycles.
- **Experimental Data:** After 10 adsorption–desorption cycles, CO₂ uptake decreased by less than 5%, indicating excellent stability. Catalytic conversion yields also showed minimal decline over repeated runs.
- **Observations and Trends:** The MOFs maintained structural integrity and performance, as confirmed by post-cycling PXRD and BET analyses. This stability is attributed to robust framework design and effective activation protocols.

#### 6.2.5 Sensor Response (if applicable)
- **Description:** Sensor response time is the duration required for a MOF-based sensor to reach 90% of its final signal upon exposure to an analyte.
- **Experimental Data:** Response times for gas sensing applications were typically in the range of a few seconds to several minutes, depending on the analyte and MOF structure.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fast response was linked to high porosity and optimized particle morphology, as observed via SEM/TEM.
