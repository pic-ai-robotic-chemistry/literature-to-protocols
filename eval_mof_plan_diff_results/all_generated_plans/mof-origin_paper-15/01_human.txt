Current research on metal−organic frameworks (MOFs) has concentrated predominantly on the properties of ordered crystalline phases.[1,2](#page-8-0) However, there is growing recognition of the importance of the physical properties of MOFs. In particular, the role of disorder, defects, and structural flexibility in installing beneficial physical behavior is now widely studied.[3](#page-8-0)−[8](#page-8-0) In fact, it has recently been demonstrated that crystallinity itself is not a prerequisite for many of the properties for which MOFs are well-known, such as porosity, catalytic activity, and chemical and structural tunability.[6](#page-8-0),[9](#page-8-0)−[12](#page-9-0) Consequently, noncrystalline or amorphous MOFs are attracting attention for applications in, e.g., ion conductivity

Conventional amorphization of MOFs is achieved through the application of high pressures, thermal treatment, or ballmilling of crystalline frameworks.[12](#page-9-0),[13](#page-9-0) Recently, the macroscale melting of several zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs, including ZIF-4 (cag), ZIF-62 (cag), and ZIF-76 (lta)) has been reported at ca. 650−900 K[.9](#page-8-0)[,14](#page-9-0)<sup>−</sup>[17](#page-9-0) The melting proceeds on a subnanosecond time frame via dissociation of the Zn−Im (Im = imidazolate, C3H3N2 <sup>−</sup>) bond, before reassociation of the Im with a different Zn center. The energy required to break the Zn−Im bond has been previously calculated as ca. 95 kJ/mol at 840 K for ZIF-4 [Zn(Im)2][.9](#page-8-0) The high-temperature liquid MOF exhibits a very high viscosity, which suppresses any recrystallization of the glasses formed by melt-quenching.[18](#page-9-0) The glasses formed upon cooling possess a continuous random network structure, like that of amorphous SiO2. [9,](#page-8-0)[13](#page-9-0)

Among the ZIF glasses known to date, ZIF-62 [Zn- (Im)x(bIm)2<sup>−</sup>x] (bIm = benzimidazolate, C7H5N2 <sup>−</sup>) is of particular interest because it has the lowest melting temperature.[16,18](#page-9-0) The glass, agZIF-62, exhibits reversible host−guest adsorption behavior, with gas uptakes of 20.1 (CO2, 273 K) and 9.3 (H2, 77 K) cm3 STP g<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> at 1 bar.[19](#page-9-0) The liquid MOF phase, however, is also of interest as it provides a platform from which materials with specific physical and mechanical properties can be designed and shaped.[20](#page-9-0) One example of such a material is the MOF crystal−glass composite formed by embedding the as-synthesized phase of MIL-53(Al) (containing unreacted ligand within the pores) within a ZIF-62 glass, which results in stabilization of the high-temperature, openpore phase of MIL-53(Al) at room temperature.[21](#page-9-0),[22](#page-9-0)

Currently, there are over 80 000 known MOF structures, although melting behavior has mainly been confined to the ZIF family.[13,23](#page-9-0) Various strategies have been proposed to construct new systems displaying accessible liquid states[24](#page-9-0) or, alternatively, to lower the melting point (Tm) of existing systems. Such efforts are highly valuable due to (i) the provision of a wider operational temperature window between melting and decomposition, which allows regulation of liquid viscosity, and (ii) the ability to combine a wider range of crystalline

Our understanding of the various factors that affect melting remains relatively poor, however, as does our knowledge of the structure, dynamics, and chemical reactivity of the liquid MOF state.

In one of the few studies performed in this area, highpressure, high-temperature diffraction demonstrated the negative melting-curve behavior of ZIF-62.[19](#page-9-0) The structure shows a reduction in melting temperature at high pressures (up to 2 GPa), although related materials with accessible dense phases do not show this behavior.[19,25](#page-9-0) The melting temperature of ZIF-62 can also be regulated via alternating the Im/ bIm ligand ratio in the structure.[16,18](#page-9-0)

Coudert and co-workers[26](#page-9-0) have also investigated the differences in thermal response between ZIF-4 and ZIF-8 [Zn(mIm)2] (mIm = 2-methylimidazolate, C4H5N2 <sup>−</sup>). The experimental T<sup>m</sup> of ZIF-4 is 863 K, while ZIF-8 decomposes before any possible melting event[.14,26](#page-9-0) Computationally, the T<sup>m</sup> values of ZIF-4 and ZIF-8 were found to be between 1200 and 1500 K, and >1750 K, respectively. This difference was ascribed to a greater energy barrier for diffusion in ZIF-8, which itself was proposed to be a result of the lack of stabilizing dispersive interactions for mIm linkers in the liquid state (due to the more porous structure of ZIF-8 compared to ZIF-4).

Here, motivated by the prospect of expanding the family of ZIF glasses, realizing new methods to regulate T<sup>m</sup> and T<sup>g</sup> (glass transition temperature), and developing a greater understanding of melting behavior and dynamics of the liquid ZIF state, we explore the synthesis of a series of novel glass-forming halogenated ZIF structures. The incorporation of halogenated ligands within MOFs has recently been shown to improve the stiffness of frameworks,[27](#page-9-0) while they are also capable of introducing permanent dipole moments and facilitating responses to electric fields[.28](#page-9-0) The electron-withdrawing nature of −F- and −Cl-functionalized ligands also provides an avenue to regulate the melting behavior of the ZIFs, yet this has not been probed. In this work, four novel mixed-ligand ZIFs are constructed using halogenated bIm-based ligands. A combined array of techniques including 19F solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and in situ terahertz/far-infrared (THz/far-IR) spectroscopy are used to study the effect of halogenation upon melting behavior, as well as the dynamic structure of the resultant liquids. The porosity and mechanical behavior of the novel ZIF glasses formed upon melt-quenching are characterized, demonstrating improvement over their nonhalogenated counterparts.

# ■ RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Crystalline Halogenated ZIF Structures. To produce ZIF glasses with accessible permanent porosity, we selected 5 chlorobenzimidazolate (5-ClbIm, C7H4N2Cl<sup>−</sup>), 5-chloro-2 methylbenzimidazolate (5-Cl-2-mbIm, C8H6N2Cl<sup>−</sup>), 5-fluorobenzimidazolate (5-FbIm, C7H4N2F<sup>−</sup>), and 6-chloro-5-fluorobenzimidazolate (6-Cl-5-FbIm, C7H3N2ClF<sup>−</sup>) linkers, alongside Im (Figure 1a). This followed from a previous report of the use of Im to promote melting and 5-ClbIm as a bulky linker to prevent the collapse of pore channels upon glass formation, allowing the diffusion of guest molecules in and out of the formed quenched glasses[.17](#page-9-0) These linkers also provided an array of halogenated functionalities, in order to study the effect on melting behavior.

Four isostructural frameworks based on the glass-forming ZIF-62 structure were synthesized via a solvothermal process, adapted from that of ZIF-62 (see [Supporting Informa](#page-7-0)[tion\)](#page-7-0).[17,21](#page-9-0) These materials are referred to as ZIF-UC-2 (6- Cl-5-FbIm), ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2mbIm), ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm), and ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm), respectively. UC indicates the university at which these structures were synthesized. The addition of aqueous sodium hydroxide solution, as reported by others[,29](#page-9-0) was found to be beneficial for the inclusion of bImbased linkers in ZIF-UC-3−5, due to facile deprotonation in solution[.29](#page-9-0) After the removal of occluded ligands from the internal pores, single-crystal X-ray diffraction was carried out on suitable crystals. All four frameworks are found to crystallize in the orthorhombic Pbca space group with the same 4- and 6 membered ring containing cag network topology as ZIF-62 ([Figures S1](#page-7-0)−S13 and [Tables S1](#page-7-0)−S8).[30](#page-9-0)

Like the reported ZIF-62 structure, the asymmetric units of ZIF-UC-2 to ZIF-UC-5 include two independent Zn atoms and four organic linkers (Figure 1b). The preference for structural inclusion of the halogenated ligands, described by their total occupancy factors, follows the order 5-FbIm (ZIF-UC-4) > 5-ClbIm (ZIF-UC-5) > 5-Cl-2-mbIm (ZIF-UC-3) > 6-Cl-5-FbIm (ZIF-UC-2) ([Table 1\)](#page-2-0). This tendency is consistent with the trend expected for their steric indexes, δ (the product of the van der Waals volume of the linker and a measure related to size),[31](#page-9-0) where bulkier halogenated ligands have lower probabilities of substituting the Im linker in the framework structure. This is consistent with our previous work.[32](#page-9-0)

Restrictions on ligand occupancy originate from either the symmetry operations of the specific crystallographic site or from the steric indexes of the halogenated linkers (see [Supporting Information\)](#page-7-0). This leads to a distribution of the halogenated ligands in the four structures, which results in different degrees of disorder. In the second case, the increased steric index of bIm-derived linkers relative to Im results in a shift of the halogenated benzimidazolate linker away from the original position to avoid clashing with a neighbor Im linker in a 100% occupied position. This shift results in partial occupancy of the crystallographic sites normally occupied by solvent molecules[,32](#page-9-0) reducing the occupancy of the halogenated linker due to the competition with solvent molecules.

Therefore, systems in which the total occupancy factor of the halogenated linker is <50% contain just one disordered imidazolate position and only two different configurations for the asymmetric unit [\(Figure 1c](#page-1-0) and [Figures S1](#page-7-0)−S13), i.e., ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm) and ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm) (Table 1). Structures with higher occupancies on the other hand, possess two disordered linker positions. This is the case for ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) and ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm). This results in four possible configurations for the asymmetric unit, creating a higher degree of structural disorder in the system.

The bulk-phase purity of the samples was further confirmed by Pawley refinement of powder X-ray diffraction data (PXRD), using lattice parameters from the determined single-crystal structures. Good agreement between the two confirms the formation of phase-pure samples of halogenated ZIFs ([Figure S14](#page-7-0)). <sup>1</sup> H solution NMR spectroscopy ([Figures](#page-7-0) [S15](#page-7-0)−S18) was carried out by digesting samples of crystalline ZIF-UC-2 to ZIF-UC-5 in a mixture of DCl/D2O (35%; 0.1 mL) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) (0.5 mL). The NCHN chemical shifts for Im (∼9 ppm region, NCHNIm), the substituted bIm linkers (∼9.5 ppm for NCHN5‑ClbIm, NCHN5‑FbIm, and NCHN6‑Cl‑5‑FbIm and ∼2.75 ppm for NCCH3N5‑Cl‑2‑mbIm) are well-resolved. In a separate experiment, to avoid excessive thermal decomposition, crystalline samples were heated above their corresponding melting onset temperatures (693 K for ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm), 683 K for ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm), 708 K for ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm), and 723 K for ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm)) and then cooled back to room temperature. The glasses formed are termed agZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm), agZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm), agZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm), and agZIF-UC-5 (5- ClbIm), with terminology in accordance with previous publications,[32,33](#page-9-0) and were used for subsequent ex situ studies. This was followed by a second DSC heating scan, at 10 K min<sup>−</sup><sup>1</sup> , which identifies T<sup>g</sup> values in each case [\(Figure 2c](#page-2-0) and [Table 2](#page-2-0)). All melt-quenched glass samples display only diffuse scattering in their PXRD patterns, while optical- and scanning electron microscopy confirm the formation of coherent, continuous, and glassy morphologies upon melt-quenching ([Figures 2d](#page-2-0), [S19, and S20](#page-7-0)). The Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra for the crystals and the corresponding melt-quenched glasses show similar features ([Figure S21](#page-7-0)). Because of the relatively close nature of the melting and thermal decomposition temperatures, the melting process was still accompanied by a small quantity of thermal decomposition for two of the crystalline samples. Specifically, <sup>1</sup> H NMR spectroscopy carried out on the glasses ([Table 2](#page-2-0)) confirms a partial loss of the bIm-derived ligand component in agZIF-UC-2 [Zn(Im)1.90(6-Cl-5-FbIm)0.10] and agZIF-UC-5 [Zn(Im)1.69(5-ClbIm)0.31]. However, agZIF-UC-3 [Zn- (Im)1.74(5-Cl-2-mbIm)0.26] and agZIF-UC-4 [Zn(Im)1.63(5- FbIm)0.37] are relatively unchanged compared with their crystalline precursors [\(Figures S22](#page-7-0)−S25).

The glass transition indicates the transition from solid to liquid-like behavior upon heating. Glasses with similar organic ligand contents possess transition temperatures which correlate with the van der Waals radius of the added side-functional group(s), i.e., agZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) (563 K and 1.47 Å) and agZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) (593 K and 1.75 Å). This has also been found previously and is related to larger functional groups increasing the extent of noncovalent interactions within glass or liquid structures[.14](#page-9-0),[35](#page-9-0) A lower than expected T<sup>g</sup> for agZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm) was observed at 523 K, which was ascribed to the low bIm-derived linker content in the glass phase.

Trends in melting point are less clear. The onsets of melting of the structures in this study, including ZIF-62 itself, are separated by 50 K. The 19 K difference between ZIF-UC-4 (5- FbIm) (T<sup>m</sup> = 694 K) and ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) (T<sup>m</sup> = 705 K) is consistent with the greater inductive withdrawal effect of −F compared with −Cl. This experimental observation was further confirmed by density functional theory (DFT) calculations ([Figures S26 and S27\)](#page-7-0), which demonstrates an average difference of 2.88 kJ/mol in Zn−N bond energy between Zn(Im)3(5-FbIm) and Zn(Im)3(5-ClbIm) tetrahedra.

ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm) possesses the lowest T<sup>m</sup> observed (663 K), i.e., lower than that of ZIF-62 (T<sup>m</sup> = 673 K).[14,17](#page-9-0) The activation free energy, ΔF‡ , for melting has previously been demonstrated to follow the van't Hoff law, ΔF‡ (T) = ΔU‡ − TΔS‡ , where ΔU‡ and ΔS‡ are activation energy and entropy, respectively.[9](#page-8-0) From a thermodynamic point of view, the melting occurs through an activation process, leading to bond breakage at the point of the metastable phase of the ZIF crystal precursor rather than at the limit of stability of the liquid phase.[36](#page-9-0) The ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm) structure contains the most sterically large ligand, and thus the crystal structure formed is the most dense out of the four materials studied here. For dense ZIFs such as this one, it follows that greater dispersive interactions between the framework and the partially dissociated ligand will reduce the energy barrier to melting. For highly porous frameworks on the other hand, a low level of dispersive interactions between framework and detached ligands generates a high activation free energy barrier for melting.[26](#page-9-0)

The 13C MAS NMR spectrum of agZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) exhibits broader signals at roughly the same isotropic chemical shifts as the spectrum of ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm). Broadening of the signals is an expected effect of increased structural disorder and the loss of crystallinity. The 19F MAS NMR spectrum of agZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm), however, is more informative. We therefore ascribe the remarkable change in the chemical shift to a considerable change in the nature of the immediate bonding environment of F upon glass formation. 19F−13C CPMAS NMR measurements confirm this [\(Figure S37](#page-7-0)). Selective cross-polarization from 19F nuclei, contributing to the narrow 19F signal of the glass, clearly enhances the signal of C5 C nuclei, i.e., of the nuclei belonging to C atoms within the benzyl ring, which are directly bonded to F. On the contrary, selective cross-polarization from 19F nuclei, contributing to the broad 19F signal of the glass, gives rise to a C spectrum in which only the two signals of Im can be detected. Surprisingly, this observation suggests that F that contributes to the broad, shifted signal is in fact not directly attached to a bIm ligand.

To further verify this observation, the 19F NMR spectrum of a digested sample of agZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) was measured (Figure 4b). The spectrum shows that only a fraction of F nuclei resonate at −114 ppm, i.e., at the chemical shift typical for F of the dissolved 5-FbIm. The spectrum also contains a weak signal at −139.7 ppm and a far stronger signal at −166.3 ppm, which can be consistent with F atoms attached to heavy atoms. On the basis of these observations, we suggest the formation of Zn−F bonds during melting and vitrification. This would explain the observed shift of about −200 ppm in the solid-state 19F MAS NMR spectrum of agZIF-UC-4 (5- FbIm), which agrees well with the 19F shift in ZnF2. [38](#page-9-0) Other sources of the shifted signal were investigated and ruled out, including the presence of paramagnetic species (through 19F relaxation measurements) and the presence of halogen bonds (through DFT-based calculations of the expected 19F chemical shifts) [\(Figures S38 and S39](#page-7-0)). Very similar observations are found also for agZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm) ([Figure S40](#page-7-0)). We suspect that the mechanism of dissociation may be similar to thermally activated radical processes in, e.g., halogenated benzene compounds (e.g., chlorobenzene).[39](#page-9-0) We might also suggest that the fate of the molecule after defluorination is to perhaps form a benzyne intermediate, allowing further reaction with imidazole to form heterocyclic structures or, alternatively, to form N-alkyl-N′-arylimidazolium salts. This, however, is far beyond the scope of this work and investigations would be necessary.[40](#page-9-0)−[42](#page-9-0)

![](_page_6_Figure_4.jpeg)

The decrease in melting temperature with greater values of X<sup>R</sup> is consistent with the computationally calculated melting mechanism for ZIFs. For example, melting has been theorized to proceed via metal−linker bond breaking at a critical temperature, where the ratio of the thermally induced vibrational amplitude of metal-linked atoms (i.e., N) and the M−N bond distance reach a critical point, i.e., Lindemann's law[.9](#page-8-0) Since X<sup>R</sup> describes the increasing deformation of the ZnN4 tetrahedra, it must also be correlated to both bond distance and the thermal vibration of the ligand. We also observe that the melting tendency has no clear correlation with the Zn−N bond strength at room temperature ([Figure S68\)](#page-7-0).

Porosity. ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5FbIm), ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2 mbIm), ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm), and ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) all display a low N2 uptake at 77 K [\(Figure S69](#page-7-0)) with relatively low level of Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) surface areas, in the range of ca. 7.10−86.73 m<sup>2</sup> /g.[50](#page-10-0) These values are consistent with the data reported in the literature, where the relatively dense structure of ZIF-62 blocks the entry and exit of N2 gas molecules into its inner framework structure.[51](#page-10-0) On the other hand, smaller gas molecules like CO2 can diffuse into the crystalline framework, and the absence of adsorption/ desorption hysteresis indicates the rapid adsorptive/desorptive kinetics for these crystals (measured at 273 K, [Figure S70](#page-7-0)). Our earlier work demonstrates that a reduction in porosity, and thus gas adsorption capacity, is to be expected after vitrification of a crystalline ZIF[.19](#page-9-0) A substantial degree of microporosity can be preserved, however, because of the presence of bulky bIm-based ligands, which prevents the total collapse of the porous interior upon liquid formation.[11,17](#page-9-0) CO2 adsorption isotherms at 273 K for the melt-quenched glass samples confirm this is also the case here (Figure 8a): all meltquenched ZIF glasses show reversible adsorption of CO2, suggesting the relatively rapid diffusion of guest molecules within these microporous structures.

The incorporation of fluorinated ligands can improve the CO2 uptake for both crystalline and glass materials. For example, despite ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) and ZIF-UC-5 (5- ClbIm) exhibiting comparable quantities of bulky bIm-based ligands ([Table 2](#page-2-0)), ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) exhibits a markedly higher CO2 uptake that is rationalized in terms of the stronger interaction between CO2 guests and framework F atoms in the narrow channels of the MOF.[52](#page-10-0) Similar behavior of other Ffunctionalized MOFs has been reported by other researchers[.53](#page-10-0)<sup>−</sup>[55](#page-10-0) After melting, agZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) displays a CO2 uptake of 42 cm<sup>3</sup> (STP)/cm<sup>3</sup> at 1 bar, which is ca. 15% higher than that for agZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) and ca. 30% higher than that for agZIF-62.[19](#page-9-0)

Mechanical Properties. Nanoindentation has been previously applied to investigate the mechanical properties including Young's moduli (E) and hardness of crystalline and amorphous MOFs.[14,21](#page-9-0) The average Young's modulus was recorded from load-displacement data (200−1000 nm) on polished glass samples (Figures S71−S74). Both agZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) and agZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) show comparable values of E ≈ 9.4 and 9.3 GPa, respectively, placing them roughly at the upper bound of the existing ZIF glasses [\(Figures 8b](#page-6-0) and S75). The mechanical properties of MOF materials do not always follow density−stiffness rules but are determined more by the primary (metal−organic coordination structure) and secondary (nonbonded interactions) networks, which provide extra support for the structural weak points.[56](#page-10-0) Consistent with these reports, we postulate that the presence of halogenated moieties on the bIm-derived ligands can introduce more effective secondary network structures via nonbonded interactions, or indeed by the Zn−F bonds, which raise E in these cases above that found for agZIF-62 (E ≈ 6.1−8.8 GPa, [Figure 8](#page-6-0)b). Attempts have also been made to prepare bulk halogenated glass samples by first pelletizing samples of agZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm), before annealing above the glass Tg. [57](#page-10-0) The resultant bulk glass (termed as agrZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2 mbIm)) has a dark brown, opaque appearance, with a diameter of 13 mm (Figure S76) and a Young's modulus of 6.66 ± 0.14 GPa.

# ■ CONCLUSION

Four new crystalline ZIFs, ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm), ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm), ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm), and ZIF-UC-5 (5- ClbIm) have been reported and have all been shown to undergo melting. The melting of ZIFs has previously been reported to be affected by the crystal structure of the precursor, the stoichiometric ratio of different ligands, and the type of metal node.[16,18](#page-9-0) The array of experimental techniques provided here show that the presence of electron-withdrawing moieties on the benzimidazolate ligand may weaken the Zn−N bond and also lead to a small reduction in the melting temperature of the ZIF crystal. The results also show that the presence of bulkier ligands results in a lower free activation energy through dispersive interaction-based stabilization of dissociated ligands in the liquid phase. These findings are encapsulated by in situ THz/far-IR studies that reveal the connection between Zn−N bond motion and melting behavior. Specifically, they provide evidence of a correlation between the ZIF melting temperature and the propensity of thermal-induced Zn−N polyhedral deformation, which provides experimental evidence of a melting mechanism being underpinned by the thermally induced vibrational amplitude of the N atoms reaching a critical point, which leads to the breaking of the Zn−N bond.

In addition, solid-state NMR results provide specific evidence for the formation of Zn−F bonding in the glass phase, implying a reduction in the overall network connectivity. The extent of this rearrangement process is dependent upon treatment temperature, although the fate of the proportion of fluorobenzimidazolate ligands from which F detaches is beyond the scope of this work. The presence of a fluorinated moiety within the glass of ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) also notably improves gas uptake for CO2, compared with ZIF-62 and ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) containing chlorinated benzimidazole ligands.

The results help explain the glass-forming ability (GFA) of ZIF materials, which were analyzed using their respective Tg/ T<sup>m</sup> ratios ([Table 2](#page-2-0)). This ratio for conventional metallic, covalent, and ionic melt-quenched glasses follows the empirical Kauzmann "2/3 Law" [34](#page-9-0) and has been used previously to suggest the resistance to recrystallization of the supercooled liquid state.[18](#page-9-0) The highest GFA (0.92) for the samples studied here is observed for ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm). This is ascribed to the presence of the sterically bulky 5-Cl-2-mbIm ligand, which suppresses the ones for diffusion in the liquid phase. This is higher than the reported GFA for ZIF-62 (0.88). At the same time, slightly lower GFAs for the ZIF-UC-2 (0.77) and ZIF-UC-4 (0.81) are attributed to the formation of Zn−F bonds, which breaks the continuity of the random network and improves the diffusivity of the ligands in ZIF liquid, thus lowering Tg. The GFAs for the halogenated ZIFs are still higher than ionic, metallic, and covalent glasses.

Importantly, together with recent progress on lowering the melting temperature of ZIFs through (i) adjustment of organic linker ratio and (ii) inclusion of multiple organic linkers and (iii) cobalt, these results may be used to further lower the melting temperature of ZIFs. Such research is essential if industrially viable working temperatures are to be achieved and larger-scale studies on the applications of such glasses are to be performed.

## **Experimental**

#### **Synthesis**

ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm): Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (220 mg, 0.74 mmol) and imidazole (1188 mg, 16.42 mmol) were dissolved in 10 mL *N,N*-dimethylformamide (DMF) within a screw top vial. After being stirred for 1 hour, 6-chloro-5-fluorobenzimidazole (297.4 mg, 1.74 mmol) was added to the mixture. The solution was heated at 403 K for 48 hours and then cooled at 5 K hour-1 .

ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm): Imidazole (60 mg, 0.89 mmol), 5-chloro-2-methylbenzimidazole (293 mg, 1.76 mmol) and zinc nitrate hexahydrate (262 mg, 0.88 mmol) were fully dissolved in a solution of 3 mL DMF and 4 mL *N,N*-diethylformamide (DEF), to which 0.25 mL of 2.5 mol dm-3 NaOH was added. The mixture was stirred for 1 hour and subsequently heated to 383 K for 5 days. The crystalline product was collected by filtration with dichloromethane.

ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) and ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm): Imidazole (120 mg, 1.77 mmol) and 5 fluorobenzimidazole (112 mg, 0.82 mmol) or 5-chlorobenzimidazole (130 mg, 0.85 mmol) were fully dissolved in a solution of 8.3 mL DMF and 5.7 mL DEF. Then zinc nitrate hexahydrate (120 mg, 0.40 mmol) was added to the solution and stirred for 1 hour. Subsequently, NaOH (0.52 mL, 2.5 mol dm-3 ) (10 wt%) was added to the mixture, and the turbid solution was then heated to 363 K for 5 days. To obtain a pure phase of product, the cooling rate was set at 5 K hour-1 .

Prior to subsequent experiments, all ZIF crystals were centrifuged and wished with dichloromethane for three times, and then placed in a vacuum oven at 450 K overnight for activation.

#### **Single crystal X-ray measurement**

Single crystal X-ray diffraction data were collected at 298 K on a Gemini E Ultra diffractometer from Oxford Diffraction with an area detector Atlas CCD. Data collection was performed using Cu Kα (λ = 1.540598 Å) radiation. The structure was solved and refined using the program WinGX version 2018.2.<sup>1</sup> More details on data collection and structural refinement are presented in the results section.

#### **Powder X-ray diffraction**

Powder X-ray diffraction was carried out under ambient conditions with a Bruker-AXS D8 diffractometer and a Cu K<sup>α</sup> (λ = 1.540598 Å) radiation source. The detector was a LynxEye position-sensitive detector in Bragg-Brentano parafocusing geometry. The 2θ range was 5° to 40°, with a step size of 0.02° and a 10s step rate. Pawley refinement was performed with the program jEdit 4.3 and TOPAS academic V5.<sup>2</sup> The unit cell parameters obtained by single crystal X-ray diffraction were used in the Pawley refinement.<sup>3</sup>

#### **Liquid-state nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy**

Liquid-state <sup>1</sup>H and <sup>19</sup>F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements were conducted with *ca.* 6 mg of sample, digested in a mixture of DCl/D2O (35%; 0.1 mL) and DMSO-d<sup>6</sup> (0.5 mL). The <sup>1</sup>H NMR measurement was performed with a Bruker Avance III 400 MHz spectrometer at ambient conditions. Chemical shifts were referenced to the residual protiosolvent signals of DMSO-d6. The <sup>19</sup>F spectra were recorded using Varian Inova 300 MHz spectrometer and referenced relative to external CFCl3.

#### **Thermogravimetric and calorimetric analysis**

A TA Instruments SDT Q600 was used for thermogravimetric (TG) analysis. Samples (*ca.* 10 mg) were held in an alumina pan under a continuous flow of argon gas at a rate of 100 ml min-1 . For the melting experiments, the heating rate was controlled at 20 K min-1 . The melting and glass transition temperatures of the sample were measured with a Netzsch 214 Polyma instrument. The sample (*ca.* 4 mg) was sealed in an aluminium crucible, with an *ca.* 0.5 mm hole on the lid. The temperature ramp was set at 20 K min-1 for the first heating (melting) cycle and then 10 K min-1 for the downscan and second heating cycle. To determine the glass transition temperature, the samples were heated to 693 K for ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm), 683 K for ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm), 708 K for ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) and 723 K for ZIF-UC-5 (5- ClbIm) in the first cycle, before being cooled back to 310 K for the second heating cycle. An empty pan was run with identical settings and this was subtracted from the original trace, in order to baseline correct all scans.

#### **Scanning electron microscopy**

The surface morphologies of the crystal and glass samples were examined with a highresolution scanning electron microscope (FEI Nova Nano SEM 450, accelerating voltage voltage 10 kV). After being vacuum dried overnight, all samples were coated with a layer of gold prior to imaging.

#### **Synchrotron X-ray total scattering and pair distribution function analysis**

XRD data were collected on the I15-1 beamline at the Diamond Light Source, UK using an Xray wavelength λ = 0.161669 Å (76.7 keV). Crystalline and glass samples were loaded into borosilicate glass capillaries of  1.17 mm (inner) diameter. Data from the samples, empty instrument and empty capillary were collected in the region of ~0.4 < Q < ~ 26 Å−1 . Corrections for background, multiple scattering, container scattering, Compton scattering and absorption were performed using the GudrunX program. <sup>4</sup> The refinement was carried out with PDFGUI software.<sup>5</sup>

#### **Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy**

Solid-state NMR measurements were performed on a 600 MHz Varian NMR system equipped with a 1.6 mm HXY magic angle spinning (MAS) probe. For all the experiments, the sample spinning frequency was 40 kHz. <sup>19</sup>F MAS NMR spectra were recorded using Hahn-echo sequence with inter-pulse delays of 25 µs (one rotation period). The 90° pulse length was 1.7 µs, 180° pulse length was 3.4 µs, and 160 scans were accumulated with the repetition delay of 5 s. The 1H-<sup>13</sup>C cross-polarization (CP) MAS experiments consisted of excitation of protons with a 90° pulse of 1.5 µs, followed by the CP block of 5 ms, and signal acquisition with highpower XiX proton decoupling. A total of 40,000 scans were accumulated with the repetition delay of 1 s. For the 1H-<sup>13</sup>C Lee-Goldburg cross-polarization (LG-CP) experiment, the LG-CP scheme with duration of 50 µs was used. The 19F-<sup>13</sup>C CPMAS experiment consisted of <sup>19</sup>F excitation pulse of 1.7 µs and the CP block of 1 ms. The number of scans was 10,000 and recycling delay was 1 s. For the selective <sup>19</sup>F-<sup>13</sup>C CPMAS experiment the <sup>19</sup>F transmitting frequency was set to the selected peak position and a low-power <sup>19</sup>F excitation pulse with duration of 10 µs was applied prior to the CP block. The contact time was 5 ms, the repetition delay 2.5 s and 3300 scans were accumulated. The two-dimensional <sup>19</sup>F spin-diffusion measurement on ZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) consisted of 50 increments along the indirect dimension with 32 scans per increment and repetition delay of 1 s; spectral width in the indirect dimension was 40 kHz. The spin-diffusion mixing time was 100 ms. The wide-range <sup>19</sup>F spin-diffusion experiment on *a*gZIF-UC-4 (5-FbIm) was performed with 80 increments and 64 scans for each increment; spectral width in the indirect dimension was 160 kHz. Spin-diffusion mixing times varied between 0 and 100 ms. <sup>19</sup>F spin-lattice (T1) and spin-spin (T2) relaxation-time measurements were carried out under MAS using the inversion-recovery and spin-echo pulse sequences, respectively. Larmor frequencies for <sup>19</sup>F and <sup>13</sup>C nuclei were 564.03 MHz and 150.73 MHz, respectively. <sup>19</sup>F chemical shifts were referenced to the signal of CFCl<sup>3</sup> and <sup>13</sup>C chemical shifts to the signal of TMS.

#### **Gas sorption and gas pycnometry (density) tests**

Nitrogen and carbon dioxide gas sorption isotherm tests were carried out with a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 instrument. For each measurement, at least 100 mg sample was used. All samples were degassed overnight at 450 K under vaccum. For measurement, the equilibration interval was 45 s and the minimum equilibration delay at p/p\*>=0.995 was 600s. The BET surface area was calculated using pyGAPS v2.0.2 (Python General Adsorption Processing Suite) framework on Pythonv3.5. <sup>6</sup> A Micromeritics Accupyc 1340 helium pycnometer was used for the density measurement. For each test, a minimum of 100 mg of sample was applied and a cycle of 10 measurements was conducted. The volumetric gas uptake (cm<sup>3</sup> (STP) / cm<sup>3</sup> adsorbent) was calculated with the measured sample density.

#### **Nanoindentation measurement**

The mechanical properties of the glass samples were measured with an MTS Nanoindenter XP system. All glass samples were mounted within epoxy resin followed by polishing with fine diamond suspension liquids. Nanoindentation experiments were carried out at ambient conditions under the dynamic displacement controlled mode, with a constant strain rate at 0.05 s -1 . The testing penetration depth of 1000 nm using a three-sided pyramidal (Berkovich) diamond indenter tip. A Poisson's ratio of 0.4 was applied. Each sample was tested for at least 20 different locations. The load-displacement data were analyzed using the Oliver and Pharr method, with the gradient of unloading curves measured at 90 % of the maximum load.<sup>7</sup>

#### **Computational methodology for bonding strength and charge density**

DFT calculations were performed using projected augmented wave (PAW)8,9 potentials as implemented in VASP code10,11 with following electrons treated explicitly: Zn 3d<sup>10</sup>4s<sup>2</sup> , O 2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>4</sup> , C 2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>2</sup> N 2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>3</sup>and H 1s<sup>1</sup> . Van der Waals functional (optB86b-vdW)<sup>12</sup> was used to treat the exchange-correlation part. A 500 eV kinetic energy cut-off and a gamma point only k-point mesh were used for all calculations. Calculations were performed on the clusters of Zn(ImH)3(5-ClbImH) and Zn(ImH)3(5-FbImH) where all the bridging nitrogens within Im and bIm are connected to a H atom. Clusters were put at the center of a 30×30×30 Å<sup>3</sup> box and relaxed until interatomic forces were smaller than 0.01 eV/Å.

#### **Pelletized bulk glass**

200 mg ZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm) was vitrified at 683 K in a tube furnace at a ramp rate of 20 K min-1 . Then the sample was held at 683 K for 14 min, before being cooled to room temperature. PXRD was performed to ensure the sample was fully amorphized. The quenched sample was ball milled at 25 Hz for 5 minutes with two 7 mm stainless steel balls to produce a fine powder. Then the powder was pressed into a 13 mm diameter pellet with the application of 10 tons for 1 min. The pellet was then heated to 653 K (i.e., above *T*<sup>g</sup> but below the decomposition temperature of the crystal) and held for 5 hours. The resultant sample was referred to as agrZIF-UC-3 (5-Cl-2-mbIm), where 'gr' refers to remelted glass.

#### **Synchrotron TeraHz Far-Infrared (THz/Far-IR) absorption spectroscopy**

THz/Far-IR absorption spectra of all samples were collected at the THz/Far-IR beamline at the Australian Synchrotron. The THz spectroscopy was performed using a Bruker IFS 125/HR Fourier Transform (FT) spectrometer. The measurements were performed with a 6 µm thick Multilayer Mylar beamsplitter. In order to obtain a high signal-to-noise ratio, a liquid heliumcooled bolometer was deployed.

The Attenuated Total Reflection (ATR) method was applied for sample analysis: powdered samples were mounted on top of the diamond ATR crystal, then held in position by applying pressure with the lever arm. For each test, around 20 mg sample was used to ensure the sample thickness was higher than the penetration depth of the beam, which can be of the order of 20- 200 µm. Prior to each sample measurement, the diamond crystal was cleaned with isopropanol, and background spectra were collected. For *in situ* heating/cooling measurements, the background spectra were recorded at each tested temperature prior to recording the corresponding sample spectra in order to obtain accurate data. The ATR heating stage and sample were enclosed in an environment of flowing argon gas. The collected spectral data were then corrected via Extended ATR correction algorithms in the OPUS 8.0 software. The peak positions in the spectra during the *in situ* heating test were assigned using NumPy module v1.15 together with Python v3.5. 13

#### **Computational details for THz/Far-IR spectra**

Standard computational chemistry calculations were carried out using Gaussian 16 for the calculation of THz/Far-IR spectra. The detailed procedure can be found in the literature. 14 Briefly, we used a series of molecular species to model the key component of interest of the materials system. They were complexes of Zn with three Im and one bIm derivatives, with an overall charge of minus two; they essentially corresponded to Zn(II) complexes. Geometries of these molecular models were optimized with the B3-LYP method <sup>15</sup> in conjunction with the 6-31+G(d,p) basis set.<sup>16</sup> Harmonic vibrational frequencies were obtained at the same level of theory and they were inspected to ensure that the optimized structures correspond to minima on their potential energy surface. Fundamental frequencies reported in the text correspond to scaled B3-LYP/6-31+G(d,p) harmonic frequencies using the literature scale factor of 0.9648. <sup>17</sup> Simulated infrared spectra were obtained by assuming a Gaussian band for each peak with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 10 cm-1 .

## ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm) (**cag** ─ Orthorhombic) Experimental and Refinement Details for **ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm)**

*(CCDC Deposition Number 1951694)*

A colourless polyhedral crystal (0.10 × 0.07 × 0.05 mm<sup>3</sup> ) of ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm) was placed in a 0.2 mm diameter borosilicate loop and mounted on a Gemini E Ultra diffractometer from Oxford Diffraction, equipped with an Atlas CCD area detector and operated at 35 kV and 35 mA, to generate Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54184 Å) at 293(2) K. A total of 10790 reflections were collected, of which 3641 were unique and 3021 of these were greater than 2*σ(I)*. The range of *θ* was from 2.44 to 63.99°. Analysis of the data showed negligible decay during collection. The structure was solved in the orthorhombic *Pbca* space group with *Z* = 8 using the SUPERFLIP method.<sup>18</sup> Modeling of electron density led to the identification of an asymmetric unit with two independent Zn atoms and four organic linkers. From those four linkers, three are unsubstituted Imidazolates with no disorder, and the fourth is disordered between 6-Cl-5-FbIm (with occupancy 0.260(7)) and Imidazole (with occupancy 0.740(7)). Note that in this fourth position the 6-Cl-5-FbIm linker is shifted away from the complementary Im position by 54.80°, which results in partial occupancy of sites normally occupied by solvent molecules. This shift avoids a steric clash with neighboring Im linkers in the fully occupied sites. This feature, in addition to a previous desolvation of the crystal caused the electron density within the void of the frameworks to be too low to model the solvent. With the exception of disordered Cl, F and four partial occupancy benzene carbon atoms, all non-hydrogen atoms have been refined anisotropically. All hydrogen atoms have been placed in geometrically located positions and their displacement parameters are tied to those of the attached carbon atoms. The unit cell of ZIF-UC-2 (6-Cl-5-FbIm) contains 1.87 Im and 0.13 6-Cl-5-FbIm per Zn. Final full matrix least squares refinement on *F* <sup>2</sup> converged to *R*<sup>1</sup> = 0.0459 (*F* >2*σF*)) and *wR*<sup>2</sup> = 0.1511 (all data), with GooF = 1.074.

## ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) (**cag** ─ Orthorhombic) Experimental and Refinement Details for **ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm)**

#### *(Deposition Number 1951696)*

A colourless tetrahedral crystal (0.05 × 0.04 × 0.03 mm<sup>3</sup> ) of ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) was placed in a 0.1 mm diameter borosilicate loop and mounted on a Gemini E Ultra diffractometer from Oxford Diffraction equipped with an Atlas CCD area detector and operated at 35 kV and 35 mA, to generate Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54184 Å) at 293(2) K. A total of 11094 reflections were collected, of which 3786 were unique and 2684 of these were greater than 2*σ*(I). The range of *θ* was from 2.43 to 64.01°. Analysis of the data showed negligible decay during collection. The structure was solved in the orthorhombic *Pbca* space group with *Z* = 8 using the SUPERFLIP method.<sup>18</sup> Modeling of electron density led to the identification of 0.39 DMF molecules in the asymmetric unit (atoms with no crystallographic symmetry). The asymmetric unit is also integrated by two Zn atoms and four linkers. From those four linkers, two are unsubstituted Im with no disorder, a third linker in a position disordered between 5-ClbIm (0.397(10) occupancy) and Im (0.603(10) occupancy); and the fourth linker is in a position disordered between 5- ClbIm(0.171(11) occupancy) and Im (0.829(11) occupancy). The third position can contain a maximum of 50% 5-ClbIm because the center of symmetry in this space group would bring the 5-ClbIm linkers related by this center into contact with each other. The 5-ClbIm linker at the fourth position is shifted away from the complementary Im position by 57.47°, which results in partial occupancy of sites normally occupied by solvent molecules. The shift happens to avoid clashing with one of the two neighboring Im linker in totally occupied sites. This feature, in addition to a previous desolvation of the crystal is responsible for the low occupancy for solvent molecules. All non-H atoms, except the solvent molecules, the four C atoms from benzene rings in both 5-ClbIm linkers and corresponding Cl atoms, have been refined anisotropically. All H atoms, have been placed in geometrically located positions and their displacement parameters are tied to those of the attached C atoms. The positions for both Cl atoms and solvent atoms were fixed to be able to separate their contributions to the residual electronic density. The unit cell of ZIF-UC-5 (5-ClbIm) contains 1.72 Im and 0.28 ClbIm per Zn. Final full matrix least - 50 squares refinement on *F* <sup>2</sup> converged to *R*<sup>1</sup> = 0.0646 (*F* >2*σF*)) and *wR*<sup>2</sup> = 0.2107 (all data) with GooF = 1.103.
