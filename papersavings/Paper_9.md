## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the two‐step solvothermal synthesis combined with the Stöber method for preparing the Fe@Si catalyst. The procedure has been documented with strict attention to reproducible conditions, specifically addressing precursor mixing, precipitation–deposition, drying, and calcination.

#### Fe2O3 Nanoparticle Preparation (Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation)
1. Dissolve 7.39 g of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and 7.21 g of PVP in 800 mL of anhydrous ethanol.  
   • Reagent dissolution is carried out at room temperature with constant stirring for 5 hours, creating a yellow suspension that indicates initial precursor formation.  
2. Transfer the solution into a stirred stainless autoclave equipped with a Teflon‑lined vessel.  
   • The mixture is heated at a constant rate of 1 °C/min until reaching 180 °C, then maintained at this temperature for 18 hours.  
   • The controlled heating and stirring ensure uniform nucleation of Fe2O3 nanoparticles.

#### Precipitation–Deposition and SiO₂ Shell Formation
3. After the solvothermal synthesis and cooling, pour the solution into a reaction vessel for the SiO₂ deposition.  
   • Add 25 mL of tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) dropwise into a mixture containing 144 mL of NH₃·H₂O (25%), 1656 mL deionized water, and 1276 mL ethanol.  
   • CTAB is introduced as a templating agent to direct the formation of a mesoporous silica shell.  
4. Stir the resulting mixture at room temperature for 48 hours.  
   • The pH is maintained at 9.0 ± 0.1 to ensure a consistent hydrolysis rate of TEOS, promoting an even SiO₂ coating.

#### Drying and Calcination
5. Collect the solid products by filtration, then wash thoroughly with distilled water.  
   • The products are dried at 120 °C for 24 hours to remove residual solvents.
6. Finally, calcine the dried catalyst at 550 °C for 6 hours.  
   • This post‑processing step removes the template (CTAB) and stabilizes the Fe–SiO₂ interaction, minimizing sintering during subsequent catalytic operations.

This systematic approach, which involves precise control over reaction parameters such as temperature, pH, and reagent addition, ensures high reproducibility and consistency in catalyst performance. The detailed numerical values and equipment specifications allow other researchers to replicate the synthesis and evaluate the catalyst under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Si

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Fe2O₃ Nanoparticles Synthesis
- **Reagents**: 7.39 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, anhydrous ethanol (sufficient volume to fully dissolve), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as a stabilizer.
- **Conditions**: Dissolution at room temperature with stirring for 5 hours, followed by solvothermal treatment in a stirrer stainless autoclave at 180 °C for 18 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in anhydrous ethanol, adding PVP to prevent nanoparticle aggregation.
  - Stir the solution for 5 hours to ensure homogeneous dissolution.
  - Transfer the solution to the autoclave, then heat at 180 °C for 18 hours with a controlled heating rate.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and stable dispersion by controlling stirring rate and temperature throughout the process.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly – SiO₂ Shell Formation via Stöber Process
- **Reagents**: Tetraethoxysilane (TEOS), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB; 6 g), silica sol (with hydroxyl groups), 144 mL NH₃·H₂O (25%), deionized water, and ethanol.
- **Conditions**: Maintain pH at 9.0 ± 0.1, perform composite stirring; CTAB introduced into a solvent mixture of 1656 mL deionized water and 1276 mL ethanol.
- **Process**:
  - Add CTAB under continuous stirring to form a templated structure.
  - Slowly introduce NH₃·H₂O to adjust the pH, then add TEOS dropwise over a 6-hour period at room temperature.
  - Stir the mixture for an extended period of 48 hours to ensure proper silica condensation around the Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.
- **Key Steps**: Strictly control pH, reagent addition rate, and stirring duration to achieve a uniform SiO₂ shell encapsulation.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Purification**:
  - Recover the solid composite through filtration.
  - Wash extensively with distilled water to remove unreacted materials and residual CTAB.
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the filtered solid at 120 °C to eliminate moisture.
- **Calcination**:
  - Calcine the dried material at 550 °C for 6 hours in air to remove organic templates and stabilize the catalyst structure.
- **Key Steps**: Careful washing and controlled drying are critical to prevent contamination and ensure effective calcination.

*Note*: Each step is documented with precise reagent quantities and operational conditions to support reproducibility. The process sequence adheres strictly to precursor preparation → catalyst assembly → purification → drying → calcination, with activation (e.g., reduction under H₂) being performed as needed.


## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X-ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD analysis was employed for phase identification of the Fe@Si catalyst. Diffraction patterns distinguish between α‑Fe₂O₃ in fresh catalysts and iron carbides/Fe₃O₄ post activation or reaction.
- **Instruments and Models**: Bruker D8 Advance.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Utilizes Co Kα radiation (λ = 1.79026 Å) at 35 kV and 40 mA.
- **Test Parameters**: Scanning performed over a 2θ range from 30° to 90° with a step size of 0.04°.
- **Key Notes**: Sample preparation involves grinding and pelletizing (often with KBr) to ensure proper alignment for reproducible diffraction patterns.

### 4.2 BET N₂ Physisorption
- **Operational Description**: BET measurements quantify surface area and pore size distributions of the catalyst.
- **Instruments and Models**: ASAP 2420.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are degassed sequentially at 90 °C for 120 minutes and 350 °C for 480 minutes under vacuum.
- **Test Parameters**: Analysis conducted at -196 °C with adsorption isotherms used for BET surface area calculation, while the BJH method determines pore size distribution.
- **Key Notes**: Proper degassing is essential to remove moisture and contaminants for accurate adsorption data.

### 4.3 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR evaluates the reduction behavior, highlighting transformations from Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ and partial reduction to FeO.
- **Instruments and Models**: Autochem II 2920, coupled with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).
- **Characterization Conditions**: A 10% H₂/90% Ar gas mixture at a flow rate of 50 mL/min under a temperature ramp from 50 °C to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Approximately 100 mg of catalyst is used with uniform loading in a U‑type tube reactor.
- **Key Notes**: Precise temperature control and gas flow are critical for capturing clear reduction peaks.

### 4.4 TEM & HAADF-STEM with EDS
- **Operational Description**: These microscopy techniques analyze particle morphology and elemental distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Talos F200X.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Catalyst samples are dispersed in ethanol for 20 minutes, then deposited onto a carbon membrane.
- **Test Parameters**: Both TEM and HAADF-STEM imaging are performed, with integrated EDS mapping to verify the spatial confinement of the Fe particles.
- **Key Notes**: Proper dispersion ensures reproducible imaging quality.

### 4.5 Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: Mössbauer measurements quantify phase compositions and iron speciation during catalyst activation and after reaction.
- **Instruments and Models**: A constant-acceleration spectrometer operating at 50 K.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are analyzed in both activated and used states.
- **Test Parameters**: Spectra are fitted using nonlinear least-squares routines to extract isomer shifts, quadruple splitting, and magnetic hyperfine fields.
- **Key Notes**: This method is sensitive to the subtle differences in iron chemical states.

### 4.6 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR)
- **Operational Description**: FT‑IR is utilized to investigate Fe–O–Si and Si–O–Si bonding environments, indicating interaction strength between Fe and SiO₂.
- **Instruments and Models**: VERTEX 70.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are mixed with KBr in a 1:50 ratio and pressed into translucent disks at room temperature.
- **Test Parameters**: Spectra are recorded over 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.
- **Key Notes**: Careful sample preparation enhances the detection of characteristic vibrational bands.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation of the Fe@Si catalyst is a dedicated pre‐reaction treatment that converts precursor iron oxides into active phases, thereby exposing essential active sites for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### Activation Temperature
Activation temperature is critical for driving the reduction and partial carburization of the Fe species. In this procedure, the catalyst is activated at 350 °C. This temperature has been optimized to facilitate the conversion of Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ and promote the early formation of active iron-carbide phases without causing excessive sintering.

#### Activation Pressure
The catalyst activation is conducted under atmospheric pressure (approximately 1 atm). Performing the activation at atmospheric conditions maintains a controlled reduction environment and ensures that any kinetic effects due to pressure variations are minimized, thus focusing on the gas–solid interactions governing phase transformation.

#### Activation Gas Composition
A syngas mixture with a H₂/CO ratio of 2 is employed during the activation phase. This specific gas composition provides the necessary reductant for the reduction of iron oxides while moderating the carbon deposition that leads to carburization. Notably, the balanced syngas environment allows for sufficient oxygen removal, although the strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction in the catalyst can limit the extent of complete reduction.

#### Activation Time
The catalyst is subjected to the activation process for 24 hours. This extended duration ensures complete conversion of the precursor phases to their active forms. While the prolonged activation ensures robust initial activity, it is observed that catalysts lacking protective structural features may eventually experience gradual deactivation, partially attributed to subtle sintering effects.

Through this well-defined activation protocol—employing precise temperature, pressure, gas composition, and time parameters—the Fe@Si catalyst is reliably transformed into an active state, thereby ensuring reproducibility and optimal performance during subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction testing.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is the operational heat level at which Fischer-Tropsch synthesis is performed. It is a critical parameter that influences both the reduction degree of iron oxides and the kinetics of hydrocarbon production.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Experiments were conducted at a fixed temperature of 280 °C.
- **Observations:** At 280 °C, the Fe@Si catalyst initially exhibits high activity; however, the catalyst gradually deactivates due to the oxidation of iron carbides by H₂O. The confinement provided by the SiO₂ shell further impedes effective removal of H₂O, contributing to deactivation trends over time.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure determines the syngas concentration and can affect both the kinetics of the reaction and the selectivity towards heavier hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The fixed-bed reactor was operated at a pressure of 2.0 MPa.
- **Observations:** Elevated pressure enhances syngas density within the reactor, thereby promoting overall reaction kinetics. It also correlates with an improved formation of heavier hydrocarbons under optimized conditions.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is essential in directing the product distribution during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis by regulating hydrogenation reactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A stoichiometric ratio of H₂/CO = 2 was maintained throughout the experiments.
- **Observations:** This defined ratio ensures balanced hydrogen availability, which is crucial for steering the synthesis towards desired hydrocarbon chains while managing methane selectivity.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, typically expressed as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), is the ratio of syngas flow rate to catalyst mass. It directly impacts reactant-catalyst contact time and, consequently, conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹ was employed.
- **Observations:** The selected GHSV reflects a high volumetric flow relative to the catalyst load, promoting a shorter residence time for the reactants. This parameter is crucial since it directly influences CO conversion levels, with potential trade-offs between conversion efficiency and catalyst stability.

Each parameter is explicitly defined and quantitatively reported in the article to ensure the reproducibility of the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis conditions, allowing future experiments to replicate these settings accurately.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase evolution of the Fe@Si catalyst were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 ADVANCE). XRD is essential for identifying crystalline phases and monitoring transformations during activation and reaction.
- **Findings:** The fresh Fe@Si catalyst exhibited only well-crystallized hematite (α‑Fe₂O₃), with characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 38.7°, 41.7°, 63.9°, and 74.1°. After activation and Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS), new peaks corresponding to iron carbides and Fe₃O₄ appeared, while the α‑Fe₂O₃ peaks diminished or disappeared, indicating phase transformation.
- **Observations:** The formation of iron carbides and Fe₃O₄ after activation is indicative of successful reduction and carburization, which are critical for FTS activity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were determined by N₂ physisorption using the BET method (ASAP 2420, Micromeritics). These parameters influence catalyst dispersion, accessibility, and mass transfer.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si displayed a high BET surface area of 652 m²/g, a pore volume of 0.64 cm³/g, and an average pore size of 2.62 nm. The mesoporous structure was achieved using CTAB as a template during synthesis.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and uniform mesopores enhance catalyst dispersion and stability. However, the SiO₂ shell and small pore size can hinder the removal of H₂O and reaction products, potentially affecting reduction and leading to deactivation.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR, Autochem II 2920) was used to study the reducibility of iron species, providing insight into the stepwise reduction process.
- **Findings:** The H₂-TPR profile of Fe@Si showed four reduction peaks: Peak I at 328 °C (0.03 mol H₂/mol Fe), Peak II at 416 °C (0.27 mol H₂/mol Fe), Peak III at 531 °C (0.49 mol H₂/mol Fe), and Peak IV at 674 °C (0.16 mol H₂/mol Fe). These correspond to the sequential reduction of Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄, partial reduction to FeO, and further reduction at higher temperatures.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell restricts H₂ access and H₂O removal, resulting in incomplete reduction compared to non-encapsulated catalysts.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Talos F200X) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) with EDS mapping were employed to examine particle morphology and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles (~50.4 nm) embedded within a porous SiO₂ shell, with each particle physically isolated. EDS mapping confirmed Fe localization within the SiO₂ matrix, demonstrating uniform distribution and effective confinement.
- **Observations:** The core–shell structure prevents agglomeration and sintering, maintaining particle size and dispersion after reaction.

#### 6.1.5 Phase Composition by Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the relative amounts of iron phases after activation and reaction, providing detailed phase composition.
- **Findings:** After activation, Fe@Si contained 52.01% iron carbide, with the remainder as Fe₃O₄ and Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ superparamagnetic ions. The extent of carbide formation was influenced by the Fe–SiO₂ interaction strength.
- **Observations:** Higher iron carbide content correlates with enhanced FTS activity, while stronger Fe–SiO₂ interactions (as in Fe-Si) suppress carbide formation.

#### 6.1.6 Metal–Support Interaction by FT-IR
- **Description:** FT-IR spectroscopy (VERTEX 70, Bruker) was used to probe Fe–O–Si and Si–O–Si bond vibrations, reflecting the interaction between Fe species and the SiO₂ support.
- **Findings:** The Fe–O–Si band (957–977 cm⁻¹) and Si–O–Si band (1120 cm⁻¹) were observed, with the Fe–O–Si/Si–O–Si intensity ratio (I/II) increasing in the order Fe@Si < Fe/Si < Fe-Si. This indicates varying interaction strengths, with Fe@Si exhibiting the weakest Fe–SiO₂ interaction.
- **Observations:** The presence of surface hydroxyl groups in the silica sol promotes Fe–O–Si bond formation, influencing the extent of metal–support interaction and, consequently, catalyst reducibility and phase evolution.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (280 °C, 2.0 MPa, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹, H₂/CO = 2), Fe@Si exhibited an initial CO conversion of 66.6%, which decreased to 47.9% after 64 hours on stream. The impregnated Fe/Si catalyst showed a similar high initial conversion but experienced a more pronounced decline due to sintering.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@Si’s high initial activity is attributed to its well-dispersed iron oxide nanoparticles. However, the gradual decrease in conversion is linked to the SiO₂ shell’s diffusion resistance, which impedes H₂O removal and promotes oxidation of active iron carbides, accelerating deactivation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating water-gas shift (WGS) activity:
  \[
  \text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 64 hours, Fe@Si displayed a CO₂ selectivity of 38.2%. The Fe/Si catalyst showed a comparable value, while the precipitated Fe–Si catalyst exhibited lower CO₂ selectivity, consistent with reduced WGS activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The elevated CO₂ selectivity for Fe@Si is attributed to the SiO₂ shell, which increases local H₂O partial pressure and enhances WGS, leading to greater CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si exhibited a CH₄ selectivity of 24.4% after 64 hours. The Fe/Si catalyst had a similar value, while Fe–Si showed lower CH₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The relatively high CH₄ selectivity for Fe@Si is associated with hindered reduction and increased oxidation of iron carbides, favoring lighter hydrocarbon formation as the catalyst deactivates.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C₅⁺ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C₅⁺,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si achieved a C₅⁺ selectivity of 24.3% after 64 hours, compared to 27.1% for Fe/Si and 46.6% for Fe–Si.
- **Observations and Trends:** The lower C₅⁺ selectivity for Fe@Si is attributed to the SiO₂ shell’s confinement effect, which hinders the desorption of heavier hydrocarbons and promotes the formation of lighter products.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth in hydrocarbon formation, with higher values favoring longer chains. It is derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** The article reports that Fe@Si exhibits a moderate α value, lower than Fe–Si, reflecting its lower selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor for Fe@Si is limited by the SiO₂ shell’s diffusion resistance, which restricts the formation and desorption of heavier hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe@Si, CO conversion decreased from 66.6% to 47.9% over 64 hours, corresponding to a deactivation rate of approximately 0.29% h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The primary cause of deactivation is the oxidation of iron carbides by H₂O retained within the SiO₂ shell. In contrast, Fe/Si deactivates mainly due to iron particle sintering.

#### 6.2.7 Olefin/Paraffin Ratio (O/P Ratio)
- **Description:** The O/P ratio in C₂–C₄ hydrocarbons reflects the relative production of olefins versus paraffins, indicating the catalyst’s ability to favor unsaturated hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si showed a lower O/P ratio compared to Fe–Si, consistent with its higher degree of reduction hindrance and lower olefin selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Stronger Fe–SiO₂ interaction in Fe–Si promotes higher O/P ratios, while the SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si limits olefin formation.

**Summary:**  
Fe@Si demonstrates high initial CO conversion and anti-sintering stability due to its core–shell structure, but suffers from rapid deactivation and lower selectivity for heavier hydrocarbons, primarily due to the SiO₂ shell’s diffusion resistance. The interplay between Fe–SiO₂ interaction strength and reduction behavior critically shapes the observed catalytic performance metrics.
