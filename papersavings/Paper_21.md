## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The AM‐Ru/SiO₂ catalysts were synthesized using an incipient wetness impregnation method executed in a sequential, step‐by‐step manner to ensure reproducibility and uniform distribution of the active phase. This method was selected due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness, with careful control over chemical ratios and process conditions.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation Method

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  - Reagents: Ruthenium nitrosyl nitrate and alkali metal nitrates (LiNO₃, NaNO₃, KNO₃, RbNO₃, CsNO₃) are combined in an aqueous solution.  
  - Ratio: A fixed alkali metal-to-Ru molar ratio of 0.5 is maintained; for potassium-promoted catalysts, additional samples are prepared with K/Ru ratios of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 1.0.  
  - Observations: Homogeneous mixing is indicated by a uniform solution, ensuring complete dissolution and interaction of precursors.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  - Process: The prepared precursor solution is carefully added to AEROSIL 380 aerosol silica until complete saturation is achieved, ensuring intimate contact between the metal species and the support.  
  - Equipment: A calibrated pipette or burette is typically used to control the dropwise addition, ensuring precision during deposition.

3. • Drying  
  - Conditions: The impregnated silica is dried at 60 °C for 4 hours in a drying oven.  
  - Rationale: This step removes residual solvent and promotes an even distribution of the precursors on the support without causing premature agglomeration.

4. • Calcination  
  - Parameters: The dried sample is calcined in air at 400 °C for 3 hours with a ramping rate of 2 °C/min.  
  - Observation: Calcination facilitates the formation of RuO₂ and aids in anchoring alkali promoters on the silica, enabling subsequent reduction to metallic Ru during post‑treatment if needed.

These carefully monitored steps ensure that every batch of catalysts is prepared with precision, meeting the reproducibility standards required for catalytic evaluation in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The approach also allows comparative analysis of the synthesis complexity, scalability, and equipment requirements inherent to the incipient wetness impregnation method.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the incipient wetness impregnation method is applied consistently for all AM‐Ru/SiO₂ catalysts (AM = Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) to ensure reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Li‐Ru/SiO₂  
- **Ruthenium nitrosyl nitrate (Ru precursor)**: Weighed in an amount calculated to achieve ca. 4.0 wt% Ru on the support.  
- **LiNO₃ (Alkali metal promoter)**: Weighed to maintain an Li/Ru molar ratio of 0.5.  

##### 3.1.1.2 Na‐Ru/SiO₂  
- **Ruthenium nitrosyl nitrate**: Weighed to target ca. 4.0 wt% Ru loading.  
- **NaNO₃**: Weighed to provide a Na/Ru molar ratio of 0.5.  

##### 3.1.1.3 K‐Ru/SiO₂  
- **Ruthenium nitrosyl nitrate**: Weighed for ca. 4.0 wt% Ru on SiO₂.  
- **KNO₃**: Weighed to maintain a K/Ru ratio of 0.5; additional samples were prepared with K/Ru ratios of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 1.0 for comparative studies.  

##### 3.1.1.4 Rb‐Ru/SiO₂  
- **Ruthenium nitrosyl nitrate**: Weighed to meet the 4.0 wt% Ru target.  
- **RbNO₃**: Weighed to establish an Rb/Ru molar ratio of 0.5.  

##### 3.1.1.5 Cs‐Ru/SiO₂  
- **Ruthenium nitrosyl nitrate**: Weighed for 4.0 wt% Ru loading.  
- **CsNO₃**: Weighed to achieve a Cs/Ru molar ratio of 0.5.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

For each catalyst formulation:  
- **Impregnation solution**: A solution prepared by dissolving the weighed ruthenium nitrosyl nitrate and the corresponding alkali metal nitrate in a minimal solvent is transferred to the AEROSIL 380 support using the incipient wetness technique. The liquid volume is adjusted to match the pore volume of the support, ensuring full saturation without excess.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

For all catalysts:  
- **Impregnated support**: Mixed thoroughly to ensure a uniform distribution of the metal precursors over the silica. After mixing, the sample is dried at 60 °C for 4 hours. Calcination in air is then carried out at 400 °C for 3 hours with a ramping rate of 2 °C/min to convert the precursors to the active catalyst phase.

This structured approach, with clearly delineated steps for weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing, secures operational clarity and full reproducibility across the AM‐Ru/SiO₂ catalyst preparations.

  - Stir until a homogeneous solution is achieved to guarantee that all primary precursors are present.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X-ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was performed to confirm the phase transformation from RuO₂ to metallic Ru and to determine crystallite sizes.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Rigaku Ultima IV X‑ray diffractometer.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Utilized Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA; operating at a scanning rate of 2°/min across a 2θ range sufficient to encompass key diffraction peaks.  
- **Test Parameters**: Sample pre-treatment involved drying at 60 °C for 4 hours and calcination in air at 400 °C for 3 hours (ramping at 2 °C/min) to ensure proper crystallization.  
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment minimizes errors in peak positions and intensities.

### 4.2 H₂-Temperature-Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Evaluated reduction behavior by pre-treating in He followed by reduction in H₂/Ar.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Micro fixed-bed reactor equipped with a thermal conductivity detector.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Pre-treatment in He at 120 °C for 60 minutes; cooling to 50 °C; heating to 800 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min under 5 vol% H₂ (balance Ar) at 30 mL/min.  
- **Test Parameters**: Ensured uniform sample loading for reproducibility.  
- **Key Notes**: Reduction peak shifts indicate promoter effects on Ru reducibility.

### 4.3 CO‑Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Mirrors H₂‑TPR steps with emphasis on CO interactions.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Conducted using a gas mixture of 5 vol% H₂ (balance Ar) at 30 mL/min, highlighting shifts in reduction peaks with promoter addition.  
- **Key Notes**: Similar operational care is required to ensure accurate interpretation of CO-induced reduction behavior.

### 4.4 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: N₂ physisorption experiments determined surface area and porosity.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements at –196 °C using the BET method; sample pretreatment ensured removal of moisture for accurate analysis.

### 4.5 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)
- **Operational Description**: Provided particle size distribution and confirmed homogeneous metal dispersion.  
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S‑TWIN operated at 200 kV.

### 4.6 X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: Investigated surface electronic states and electron donation effects.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Thermo Fisher Scientific K‑Alpha with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV); calibration referenced C1s at 284.8 eV.  
- **Key Notes**: Pre‑reduction in H₂ at 450 °C for 4 hours ensured minimal surface contamination.

### 4.7 In situ Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS)
- **Operational Description**: Monitored CO chemisorption dynamics on the catalyst surface.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Pre‑treated in H₂ at 450 °C for 1 hour, cooled under Ar to 50 °C or 270 °C, then exposed to CO; scan range focused on 2100–1850 cm⁻¹.

### 4.8 ICP‑OES / XRF

- **Key Notes**: Provided elemental quantification for catalyst composition verification.

- **Observations:** This high temperature ensures complete reduction of the Ru precursor, enhancing electron density on Ru. Notably, the process achieves full metal reduction while maintaining uniform nanoparticle dispersion without evidence of carbonization.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying the active phase and assessing nanoparticle stability under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis confirmed the complete transformation of RuO₂ to metallic Ru after reduction, as evidenced by the disappearance of RuO₂ peaks (JCPDS 43-1027) and the emergence of metallic Ru peaks (JCPDS 06-0663). The metallic Ru phase persisted after catalytic testing, indicating structural stability. The crystallite size of Ru in alkali-promoted catalysts was calculated to be approximately 6 nm using Scherrer’s equation.
- **Observations:** The addition of alkali metal promoters led to a decrease in Ru particle size and enhanced metal dispersion, both of which are favorable for Fischer-Tropsch activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were measured by N₂ physisorption (BET and BJH methods), providing insight into catalyst accessibility and support structure.
- **Findings:** The unpromoted Ru/SiO₂ catalyst exhibited a BET surface area of 307 m²·g⁻¹. Upon alkali metal promotion, the surface area decreased to 210–267 m²·g⁻¹, while the pore volume (1.0–1.2 cm³·g⁻¹) and average pore size (15.5–19.4 nm) remained largely unchanged. The mesoporous structure of the SiO₂ support was retained across all samples.
- **Observations:** The decrease in surface area is attributed to partial pore blockage by the promoters, but the preservation of mesoporosity ensures effective reactant diffusion and catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility
- **Description:** H₂-temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was employed to assess the reducibility of Ru species, which influences the formation of the active metallic phase.
- **Findings:** All catalysts displayed a single reduction peak corresponding to the reduction of RuO₂ to Ru. The reduction peak for unpromoted Ru/SiO₂ appeared at 134 °C. With alkali metal addition, the reduction peak shifted to higher temperatures, with the shift increasing as the alkali metal’s electronegativity decreased.
- **Observations:** Lower electronegativity alkali metals (e.g., Cs) more strongly inhibit RuO₂ reduction, reflecting stronger metal-support-promoter interactions.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to evaluate the dispersion and size distribution of Ru nanoparticles, which are critical for catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed homogeneous dispersion of Ru nanoparticles on SiO₂, with average particle sizes ranging from 4.7 to 5.7 nm for alkali-promoted catalysts. These values were consistent with XRD-derived crystallite sizes. Particle size distributions were determined by measuring at least 200 nanoparticles per sample.
- **Observations:** The introduction of alkali metals led to smaller and more uniformly distributed Ru nanoparticles, which correlates with increased catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition and Promoter Loading
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) and X-ray fluorescence (XRF) were used to verify Ru and alkali metal loadings.
- **Findings:** All AM-Ru/SiO₂ catalysts had a Ru loading of approximately 4.0 wt.% and an AM/Ru molar ratio of 0.5. For K-promoted samples, K/Ru ratios of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 1.0 were confirmed.
- **Observations:** Accurate control of promoter loading ensures reliable correlation between catalyst composition and performance.

#### 6.1.6 Electronic Structure and Surface Chemistry
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) were conducted to probe the electronic environment of Ru and the effect of alkali promoters.
- **Findings:** XPS revealed a shift in the Ru 3p₃/₂ binding energy to lower values with decreasing alkali metal electronegativity, indicating increased electron density on Ru. XANES showed the absorption edge moving closer to that of Ru foil for catalysts with lower electronegativity promoters, confirming the metallic state and electron donation from the promoters.
- **Observations:** Enhanced electron density on Ru, especially with Cs promotion, is linked to suppressed secondary hydrogenation and increased selectivity for long-chain α-olefins.

#### 6.1.7 CO Adsorption Behavior
- **Description:** In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was used to study CO adsorption modes, which are relevant to catalytic selectivity.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS distinguished between linear-type (active) and twin-type (inactive) CO adsorption. The proportion of twin-type CO increased with decreasing alkali metal electronegativity, indicating a promoter-dependent modulation of surface chemistry.
- **Observations:** The stabilization of twin-type CO by lower electronegativity alkali metals correlates with reduced hydrogenation activity and enhanced olefin selectivity.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standardized conditions (270 °C, 5 bar, WHSV 3000 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹, H₂/CO = 2), the bare Ru/SiO₂ catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 54.5%. Upon addition of alkali metal promoters (AM = Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs; AM/Ru = 0.5), CO conversion varied with promoter electronegativity: higher values were observed for Li– and Na–Ru/SiO₂, while K–, Rb–, and Cs–Ru/SiO₂ exhibited lower conversions. For potassium-promoted catalysts, increasing the K/Ru ratio (0.1–1.0) led to a marked decrease in CO conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion decreases as the electronegativity of the alkali metal promoter decreases, indicating a trade-off between activity and selectivity for long-chain olefins.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity for AM–Ru/SiO₂ catalysts ranged from 0.2% to 7.0%. Selectivity increased with decreasing alkali metal electronegativity, with Cs–Ru/SiO₂ at the upper end of this range.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low CO₂ selectivity across all samples indicates minimal water–gas shift activity. Higher K/Ru ratios also led to increased CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** All alkali metal-promoted Ru/SiO₂ catalysts exhibited low CH₄ selectivity (≤4.2%). For K–Ru/SiO₂, CH₄ selectivity remained around 2.0% for K/Ru = 0.1–0.5, increasing to 5.5% at K/Ru = 1.0.
- **Observations and Trends:** The consistently low methane selectivity demonstrates effective suppression of C₁ byproduct formation, especially at moderate promoter loadings.

#### 6.2.4 Olefin Selectivity and Product Distribution
- **Description:** Olefin selectivity is the fraction of total hydrocarbons produced as olefins, with particular attention to lower olefins (C₂–C₄) and long-chain α-olefins (C₅⁺).
- **Experimental Data:** Alkali metal promotion increased overall olefin selectivity. Na–Ru/SiO₂ achieved 40.0% selectivity to lower olefins (C₂–C₄), while Cs–Ru/SiO₂ reached 85.2% selectivity for long-chain α-olefins (C₅⁺). The C₅⁺ hydrocarbon fraction increased from 79.9% (Ru/SiO₂) to 92.5% (Cs–Ru/SiO₂).
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher-electronegativity promoters (Li, Na) favor lower olefins, while lower-electronegativity promoters (K, Rb, Cs) dramatically enhance long-chain α-olefin selectivity. Excessive K loading, however, increases C₁ byproducts and reduces overall activity.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of hydrocarbon chain growth, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory (ASF) model. Higher α values indicate a greater tendency for long-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Data:** The α factor increased with decreasing promoter electronegativity, correlating with the observed rise in C₅⁺ olefin selectivity for K, Rb, and Cs-promoted catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced electron donation from lower-electronegativity alkali metals increases Ru electron density, promoting chain growth and favoring long-chain α-olefin production.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring changes in Ru nanoparticle size and state post-reaction.
- **Experimental Data:** TEM and XANES analyses confirmed that Ru nanoparticles retained their size (~6 nm) and metallic state after reaction across all promoted catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** The preservation of Ru dispersion and mesoporous structure indicates robust catalyst stability under reaction conditions.
