## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

In this study, the Co/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalyst is synthesized using an incipient wetness impregnation technique. This method is chosen for its reproducibility, simplicity, and scalability when preparing catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation Method

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   – Reagent: An aqueous solution of Co(NO₃) is prepared so that cobalt constitutes 20 wt.% of the final catalyst.  
   – Support: Fujisilicia Q‑10 silica gel (surface area: 300 m²/g; pore volume: 1.0 ml/g) is used as the support.  
   – Equipment: Mixing is performed using standard laboratory glassware under controlled conditions.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition (if reported)  
   – In cases where a precipitation–deposition step is applied, the support precursor is added dropwise with continuous stirring.  
   – Note: For the present Co/SiO₂ catalyst, no additional precipitation–deposition step is required, but the protocol is outlined for comparative purposes.

3. • Drying  
   – The impregnated support is dried at approximately 80°C (conditions similar to those used in conventional impregnation methods) until a consistent weight is achieved.  
   – Observation: The system typically exhibits a transition from a wet paste to a powdery solid.

4. • Calcination  
   – The dried catalyst is calcined in air. Although exact parameters can vary, typical conditions include heating at 450°C for 5 h to decompose the nitrate and develop the active oxide phase.  
   – Equipment: A muffle furnace with precise temperature control is used, ensuring reproducibility.

5. • Reduction  
   – Finally, the calcined material is reduced in a flowing hydrogen atmosphere at 673 K for 10 h to generate the active metallic cobalt phase.  
   – This step is critical for achieving optimal catalytic performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### Comparative Analysis

The incipient wetness impregnation method is relatively straightforward and cost‑effective, favoring scale‑up due to its simplicity. It offers excellent reproducibility with controlled precursor loading. However, precision in drying and calcination is essential to avoid inhomogeneities, and additional equipment (such as a controlled hydrogen reduction facility) is required. Overall, the methodology yields catalysts with high activity and specificity toward desired product distributions, enabling subsequent modifications (e.g., physical mixing with H‑ZSM‑5 or Pd/SiO₂) to tailor catalytic performance.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed by the catalyst, typically expressed as a percentage:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The Co/SiO₂ catalyst achieved 100% CO conversion under conditions of H₂/CO = 3.0, 1.0 MPa, 513 K, and W/F = 5.1 g h mol⁻¹. This high conversion was maintained across both the standard catalyst and systems incorporating H‑ZSM‑5 and Pd/SiO₂.
- **Observations and Trends:** The incorporation of H‑ZSM‑5 or Pd/SiO₂ did not diminish CO conversion, indicating robust catalytic activity and efficient utilization of the feedstock under the tested conditions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Both Co/SiO₂ and Co/SiO₂ + ZSM-5 systems exhibited CO₂ selectivity of approximately 2% under identical reaction conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of ZSM-5 did not significantly alter CO₂ selectivity, suggesting that secondary oxidation reactions remained minimal regardless of catalyst composition.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity denotes the fraction of methane among the converted CO products:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity was 18% for the Co/SiO₂ + ZSM-5 system. Incorporation of Pd/SiO₂ reduced methane selectivity to 11%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The decrease in methane selectivity upon Pd/SiO₂ addition is attributed to enhanced hydrogenation of olefins, which suppresses secondary methane formation during hydrocracking.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity and Hydrocarbon Distribution

- **Observations and Trends:** The use of acidic zeolites and Pd/SiO₂ promotes hydrocracking and isomerization, resulting in lighter, branched hydrocarbons and a suppressed heavy hydrocarbon fraction.

- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon synthesis (0 < α < 1).
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, product distributions imply a lower α for systems containing H‑ZSM‑5 and Pd/SiO₂, as evidenced by the shift from heavy waxes to lighter iso-paraffins.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of zeolite and hydrogenation components reduces the effective α, favoring shorter, branched hydrocarbons over long-chain products.
