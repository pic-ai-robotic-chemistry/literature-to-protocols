## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe–Mn bimetallic catalysts is achieved by a low‑temperature coprecipitation method designed to favor the formation of highly dispersed nano‑catalytic materials. This procedure, performed at –10 °C, employs a systematic sequence of steps to ensure reproducibility and well‑defined phase formation.

#### Low‑temperature Coprecipitation Method
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**
   - Precursors: FeCl₂·4H₂O and MnCl₂·4H₂O are dissolved in ethylene glycol.
   - Environment: The dissolution is conducted under vigorous stirring in a nitrogen‑protected atmosphere to prevent oxidation.
   - Metal ion concentration: The total concentration is fixed at 0.1 mol with Fe/Mn molar ratios defined as Femn(x:1) (e.g., 10:1, 4:1, 2:1, 1:1).

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**
   - An aqueous Na₂CO₃ solution (0.2 M) is introduced into the precursor solution at a controlled rate of 1.2 mL/min. This slow addition is critical for ensuring uniform nucleation and deposition.
   - Observation: A visible precipitate forms during the addition, confirming the initiation of co‑precipitation.
   - Aging: The mixture is allowed to age for 1 hour to stabilize the precipitate uniformly.

3. **Drying**
   - Post‑washing: The precipitate is thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove soluble impurities and with ethanol to assist in dispersion.
   - Drying conditions: The washed solid is then dried at 60 °C overnight.

4. **Calcination**
   - Thermal Treatment: The dried sample is calcined at 450 °C for 4 hours to develop the desired oxide phases and enhance crystallinity.
   - Comparative Analysis: This mild calcination condition is cost‑effective and helps maintain a high surface area compared to conventional high‑temperature methods.

5. **Additional Post‑treatments**
   - Activation: Before catalytic performance testing, an in situ reduction is performed at 350 °C for 5 hours, converting the iron oxides into the active metallic phases.
   - Advantages: The low‑temperature coprecipitation offers improved nucleation control, scalability, and reduced energy consumption, while facilitating the formation of mixed Fe–Mn oxides and MnCO₃ beneficial to catalytic performance.

This detailed methodological framework ensures that all numerical values, reagent specifics, and reaction conditions are clearly defined, enabling precise replication and comparative analysis by future researchers.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to assess the crystalline phases, detect phase transitions (e.g., formation of Fe oxides, mixed Fe–Mn oxides, and iron carbides), and evaluate the decrease in crystallinity with increasing Mn doping. During catalyst activation, samples were reduced in a 5% H₂/Ar atmosphere from room temperature to 350 °C (ramp rate of 5 °C/min, held for 5 hours), and XRD patterns were recorded at specified temperatures.
- **Instruments and Models**: Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV, with a scanning range from 10° to 80° (2θ).
- **Test Parameters**: Step size of 0.02°; scanning rate of 0.24°/min.
- **Key Notes**: Diffraction peaks broaden and shift with increased Mn doping, indicating decreased crystallinity and lattice modifications.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: This method determined the reduction behavior of the catalysts. The analysis monitored the reduction of Fe₂O₃ to metallic Fe and the effect of Mn on reduction profiles.
- **Instruments and Models**: A micro fixed-bed reactor coupled with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).
- **Characterization Conditions**: 10% H₂/Ar flow at 50 mL/min; temperature ramped from 30 °C to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: In situ catalyst pre-treatment involved reduction at 350 °C for 5 hours followed by cooling under Ar.
- **Key Notes**: Small amounts of Mn lower reduction temperatures, while higher Mn content retards Fe(II) reduction.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation step is a critical pre-reaction treatment aimed at converting the calcined precursor oxides into the active phases necessary for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Activation at 350 °C is essential for initiating the reduction and controlled carburization of iron oxides. The controlled heating profile—typically rising from room temperature to 350 °C at a ramp rate of approximately 5 °C per minute—ensures that the monometallic Fe precursors are rapidly reduced to metallic iron, whereas the Fe–Mn bimetallic catalysts require a longer hold to promote the formation of active iron carbide phases. This temperature setting balances the conversion of oxide phases and prevents over‐reduction, maintaining optimal active site exposure.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
The pre-reaction activation is performed without specifying a distinct pressure for this step. Unlike the reaction testing conditions, where a pressure of 2.0 MPa is applied, the activation process is executed under ambient or non-specified pressure conditions. This approach focuses on gas-solid interactions modulated by other parameters such as temperature and gas composition.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition  
A syngas mixture of 5% CO, 5% H₂, and 90% Ar is employed during activation. This particular composition is designed to not only reduce the catalyst but also to promote rapid carburization. The inclusion of CO is instrumental in directing the transformation of Fe oxides into active iron carbide phases, while H₂ aids in achieving effective metal reduction. Such a dual-gas atmosphere facilitates the formation of finely dispersed active configurations, crucial for high catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
No explicit space-time velocity is reported for the activation step. The gas hourly space velocity of 4000 h⁻¹ mentioned in the article applies exclusively to reaction testing. During activation, the focus remains on achieving uniform gas-catalyst contact under the specified gas composition.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
A duration of 5 hours at 350 °C is maintained to ensure thorough in situ reduction and carbide formation. This extended activation period is justified by experimental observations that emphasize its role in ensuring that the active iron carbide phase develops fully, ultimately contributing to enhanced catalyst performance and stability.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch catalytic tests were performed within a temperature window of 260 °C to 350 °C. This range is critical for both activating the Fe–Mn catalysts and ensuring the formation of active phases, such as metallic iron and iron carbides. During the activation phase, the temperature is gradually ramped from room temperature to 350 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and held at 350 °C for 5 hours. Such a controlled heating regime facilitates the reduction of Fe oxides and encourages the proper carburization necessary for sustained catalytic activity. Observations indicate that operating under these conditions helps maintain catalyst stability while mitigating excessive carbon deposition.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The reaction was conducted under a constant pressure of 2.0 MPa in a stainless-steel fixed-bed reactor. This elevated pressure aids in enhancing the syngas concentration, thereby impacting the overall catalyst performance. The controlled high-pressure environment contributes to a balanced feed distribution and influences the selectivity trends observed in the formation of light olefins. The set pressure, therefore, plays an essential role in adjusting the reaction kinetics, ensuring sufficient CO conversion and maintaining reproducibility across tests.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
A syngas mixture consisting of 5% CO, 5% H₂, and 90% Ar was employed, which yields an effective H₂/CO ratio of 1. This specific ratio is pivotal in directing the reaction pathways toward the selective formation of light olefins, while also controlling byproduct formation such as methane and CO₂. The balanced ratio impacts the dissociative adsorption of CO and ultimately influences the distribution of hydrocarbon products.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Gas Hourly Space Velocity (GHSV)  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) was maintained at 4000 h⁻¹. This parameter is defined as the volume flow rate of the syngas relative to the catalyst volume and is crucial for determining the exposure time of the reactants over active sites. A GHSV of 4000 h⁻¹ ensures measurable conversion rates while also supporting catalyst stability during prolonged reaction periods.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of Fe–Mn bimetallic catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), including both ex situ and in situ measurements. XRD analysis is critical for identifying active phases and understanding the influence of Mn on phase transitions.
- **Findings:** Pure Fe catalysts exhibited diffraction peaks corresponding to α‐Fe₂O₃ and γ‐Fe₂O₃. With increasing Mn content, these peaks became broader and less intense, indicating decreased crystallinity. At higher Mn levels, α‐Fe₂O₃ peaks disappeared, and new peaks corresponding to Mn₂O₃ and mixed Fe–Mn oxides (e.g., Mn₀.₅₀₃Fe₀.₄₉₇O) emerged. The addition of Mn caused diffraction patterns to shift to lower angles, attributed to lattice expansion from Mn³⁺ incorporation. In spent catalysts, characteristic peaks for iron carbides (χ‐Fe₅C₂ at 39.4°, 40.9°, 41.4°, 43.5°, 47.3°, 50.2°, and 58.3°) were observed, with their intensity decreasing as Mn content increased.
- **Observations:** Mn doping promotes the formation of mixed Fe–Mn oxides and suppresses the crystallization of iron carbides, stabilizing oxidized Fe species and retarding carburization. Rietveld refinement confirmed a decrease in χ‐Fe₅C₂ content with higher Mn ratios.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Valence States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Mössbauer spectroscopy were employed to determine the surface composition and oxidation states of Fe and Mn. These techniques elucidate the electronic environment and phase distribution, which are crucial for catalytic behavior.
- **Findings:** XPS spectra revealed Fe 2p₃/₂ binding energies for Fe³⁺ (710.8–711.2 eV), Fe²⁺ (709.5–710.0 eV), and FeCx (707.0–708.0 eV). The as-prepared catalysts predominantly contained Fe(III), with Mn enrichment at the surface. After reaction, XPS and Mössbauer analyses detected iron carbides (χ‐Fe₅C₂, ε'-Fe₂.₂C, θ-Fe₃C), metallic Fe, and iron oxides. Increasing Mn content led to a higher proportion of oxidized Fe species and a reduction in iron carbide formation.
- **Observations:** Mn addition stabilizes higher Fe oxidation states and suppresses excessive carburization, as evidenced by the lower χ‐Fe₅C₂ content in high-Mn catalysts.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption using the BET method was conducted to assess specific surface area, a key parameter for catalytic activity due to its influence on active site dispersion and reactant accessibility.
- **Findings:** The specific surface area increased markedly with Mn content: Fe (25 m²/g), FeMn(10:1) (57 m²/g), FeMn(4:1) (88 m²/g), FeMn(2:1) (83 m²/g), FeMn(1:1) (90 m²/g), and Mn (69 m²/g). Samples were degassed at 200 °C for 6 h prior to measurement.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface area and reduced crystallite size with Mn doping indicate improved dispersion and potential for higher catalytic efficiency.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) provided insights into particle morphology, size distribution, and crystallinity. The Scherrer equation was used to corroborate crystallite sizes from XRD data.
- **Findings:** Monometallic Fe formed nanospheres (20–40 nm), while FeMn(4:1) exhibited ellipsoidal particles (10–30 nm) with highly ordered crystals. FeMn(1:1) showed amorphous morphology and significant lattice distortion, with average crystallite sizes decreasing from 19.6 nm (Fe) to 4.4 nm (FeMn(1:1)). EDS mapping confirmed uniform Fe and Mn distribution.
- **Observations:** Low-temperature coprecipitation and Mn doping promote the formation of smaller, well-dispersed particles, reduce crystallinity, and introduce lattice disorder, all of which are beneficial for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Redox Properties
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was used to evaluate the reducibility of Fe species and the effect of Mn on reduction behavior.
- **Findings:** Small Mn additions (FeMn(10:1), FeMn(4:1)) lowered Fe₂O₃ reduction temperatures, while higher Mn content (FeMn(2:1), FeMn(1:1)) shifted reduction peaks to higher temperatures and introduced new peaks for Mn oxide reduction. This indicates that high Mn content stabilizes Fe–Mn mixed oxides, making them more resistant to reduction.
- **Observations:** Mn acts as both an electronic and structural promoter, facilitating reduction at low concentrations but stabilizing oxidized phases and retarding full reduction at higher loadings.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  X_{\text{CO}}(\%) = \frac{Q_{\text{out}} \sum v_i C_i}{Q_{\text{in}} v_{\text{CO}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( Q_{\text{in}} \) and \( Q_{\text{out}} \) are the inlet and outlet gas flow rates (mL/min), \( v_i \) is the volume fraction of component \( i \) in the outlet, \( C_i \) is the carbon number of component \( i \), and \( v_{\text{CO}} \) is the CO fraction in the feed.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (260 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1, GHSV = 4000 h⁻¹), the FeMn(4:1) catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 7.6%. Other Fe–Mn bimetallic catalysts exhibited lower conversions, with the FeMn(10:1) and FeMn(2:1) samples showing reduced activity, and FeMn(1:1) displaying the lowest conversion due to excessive Mn content.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion follows a volcano-type trend with respect to the Fe/Mn ratio, peaking at FeMn(4:1). This optimal ratio balances improved dispersion and electronic effects from Mn with the retention of active iron carbide phases.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  S_{\text{CO}_2}(\%) = \frac{Q_{\text{out}} v_{\text{CO}_2} C_{\text{CO}_2}}{Q_{\text{in}} v_{\text{CO}} X_{\text{CO}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The monometallic Fe catalyst exhibited a CO₂ selectivity of 44%. In contrast, Fe–Mn bimetallic catalysts showed significantly lower CO₂ selectivities, ranging from 20% to 27%, with FeMn(4:1) at the lower end of this range.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Mn suppresses CO₂ formation, indicating reduced water–gas shift activity and a shift in product distribution toward hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  S_{\text{CH}_4}(\%) = \frac{Q_{\text{out}} v_{\text{CH}_4} C_{\text{CH}_4}}{Q_{\text{in}} v_{\text{CO}} X_{\text{CO}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The highest CH₄ selectivity (15%) was observed for FeMn(4:1). Mn-promoted catalysts generally exhibited lower CH₄ selectivity than monometallic Fe, with a volcano-type dependence on the Fe/Mn ratio.
- **Observations and Trends:** Mn addition inhibits secondary hydrogenation, reducing CH₄ formation and favoring higher carbon number products.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity quantifies the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  S_{C_3^+}(\%) = \frac{Q_{\text{out}} v_{C_3^+} C_{C_3^+}}{Q_{\text{in}} v_{\text{CO}} X_{\text{CO}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Mn addition increases the selectivity for higher carbon number products, with FeMn(4:1) showing the highest selectivity for C₂–C₄ olefins (60.6%, CO₂-free basis).
- **Observations and Trends:** Both C₂–C₄ olefin and CH₄ selectivities exhibit a volcano-type trend, peaking at FeMn(4:1), while excessive Mn content leads to a decline in C₃⁺ selectivity due to loss of active carbide phases.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory (ASF) distribution:
  \[
  \ln\left(\frac{W_n}{n}\right) = n \ln \alpha + \ln\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{\alpha}\right)
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the mass fraction of hydrocarbons with \( n \) carbon atoms.
- **Experimental Data:** The article confirms the use of ASF fitting for α determination, but specific α values are not explicitly reported.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal Mn content enhances chain growth probability, correlating with increased selectivity for longer-chain olefins.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Time-on-stream studies reveal that FeMn(4:1) maintains superior stability, while higher Mn content accelerates deactivation due to the formation of inactive MnCO₃, which blocks access to active iron carbides.
- **Observations and Trends:** Mn promotes catalyst stability at optimal levels, but excessive Mn leads to rapid deactivation and loss of activity.
