## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe₃O₄@MnO₂ catalysts is achieved using a one‑pot hydrothermal synthesis that ensures precise control over dopant levels, from Mn‑free to Mn‑50. The method employs a sequence of well‐defined steps to guarantee reproducibility and clear catalyst formation.

#### • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
1. Dissolve 13.9 g of FeSO₄·7H₂O and 10.0 g of poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP) in 1 L of water.  
2. Transfer the solution into a stainless steel autoclave and heat the mixture at 90 °C for 1 hour.  
3. Add 40 mL of NaOH to the heated solution; observe the formation of a green suspension indicative of an initial pH adjustment and nucleation of Fe₃O₄.  

#### • Precipitation–Deposition  
4. Following the formation of the green suspension, continue stirring for 4 hours until the suspension turns black, confirming the formation of iron oxide nanoparticles.  
5. Introduce an aqueous solution of KMnO₄ dropwise to the black suspension. This step is essential as the KMnO₄ is reduced to form a dark brown precipitate of MnO₂, coating the Fe₃O₄ core. The controlled dropwise addition guarantees uniform manganese oxide deposition.

#### • Drying and Post‑processing  
6. After the dropwise addition, allow the system to age for 12 hours at the reaction conditions.  
7. Collect the resulting catalyst by centrifugation, then wash repeatedly with deionized water and ethanol.  
8. Dry the washed catalyst at ambient conditions before sieving the final product to a mesh size of 40–60.  
   
### Comparative Analysis  
While the one‑pot hydrothermal approach offers simplicity and controlled Mn doping, it emphasizes reproducibility through predefined reagent concentrations and exact temperature and stirring conditions. Advantages include a uniform core–shell architecture and scalability, whereas the reliance on precise temperature control and dropwise addition of KMnO₄ may increase synthesis complexity compared to other methods.  

Each step has been optimized to capture observable changes, such as the transition of the suspension from green to black and then to dark brown upon KMnO₄ addition, ensuring all numerical values (e.g., 90 °C, 1 h, 4 h, and 12 h) are accurately maintained for reproducibility.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₃O₄@MnO₂ (Mn‑free, Mn‑10, Mn‑18, Mn‑33, Mn‑50)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 13.9 g FeSO₄·7H₂O  
  • 10.0 g PVP  
  • 40 mL NaOH  
  • Deionized water (1 L)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Temperature: 90 °C  
  • Stirring: Constant (ensuring full dissolution)  
  • Duration: 1 hour for dissolution, then 4 hours post pH adjustment  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve 13.9 g FeSO₄·7H₂O and 10.0 g PVP in 1 L deionized water at 90 °C. PVP plays a key role as a surfactant to limit Fe₃O₄ particle aggregation.  
  2. Once a homogeneous solution is achieved, slowly add 40 mL NaOH. The addition adjusts the pH and forms a green ferrous hydroxide suspension.  
  3. Maintain stirring at 90 °C for 4 hours; observe the color transition from green to black, indicating initial Fe₃O₄ formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Aqueous solution of KMnO₄ (concentration varies to achieve Mn‑free through Mn‑50 dopant levels)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Temperature: Continued at 90 °C  
  • Addition: KMnO₄ added dropwise using a calibrated dropper to ensure slow, controlled introduction  
- **Process**:  
  1. Introduce the KMnO₄ solution dropwise to the black suspension. The dropwise addition ensures uniform MnO₂ coating formation on preformed Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles.  
  2. Continue stirring at 90 °C and hold the reaction for 1 hour to ensure complete reduction of KMnO₄, with the Mn dopant level tailored by the KMnO₄ concentration.

##### Step 3: Purification  
- **Process**:  
  1. Collect the resultant suspension via centrifugation.  
  2. Wash several times with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual ions and impurities, ensuring optimal purity.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry the washed catalyst using conventional laboratory drying equipment until moisture is removed (conditions to be optimized based on equipment).  
- **Calcination**:  
  • Although specific calcination parameters are not reported, this step may involve a controlled heat treatment if necessary.

##### Step 5: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • In situ reduction under H₂ at 350 °C, using a flow rate of 26 mL·min⁻¹ for 10 hours.  

**Key Steps & Cautions**:  
• Strict temperature and stirring protocols are essential during both precursor dissolution and pH adjustment.  
• The dropwise addition of KMnO₄ must be carefully controlled to achieve a uniform MnO₂ coat.  
• Ensure reproducibility by documenting exact reagent quantities and timing at each stage.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction involves an in situ hydrogen pre‑reduction step aimed at converting iron oxide precursors into active metallic sites while optimizing the electronic interaction with the MnO₂ promoter.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for promoting the reduction of iron oxides (primarily Fe₃O₄) to form catalytically active sites and to facilitate electron transfer from the MnO₂ shell.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 350 °C.
- **Observations:** At this temperature, effective in situ reduction is achieved; higher Mn loading enhances the electron transfer from MnO₂ to Fe₃O₄, thereby improving the formation of active sites. The chosen temperature is sufficiently high to ensure reduction without inducing excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process ensures that the reduction is complete and uniform throughout the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The hydrogen pre‑reduction is maintained for 10 hours.
- **Observations:** A 10‑hour activation time has been found adequate to achieve full reduction of the iron oxide precursors without leading to significant deactivation processes such as sintering.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure governs the reaction kinetics and maintains the proper environment for effective hydrogen reduction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The process is conducted at 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** The mild pressure setting supports sufficient hydrogen exposure for reduction while preserving the structural integrity of the catalyst.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Gas and Flow Rate
- **Definition:** The nature and flow rate of the reducing gas play essential roles in controlling the reduction extent and ensuring uniform catalyst activation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen (H₂) is employed as the reducing agent with a flow rate of 26 mL/min. Approximately 0.5 g of catalyst, diluted with an equal mass of quartz sand, is used to promote even gas distribution.
- **Observations:** The controlled flow rate ensures thorough contact between hydrogen and the catalyst particles, leading to an efficient reduction process that ultimately enhances Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is critical for activating syngas and influencing the catalyst performance. In this study, Fischer-Tropsch catalytic tests were performed over a temperature range of 280–360 °C. Under optimal conditions for the Mn‑50 catalyst, the reaction temperature was set at 320 °C. At this temperature, CO conversion increased steadily, and experiments indicated an optimal balance between overall conversion and selectivity for linear α‑olefins, with catalyst performance remaining stable over prolonged runs (up to 100 hours).

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure directly affects the concentration of reactants and, consequently, the hydrocarbon formation process. Experiments were conducted within a pressure range of 1.0–4.0 MPa. The optimal pressure for the Mn‑50 catalyst was determined to be 2.0 MPa. At this pressure, the catalyst achieved a favorable balance; CO conversion improved significantly while the selectivity for olefins, and specifically linear α‑olefins, reached its maximum levels. These results are indicative of the positive impact of controlled pressure adjustments on catalytic activity.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The syngas feed employed in the Fischer-Tropsch process maintained a stoichiometric H₂/CO ratio of 1:1. This ratio was chosen to provide an appropriate balance for both hydrocarbon chain growth and hydrogenation reactions. Although the article does not detail the mechanistic role of this ratio extensively, the fixed ratio contributed to ensuring consistent performance and product distribution during the reactions.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is defined as the volumetric flow rate of syngas per unit catalyst mass and is a key parameter in controlling reactant exposure time. The study investigated GHSV values in the range of 3000–12,000 h⁻¹, with the optimal catalytic performance recorded at 3000 h⁻¹. An increase in GHSV to around 6000 h⁻¹ was observed to boost CO conversion momentarily; however, further increases led to lower conversion and shifted the product distribution toward lighter hydrocarbons, thereby affecting the selectivity towards longer-chain olefins.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to determine the crystal structure and phase composition of the Fe₃O₄@MnO₂ catalysts. This analysis is crucial for identifying the presence of active phases and understanding the structural evolution with Mn doping.
- **Findings:** The XRD patterns of all samples display characteristic peaks at 2θ values of 30.0°, 35.4°, 43.0°, 53.4°, 57.0°, and 62.5°, corresponding to the face-centered cubic Fe₃O₄ phase (JCPDS 19-0629), confirming the retention of the Fe₃O₄ core structure. With increasing Mn content, a new peak emerges at 2θ ≈ 40.5°, attributed to amorphous MnO₂. No diffraction peaks corresponding to an Fe/Mn solid solution are observed.
- **Observations:** The absence of Fe/Mn solid solution indicates that MnO₂ forms a shell over the Fe₃O₄ core rather than integrating into the lattice, which preserves the reducibility and dispersion of iron species.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to examine particle morphology and size, which influence catalyst dispersion and surface accessibility.
- **Findings:** The Mn-free catalyst exhibits spherical nanospheres with an average size of ~26 nm (TEM), and a crystallite size of 40.7 nm (Scherrer equation, XRD). As Mn content increases, particles transition to flattened, plate-shaped nanostructures with rough surfaces. For Mn-50, TEM reveals plate-shaped architectures with an average edge length of 66 nm and thickness of 5 nm. Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping confirms a Mn-rich shell.
- **Observations:** The morphological evolution from spheres to nanoplates with higher Mn loading enhances surface area and exposes more active sites.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption–desorption (BET) analysis quantified surface area, pore volume, and pore size, which are critical for reactant diffusion and catalyst efficiency.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area increases markedly from 13.45 m²/g (Mn-free) to 191.59 m²/g (Mn-50). Pore volume rises from 0.06 cm³/g to 0.73 cm³/g, while average pore size decreases from 20.78 nm to 12.47 nm with higher Mn content.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface area and mesoporosity with Mn doping facilitate improved dispersion and diffusion, supporting higher catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Composition and Electronic Structure
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) assessed surface elemental composition and electronic interactions between MnO₂ and Fe₃O₄.
- **Findings:** Increasing Mn loading leads to a higher Mn/Fe ratio at the surface and intensified MnO₂ signals. The Fe 2p peak shifts to lower binding energy by ~0.3 eV (from 10% to 50% Mn), while Mn 2p shifts to higher binding energy, indicating electron transfer from MnO₂ to Fe₃O₄.
- **Observations:** Enhanced electron transfer with higher Mn content is linked to improved reducibility and the formation of active iron carbide phases.

#### 6.1.5 Reducibility
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) evaluated the reducibility of iron and manganese species, which affects the formation of active sites.
- **Findings:** The Mn-free catalyst shows two main reduction peaks at 350–450 °C (Fe₃O₄ to FeO) and 600–800 °C (FeO to Fe). Mn-doped samples exhibit an additional peak at 400–600 °C (MnO₂ reduction) and overall reduction peaks shift to lower temperatures.
- **Observations:** The MnO₂ shell facilitates easier reduction of both Mn and Fe species, promoting the generation of active phases for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.6 Formation of Active Iron Carbides
- **Description:** The presence of iron carbides (FeCx), the active phase for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, was confirmed by XRD and supported by XPS and Raman spectroscopy of spent catalysts.
- **Findings:** After reaction, a broad XRD peak at 2θ ≈ 43° appears, corresponding to FeCx. Higher Mn content promotes the formation of these carbides.
- **Observations:** The increased formation of FeCx with Mn doping correlates with enhanced catalytic performance and selectivity for linear α-olefins.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under optimized conditions (320 °C, 2.0 MPa, 3000 h⁻¹, H₂ pre-reduction at 350 °C for 10 h), the Mn-50 catalyst achieves a CO conversion of 75.47%. Across the catalyst series, CO conversion decreases with increasing Mn content: Mn-free (87.64%), Mn-10 (80.12%), Mn-18 (76.45%), Mn-33 (72.31%), and Mn-50 (67.90%) under standard conditions (280 °C, 20 MPa, 3000 h⁻¹).
- **Observations and Trends:** While higher Mn loading slightly reduces CO conversion, it enhances selectivity for desired products. The Mn-50 catalyst maintains high conversion with improved product distribution.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Mn-50, CO₂ selectivity is 47.11% under optimal conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity remains high (~47%) across all Mn levels, attributed to the inherent water–gas shift activity of Fe-based catalysts. Mn doping does not significantly alter this parameter.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn-50 catalyst exhibits a CH₄ selectivity of 3.58%. CH₄ selectivity decreases with increasing Mn: Mn-free (15.06%), Mn-10 (10.12%), Mn-18 (7.45%), Mn-33 (5.24%), Mn-50 (3.58%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Mn content effectively suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms. It is calculated on a carbon basis, excluding CO₂.
- **Experimental Data:** For Mn-50, C₄⁺ alkene selectivity reaches 65.81% under standard conditions and 64.95% under optimal conditions. The proportion of linear α-olefins (LAOs) in C₄⁺ alkenes is 87.10% (standard) and 91.04% (optimal).
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher Mn loading significantly increases C₄⁺ and LAO selectivity, with Mn-50 outperforming lower Mn-content catalysts.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, influencing product distribution. Higher α values favor long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn-50 catalyst demonstrates an increased α factor compared to Mn-free and lower Mn-content catalysts, correlating with enhanced C₄⁺ and LAO selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The rise in α with Mn doping shifts the product slate toward heavier, more valuable olefins.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over extended operation.
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn-50 catalyst maintains stable CO conversion (~50%) and high C₄⁺/LAO selectivity over a 100-hour test.
- **Observations and Trends:** Minimal performance loss is observed, indicating excellent long-term stability and low deactivation under reaction conditions.

Collectively, these metrics demonstrate that the Fe₃O₄@MnO₂ catalyst with high Mn loading (Mn-50) achieves a favorable balance of high CO conversion, suppressed methane formation, and exceptional selectivity for long-chain linear α-olefins, with robust operational stability.
