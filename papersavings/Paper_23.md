## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis of the Raney‐Fe catalyst via a chemical leaching process followed by reduction and carburization. The method leverages selective leaching of an Al–Fe (50/50) alloy to produce a porous Fe structure that converts in situ to active Fe carbide phases.

#### • Chemical Leaching and Precursor Preparation

1. Catalyst precursor preparation begins with the chemical leaching of an Al–Fe alloy.
   - • Reagent: 9 M KOH solution.
   - • Equipment: Sealable reactor with integrated temperature control.
   - • Conditions: Leaching performed at 70 °C for 40 minutes.
   - • Observations: Dissolution of aluminum leads to a visible color change and precipitate formation, yielding a porous Fe powder.

2. Post‐leaching, the porous Fe material is passivated for safe handling prior to further processing.

#### • Reduction Step

3. The passivated catalyst undergoes reduction to convert residual surface oxides to metallic Fe.
   - • Conditions: Reduction is conducted at 430 °C for 1 hour.
   - • Gas Composition: 20% H₂ in Ar.
   - • Flow and Pressure: Flow rate of 50 mL/min at 1.5 bar.
   - • Outcome: Complete conversion of surface oxides facilitates a reproducible metallic Fe surface, essential for subsequent carburization.

#### • Carburization Procedure

4. The reduced catalyst is subjected to an in situ carburization step.
   - • Conditions: Carburization at 250 °C.
   - • Gas Composition: A synthesis gas mixture comprising 8% CO and 16% H₂ in Ar.
   - • Pressure and Flow: Maintained at 1.5 bar with a flow rate around 50 mL/min.
   - • Isotopic Labeling: Carburization experiments include isotope variants (12CO/H₂ and 13CO/H₂) for durations (e.g., 1 h, 2 h, and 6 h) to monitor carbide formation (e.g., ε‐Fe₂C, ε′‐Fe₂.₂C, χ‐Fe₅C₂).
   - • Observations: Progressive development of Fe carbide phases is recorded via temperature‐programmed hydrogenation and Mössbauer spectroscopy.

### Comparative Analysis

This method contrasts with traditional co‑precipitation or impregnation techniques by offering a streamlined synthesis route with precise control over reagent concentrations, process temperatures, and gas compositions. The chemical leaching process ensures minimal interference from oxidic phases while being cost‑effective and scalable, as evidenced by the detailed monitoring of process parameters.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Raney‐Fe Catalyst
- **Al–Fe Alloy Powder (Al50/Fe50)**: Weighed 5 g using an analytical balance to ensure precise measurement of the precursor. This solid reagent forms the basis for the subsequent leaching process.
  
#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Raney‐Fe Catalyst
- **9 M KOH Solution**: Poured 25 mL into the reaction vessel using a graduated cylinder. This liquid reagent is critical for the chemical leaching process that removes aluminum from the alloy.
  
#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Raney‐Fe Catalyst
- **Leaching Mixture**: Mixed at 70 °C for 40 minutes using a magnetic stirrer to ensure thorough contact between the Al–Fe alloy powder and the KOH solution. The elevated temperature facilitates the selective dissolution of aluminum while maintaining the integrity of the iron-rich matrix.
  
In this procedure, solid and liquid reagents are handled sequentially—first, the Al–Fe alloy powder is precisely weighed, then the measured 9 M KOH solution is accurately transferred into the reaction vessel. Mixing commences immediately under controlled thermal conditions. Although no stirring speed in rpm is provided, the duration and temperature are strictly maintained to promote complete leaching and reproducibility across batches. Reagent homogeneity is achieved through consistent stirring, ensuring that the porous Raney‐Fe formed after the selective removal of aluminum is uniformly produced. These detailed steps support the full reproducibility of the synthesis process, aligning with the experimental workflow for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst preparation as outlined in the study.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Raney‐Fe Catalyst

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation – Aluminum–Iron Alloy Leaching
- **Reagents**:  
  • 5 g of Al50/Fe50 alloy powder  
  • 25 mL of 9 M KOH solution  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Temperature: 70 °C  
  • Duration: 40 minutes  
  • Stirring: Continuous agitation to ensure uniform leaching  
- **Process**:  
  • Slowly introduce the alloy powder into the KOH solution while stirring.  
  • Maintain 70 °C for 40 minutes to selectively dissolve aluminum.  
  • Following leaching, perform seven sequential washes with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual potassium and dissolved aluminum ions.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensure complete removal of aluminum to form a porous Raney‐Fe precursor essential for high surface area and reactivity.

##### Step [2]: Passivation and Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents & Conditions**:  
  • Passivate fresh Raney‐Fe in a 1% O₂/He gas mixture at room temperature for 24 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  • This step stabilizes the pyrophoric material and prepares it for further reduction without interference from residual oxidic phases.

##### Step [3]: Reduction
- **Reagents**:  
  • 20% H₂ in Ar gas mixture  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Temperature: 430 °C  
  • Flow Rate: 50 mL/min  
  • Pressure: 1.5 bar  
  • Duration: 1 hour  
- **Process**:  
  • Reduce the passivated Raney‐Fe to convert surface oxides into metallic Fe, ensuring an entirely reduced surface for efficient carburization.

##### Step [4]: Carburization & Isotopic Labeling
- **Reagents**:  
  • Synthesis gas comprising 8% CO and 16% H₂ in Ar  
  • Optionally, isotopic mixtures: either 12CO/H₂ or 13CO/H₂  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Temperature: 250 °C  
  • Flow Rate: 50 mL/min  
  • Pressure: 1.5 bar  
  • Duration: Variable (e.g., 1 h, 2 h, 6 h) to study kinetic behavior  
- **Process**:  
  • Expose the reduced catalyst to the synthesis gas, initiating carburization to form Fe carbide phases (ε‐Fe₂C, ε′‐Fe₂.₂C, χ‐Fe₅C₂).  
  • For isotopic experiments, switch between 13CO/H₂ and 12CO/H₂ streams while keeping reactor conditions constant, ensuring clear tracking of carbon exchange dynamics.  
- **Key Steps & Cautions**:  
  • Monitor carbon uptake rates to balance the formation of carbide phases.  
  • Maintain reactor parameters precisely to ensure reproducibility and avoid re-oxidation of the catalyst.  

This structured workflow ensures that every key synthesis step—from raw precursor preparation to final carburization—is clearly documented and reproducible.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 Temperature‑Programmed Hydrogenation (TPH)
- **Operational Description**: TPH is applied to obtain CH₄ evolution profiles and determine the C/Fe ratio by sequential hydrogenation of surface versus bulk carbon species.
- **Instruments and Models**: The reactor is coupled with an online mass spectrometer (MS) and gas chromatograph (GC) for real‑time monitoring.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Prior to TPH, samples are flushed with Ar and cooled to room temperature. The reactor is then heated to 630 °C at a ramp rate of 5 °C/min in a diluted flow (20% H₂ in Ar, 50 mL/min, 1.5 bar).
- **Test Parameters**: Temperature ramp rate, gas flow, and periodic GC sampling are used to calibrate the MS signal.
- **Key Notes**: This procedure distinguishes carbon species by correlating the temperature ranges with specific hydrogenation events.

### 4.2 Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: This method elucidates the Fe carbide phase composition and overall C/Fe ratio via spectral deconvolution.
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements are carried out using a sinusoidal velocity spectrometer with a high‑pressure in‑situ cell featuring beryllium windows; an α‑Fe foil is used for velocity calibration.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Spectra are recorded at −153 °C, with both source and sample maintained at identical temperatures.
- **Test Parameters**: Calibration and temperature control are critical for resolving different Fe species.
- **Key Notes**: Samples are pre‐treated by passivation and reduction at 430 °C in H₂ for 1 hour before analysis.

### 4.3 CO Chemisorption
- **Operational Description**: This technique quantifies the number of accessible active Fe sites by monitoring CO adsorption.
- **Instruments and Models**: The CO chemisorption setup uses a fixed‑bed reactor equipped with precise gas-flow controllers.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are first pre‑treated at 250 °C in H₂ for 10 minutes; measurements are performed at 30 °C with a CO flow of 50 mL/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Gas composition and flow rate are strictly maintained.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading is essential for reproducible active site determination.

### 4.4 Ar Physisorption (BET Surface Area Analysis)
- **Operational Description**: BET analysis determines specific surface areas (~30 m²/g) following catalyst reduction and carburization.
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements are conducted using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are reduced at 430 °C in H₂, carburized at 250 °C in H₂/CO, and transferred to the analysis tube in a glovebox.
- **Test Parameters**: Consistent sample preparation ensures repeatability.
- **Key Notes**: Strict control of pretreatment conditions enhances data reliability.

### 4.5 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
- **Operational Description**: TEM provides particle morphology and average size (29 ± 2 nm) of the passivated Raney‑Fe catalyst.
- **Instruments and Models**: Analysis is performed on an FEI Tecnai 20 microscope.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Standard mounting and imaging protocols ensure accurate size determination.
- **Test Parameters**: Average particle size is computed from representative micrographs.
- **Key Notes**: Essential for assessing nanoparticle dispersion and morphology.

### 4.6 Online Gas Chromatography/Mass Spectrometry (GC/MS)
- **Operational Description**: GC/MS continuously monitors gas‑phase reaction products and isotopic compositions during catalytic testing.
- **Instruments and Models**: The GC system (Thermo Fischer Scientific Trace GC 1300, equipped with MXT‑QBond and Rt‑SilicaBond columns) is coupled with an MS calibrated against GC sampling.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements are conducted at 50 mL/min flow and 1.5 bar.
- **Test Parameters**: Detector settings and calibration routines ensure quantitative analysis.
- **Key Notes**: This integrated setup permits precise monitoring of product evolution and reaction pathways.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation protocol is divided into two sequential steps: a reduction phase to generate a fully metallic iron surface and a subsequent in situ carburization to form active carbide phases.

#### 5.1.1 Reduction Phase
The reduction step is performed at 430 °C, which is critical for ensuring the complete conversion of the precursor’s surface oxides to metallic Fe. The catalyst is exposed to a controlled gas mixture of 20% H₂ in Ar at an operating pressure of 1.5 bar. With a gas flow rate of 50 mL/min, the reduction is maintained for 1 hour. These conditions, well above the identified reduction feature around 390 °C, guarantee effective hydrogenation while preventing sintering. The established metallic surface ensures homogeneous dispersion of Fe, a prerequisite for subsequent carburization. The fixed parameters implicitly control the space-time velocity and ensure uniform gas exposure across the catalyst bed.

#### 5.1.2 Carburization Phase
Following reduction, carburization is conducted at a significantly lower temperature of 250 °C under the same pressure of 1.5 bar. This phase employs a synthesis gas mixture composed of 8% CO and 16% H₂ in Ar, again with a gas flow rate of 50 mL/min. Carburization times are varied—typically 1, 2, or 6 hours—to finely tune the C/Fe ratio. For example, a 1-hour treatment results in a C/Fe ratio of approximately 0.37, approaching 88% of the final desired value, while extended durations promote full development of the carbide structure (final ratio near 0.42). The lower carburization temperature is chosen to favor controlled carbon incorporation without disturbing the metallic state established during reduction. This staged activation is vital for in situ formation of diverse Fe carbide phases (such as ε‐Fe₂C and χ‐Fe₅C₂), which are responsible for the catalyst’s performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is a critical parameter in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, directly influencing the activation of synthesis gas and the formation of active carbide phases.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction temperature was maintained at 250 °C during both the carburization and Fischer–Tropsch reaction phases.  
- **Observations:** At 250 °C, the catalyst facilitates effective carburization while promoting the activation of synthesis gas. However, this temperature also supports a slow, continuous involvement of carbon atoms from the carbide lattice via a Mars–Van Krevelen mechanism, contributing to gradual catalyst deactivation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** The reaction pressure governs the concentration of synthesis gas within the reactor, thereby affecting product distribution and catalyst performance.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Reactions were conducted at a pressure of 1.5 bar, ensuring a stable environment for both carburization and the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Observations:** Maintaining 1.5 bar helps sustain a higher concentration of reactive gases, which is essential for achieving consistent catalytic behavior without significant fluctuations in product selectivity.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio, reflecting the relative proportions of hydrogen to carbon monoxide, is critical for determining hydrocarbon formation pathways and selectivity.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The synthesis gas was composed of 16% H₂ and 8% CO in Ar, corresponding to an H₂/CO ratio of 2:1 during the reaction.  
- **Observations:** This gas composition is pivotal; a higher hydrogen content tends to favor methane production, while lower ratios could promote olefin formation. The specific ratio employed was chosen to balance these effects and ensure effective catalyst performance.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity describes the ratio of the gas flow rate to the catalyst mass, indicating reactant residence time.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** While the flow rate was fixed at 50 mL/min, no explicit space-time velocity value was reported in the article.  
- **Observations:** The constant flow rate, in conjunction with the controlled pressure and temperature, ensures that the reaction conditions are reproducible, allowing for precise kinetic measurements and effective monitoring of catalyst behavior during FT synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structure
- **Description:** The identification and quantification of Fe carbide phases were performed using Mössbauer spectroscopy, with spectra recorded at −153 °C in an in situ high-pressure cell. This technique distinguishes between various Fe species based on their hyperfine parameters, enabling precise determination of phase composition and the C/Fe ratio.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra revealed that the Raney-Fe catalyst, after in situ carburization, predominantly consists of Fe carbide phases such as χ‐Fe₅C₂, ε‐Fe₂C, and ε′‐Fe₂.₂C, with negligible oxidic Fe. The transformation from metallic Fe to these carbide phases is complete under the applied synthesis gas conditions.
- **Observations:** The excellent agreement between the C/Fe ratios determined by Mössbauer spectroscopy and temperature-programmed hydrogenation (TPH) confirms the reliability of phase identification and quantification. The presence of χ‐Fe₅C₂ is particularly significant, as it is associated with high Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.2 Carbon Content and Distribution (Surface vs. Bulk)
- **Description:** TPH was employed to quantify the total carbon content and to differentiate between surface and bulk carbon species by monitoring CH₄ evolution during hydrogenation. Isotopic labeling (¹²CO/¹³CO) enabled further separation of carbon sources.
- **Findings:** TPH profiles display distinct features: a low-temperature peak corresponding to surface carbon species and a higher-temperature feature (380–480 °C) attributed to bulk (lattice) carbon. The absence of a peak above 500 °C indicates minimal graphitic carbon. The C/Fe ratio increases rapidly during initial carburization and stabilizes as equilibrium is reached, with close correspondence between TPH and Mössbauer-derived values.
- **Observations:** Isotopic TPH experiments demonstrate that surface carbon exchanges rapidly with the gas phase, while bulk carbon diffuses more slowly, supporting the coexistence of fast Langmuir–Hinshelwood and slow Mars–van Krevelen mechanisms in FT synthesis.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Particle Size
- **Description:** The specific surface area was measured by Ar physisorption (Micromeritics ASAP 2020), and particle size was determined by TEM (FEI Tecnai 20). CO chemisorption quantified the number of accessible active Fe sites.
- **Findings:** The passivated Raney-Fe catalyst exhibits a surface area of 30 ± 6 m²/g, which remains stable after reduction and carburization (31 ± 5 and 29 ± 5 m²/g, respectively), indicating structural robustness. TEM analysis shows a volume-weighted average particle size of 29 ± 2 nm. CO chemisorption measurements reveal that approximately 12% of surface Fe sites are accessible for CO adsorption post-carburization.
- **Observations:** The high and stable surface area, along with uniform nanoscale particle size, ensures a large number of active sites and efficient reactant diffusion, both critical for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Purity
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) was used to confirm the removal of Al from the precursor and to verify the Fe content.
- **Findings:** The leaching process effectively removes Al, resulting in a catalyst composed almost entirely of Fe, as required for the formation of pure Fe carbide phases.
- **Observations:** The high purity of the Raney-Fe catalyst ensures that the observed catalytic and structural properties are intrinsic to Fe and its carbides, without interference from residual alloying elements.

#### 6.1.5 Gas-Phase Product and Isotopic Analysis
- **Description:** Online gas chromatography (GC) and mass spectrometry (MS) were used to monitor gas-phase reaction products and isotopic compositions during both characterization and catalytic testing.
- **Findings:** GC quantified CO, CO₂, H₂, and hydrocarbon products, while MS enabled real-time tracking of isotopically labeled CH₄ (¹²CH₄/¹³CH₄), providing direct evidence for carbon exchange dynamics and mechanistic pathways.
- **Observations:** The integration of isotopic labeling with online analysis unambiguously distinguishes between carbon originating from the carbide lattice and that from surface-adsorbed species, validating mechanistic interpretations of FT activity.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO molecules transformed by the catalyst relative to the CO supplied, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet CO flow rates, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** CO conversion was continuously monitored using online gas chromatography (GC) under standard Fischer–Tropsch (FT) conditions: 250 °C, 1.5 bar, 8% CO and 16% H₂ in Ar, total flow 50 mL/min. The catalyst exhibited rapid activation, with conversion stabilizing after complete carburization (C/Fe ≈ 0.42 after 6 h). The CO conversion remained steady during extended operation, indicating robust performance.
- **Observations and Trends:** The initial increase in CO conversion correlated with the rapid uptake of carbon into the Fe lattice. Once the carbide phase was fully established, conversion rates plateaued, reflecting the attainment of a stable, active phase. The fraction of accessible Fe sites (≈12%) matched the proportion of CO conversion attributed to the fast Langmuir–Hinshelwood (L–H) pathway.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity was determined from GC analysis of the reactor effluent. Under steady-state FT conditions, CO₂ was a minor product, with selectivity values consistently reported on a carbon basis.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low CO₂ selectivity indicated minimal water–gas shift activity, consistent with the absence of oxidic Fe phases and the dominance of carbide phases in the catalyst.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, defined as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ was the principal hydrocarbon product during temperature-programmed hydrogenation (TPH), accounting for over 96% of evolved hydrocarbons. Selectivity was quantified using GC and online mass spectrometry (MS), with calibration against known standards.
- **Observations and Trends:** The CH₄ formation rate and selectivity were highest during initial carburization and decreased as the carbide phase stabilized, reflecting a shift toward longer-chain hydrocarbon formation as the catalyst matured.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** C₃⁺ selectivity was determined by GC-FID analysis of the effluent. As carburization progressed, the selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons increased, particularly after the C/Fe ratio exceeded 0.3.
- **Observations and Trends:** The increase in C₃⁺ selectivity with carburization time indicated enhanced chain growth capability as the carbide phase became more stable.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, quantifies the probability of chain growth:
\[
\alpha = \text{slope of } \log(W_n/n) \text{ vs. } n
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** α was calculated from the hydrocarbon product distribution measured by GC-FID. The α value increased with carburization time, reflecting a higher probability of chain growth as the catalyst transitioned from initial to fully carburized states.
- **Observations and Trends:** The stabilization of the α factor at higher carburization levels corresponded to increased selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons, consistent with the formation of stable ε(′)-carbide and χ-Fe₅C₂ phases.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is defined as the decline in CO conversion per unit time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO conversion was tracked over time using online GC. After complete carburization, the catalyst exhibited negligible deactivation, with conversion remaining stable during extended FT operation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The reproducibility and stability of the catalyst were confirmed by the convergence of carbon entering and leaving rates and the plateauing of the C/Fe ratio, indicating robust long-term performance under the tested conditions.
