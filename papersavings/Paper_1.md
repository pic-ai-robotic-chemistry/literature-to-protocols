## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis follows a structured sequence to ensure reproducibility and precise control over the active phases. The synthesis methods for Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic) and Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic) catalysts are based on a hydrothermal preparation of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles, deposition–precipitation of the Mn promoter, and subsequent surface modifications.

#### Hydrothermal Synthesis and Calcination of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   – Dissolve 7.5 g of FeCl₃·6H₂O in 110 mL of water.  
   – Add 12.4 g of sodium acetate trihydrate and 3.8 g of hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide; stir for 0.5 h.  
   – Introduce 26 mL of ethylenediamine and stir for an additional 1 h.
2. • Hydrothermal Treatment  
   – Transfer the mixture to a hydrothermal reactor and treat at 200 °C for 10 h.
3. • Washing and Drying  
   – Wash the product with water and ethanol, then dry at 100 °C for 12 h.
4. • Calcination  
   – Calcine the dried material at 500 °C for 5 h to enhance crystallinity and form stable Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.

#### Deposition–Precipitation and Promoter Incorporation
1. • Precipitation–Deposition (for Mn promoter)  
   – Disperse the synthesized Fe₂O₃ in ethanol; add 5 mL of an aqueous solution containing 0.078 g of KMnO₄.  
   – Stir the mixture at 30 °C for 2 h (with ultrasonication as needed) to promote uniform Mn deposition on the Fe₂O₃ surface.
   
#### SiO₂ Coating and Surface Modifications
1. • Hydrophilic Coating via Modified Stöber Method  
   – In an ethanol and ammonia solution at 30 °C, add tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) in volumes of 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL to control SiO₂ shell thickness.
2. • Hydrophobic Modification (for Fe@Mn@xSi-c)  
   – Treat the SiO₂-coated catalyst with chlorotrimethylsilane in n‑hexane under ultrasonic conditions at room temperature for 1 h.  
   – Follow with centrifugation and vacuum drying at 80 °C for 6 h.

Each step is carefully optimized to control the Fe carbide active phases during subsequent reduction–carburization and to prevent oxidation or chlorine poisoning, ensuring a reproducible catalyst synthesis for Fischer–Tropsch applications.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic)
- **Fe₂O₃**: Weighed 0.5g using an analytical balance to ensure precise loading of the iron precursor.
- **KMnO₄**: Weighed 0.078g to serve as the manganese promoter in the deposition–precipitation process.

##### 3.1.1.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic)
- **Fe₂O₃**: Weighed 0.5g using an analytical balance; the same iron precursor is used as for the hydrophilic catalyst.
- **KMnO₄**: Weighed 0.078g, maintaining consistency between the catalyst types prior to hydrophobic modification.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic)
- **Ethanol**: Poured 150 mL into the reaction vessel containing the pre-weighed precursors to facilitate dispersion.
- **Tetraethyl Orthosilicate (TEOS)**: Transferred x mL (where x = 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8) into the mixture, thereby allowing controlled formation of the SiO₂ shell.
- **KMnO₄ Aqueous Solution**: Added dropwise using a peristaltic pump to ensure a gradual and uniform introduction of the manganese promoter.

##### 3.1.2.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic)
- **Ethanol**: Poured 70 mL into the cooled catalyst vessel post vacuum oven treatment, preparing the material for surface modification.
- **Chlorotrimethylsilane**: Transferred 0.5 mL directly into the reaction mixture to initiate hydrophobic modification of the SiO₂-coated catalyst.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic)
- **Catalyst Precursor Solution**: Stirred at 30 °C for 3 hours after addition of TEOS and ammonia solution, followed by a further 4-hour stirring period to promote homogeneity and effective SiO₂ coating.
- **Mn Promoter Dispersion**: Mixed at 30 °C for 2 hours during the deposition–precipitation step to ensure uniform distribution of the manganese species.

##### 3.1.3.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic)
- **Surface Modification Mixture**: First stirred to uniformly integrate the reagents, then subjected to ultrasonic treatment at room temperature for 1 hour following the addition of chlorotrimethylsilane, thereby achieving a consistent hydrophobic surface while preventing residual chlorine contamination.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 12.4 g sodium acetate trihydrate, 3.8 g hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide; dissolved in a specified volume of deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Hydrothermal treatment at elevated temperatures, followed by calcination at 500 °C for 5 hours in air; drying at 100 °C.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the reagents to form a homogeneous solution.
  - Conduct hydrothermal synthesis to form Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.
  - Calcine under prescribed conditions to ensure phase transformation.
- **Key Steps**: Accurate reagent weighing and monitored temperature ramps are crucial to prevent formation of undesired phases.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly – Mn Promoter Deposition
- **Reagents**: 0.5 g preformed Fe₂O₃, 5 mL aqueous solution with 0.078 g KMnO₄, and 300 mL ethanol.
- **Conditions**: Stirring at 30 °C for 2 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Disperse Fe₂O₃ in ethanol.
  - Add the KMnO₄ solution dropwise while stirring to ensure uniform deposition.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain constant stirring and temperature for uniform promoter incorporation.

##### Step 3: SiO₂ Coating via Modified Stöber Method
- **Reagents**: TEOS (varying amounts: 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL), 2.5 mL ammonia solution (25–28%), and 10 mL water; catalyst dispersed in 150 mL ethanol.
- **Conditions**: Reaction at 30 °C for 4 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Add TEOS to the catalyst suspension.
  - Introduce ammonia and water to facilitate hydrolysis and condensation.
- **Key Steps**: Control of TEOS dosage is critical for tailoring SiO₂ shell thickness.

##### Step 4: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Process**:
  - Purify the catalyst by centrifugation and wash with water and ethanol.
  - Dry in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for 10 hours followed by a post-treatment at 80 °C for 6 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete removal of residual reagents and moisture.

##### Step 5: Activation (Optional for Fe@Mn@xSi)
- **Process**: Reduce the dried catalyst in pure H₂ at 350 °C for 20 hours to transform phases.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic)

##### Step 1: Hydrophobic Modification
- **Reagents**: 0.5 g SiO₂-coated catalyst, 70 mL n‑hexane, 0.5 mL chlorotrimethylsilane.
- **Conditions**: Room temperature with ultrasonic treatment for 1 hour.
- **Process**:
  - Mix the catalyst with n‑hexane and add chlorotrimethylsilane.
  - Perform ultrasonic treatment to ensure thorough surface modification.
  - Purify through centrifugation, wash with n‑hexane, and dry under vacuum.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor ultrasonic exposure and subsequent washing to prevent residual chlorine poisoning.

This systematic workflow—from raw chemical precursor preparation through catalyst assembly, purification, drying, calcination, and optional activation—ensures clarity and replicability for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is carried out as a two‐stage pre‐reaction treatment to ensure the formation of the active iron carbide phase essential for effective Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Activation temperature is a critical parameter controlling reduction and carburization. Initially, the catalyst is reduced in a pure H₂ environment at 350 °C. This temperature is optimized to convert Fe₂O₃ into metallic Fe without causing significant sintering. Following reduction, a syngas activation is performed at 320 °C to carburize the metallic phase into the desired iron carbide. These sequential temperatures ensure that the catalyst's active sites are exposed while minimizing the risk of over‐heating, which could lead to particle agglomeration.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
The activation procedure is executed under a controlled pressure of 0.1 MPa during both the H₂ reduction and the subsequent syngas carburization. This low-pressure environment promotes a gradual and complete phase transformation, ensuring that the reduction kinetics remain controlled and that unwanted oxidation is minimized.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
The gas composition plays a central role in the activation strategy. For the reduction step, pure H₂ is employed to efficiently convert the iron oxide precursor. In the subsequent activation phase, a syngas mixture with an H₂/CO molar ratio of 2 is used. The initial pure H₂ atmosphere promotes full reduction, while the CO-rich syngas drives the carburization process, forming the active iron carbide phase with minimal formation of oxidized or undesired phases.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
During the syngas activation and subsequent reaction phases, the gas is introduced at a space-time velocity of 5000 mL/(g_cat·h). This controlled flow rate ensures uniform gas–catalyst interaction, contributing to reproducibility in catalyst activation and preventing over-reduction.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
The overall activation is divided into a 20-hour reduction in pure H₂, followed by a 5-hour syngas activation, totaling 25 hours. This duration is meticulously selected to ensure complete phase transformation while avoiding prolonged exposure that could result in sintering or deactivation, thereby yielding a stable and highly active catalyst for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction conditions for the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation are meticulously defined to ensure reproducibility and to elucidate the fundamental impacts on catalyst performance, particularly for the Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic) and Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic) catalysts.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
• **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical in driving the reduction and carburization processes necessary for forming the iron carbide active phases, thereby influencing the catalyst’s overall activity and stability.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests are performed at 320 °C.  
• **Observations:** At this temperature, the effective formation of the desired iron carbide phase is promoted, while the hydrophobic catalyst notably resists the oxidation of Fe carbide to Fe₃O₄, enhancing long-term catalyst stability.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
• **Definition:** The operating pressure directly influences the concentration of syngas (CO + H₂) in the reactor, which in turn affects both the conversion efficiency and product selectivity.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction is conducted at a pressure of 2.0 MPa using a stainless fixed-bed reactor.  
• **Observations:** This elevated pressure is integral to achieving effective CO conversion and directing the product distribution toward the formation of long-chain hydrocarbons, while concurrently helping to stabilize the active phase.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
• **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio controls the hydrogenation environment, critically directing the hydrocarbon chain length and influencing side reactions such as methane formation.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** A constant H₂/CO ratio of 2 is used throughout all catalytic tests.  
• **Observations:** Maintaining this ratio ensures a balanced hydrogen supply, thereby reducing methane selectivity and optimizing the hydrocarbon distribution.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
• **Definition:** The space-time velocity, expressed as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), determines the contact time between reactants and the catalyst, which is essential for maintaining robust catalytic performance.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV is set to 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹.  
• **Observations:** This high flow rate enhances mass transfer, thereby ensuring efficient reactant-catalyst interaction and contributing to sustained catalyst activity during continuous operation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The evolution of iron phases during catalyst synthesis and reaction was monitored using ex situ and in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD). In situ XRD enabled real-time tracking of phase transformations under syngas and CO + H₂O atmospheres, with particular attention to the formation of active iron carbides (χ-Fe₅C₂, θ-Fe₃C) and undesired oxidized phases (Fe₃O₄).
- **Findings:** For hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts, XRD patterns revealed that Fe₂O₃ was reduced to Fe₃O₄ at 350 °C, then to FeO at 400 °C, and subsequently carburized to Fe₅C₂ at 500 °C. In situ XRD under syngas at 320 °C showed the transformation of Fe₂O₃ to χ-Fe₅C₂ and θ-Fe₃C, with the appearance of Fe₃O₄ peaks (35.4°, 57.0°, 62.5°) indicating oxidation, especially in hydrophilic samples after exposure to CO + H₂O. Thicker SiO₂ shells, controlled by TEOS dosage, led to diminished FexC peaks and increased Fe₃O₄ content.
- **Observations:** The presence and intensity of Fe₅C₂ peaks (44.2°) correlated with higher catalytic activity, while increased Fe₃O₄ signaled deactivation. Hydrophobic modification (Fe@Mn@xSi-c) stabilized the carbide phases and suppressed Fe₃O₄ formation.

#### 6.1.2 Iron Phase Composition (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the relative proportions of iron phases after reaction, providing insight into the effects of hydrophobization and shell thickness.
- **Findings:** For Fe@Mn@0.2Si (hydrophilic), the post-reaction composition was 59.7% χ-Fe₅C₂, 34.3% Fe₃O₄, and 6.0% Fe³⁺ (spm). In contrast, Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c (hydrophobic) contained 74.1% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ-Fe₃C, with no Fe₃O₄ detected.
- **Observations:** Hydrophobic surface modification promoted the formation and retention of active carbide phases, while minimizing oxidation to Fe₃O₄.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction and Carburization Behavior (H₂-TPR and CO-TPR)
- **Description:** H₂-TPR and CO-TPR profiles assessed the reducibility and carburization of iron species, with temperature-programmed measurements indicating the accessibility of internal iron phases.
- **Findings:** As SiO₂ shell thickness increased, reduction and carburization peaks shifted to higher temperatures and decreased in area, reflecting hindered CO diffusion. In hydrophilic catalysts, Fe₂O₃ was fully reduced and carburized, while in hydrophobic, chlorine-containing samples, only slight conversion to Fe₃O₄ occurred, with no FexC formation even at 500 °C.
- **Observations:** Optimal shell thickness allowed effective reduction and carburization; excessive thickness or residual chlorine impeded active phase formation.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Elemental Distribution (TEM and EDS)
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) provided morphological and compositional analysis, confirming core–shell architecture and elemental uniformity.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed Fe@Mn nanoparticles homogeneously coated by amorphous SiO₂, with shell thickness tunable via TEOS dosage. EDS mapping verified uniform distribution of Fe, Mn, and Si. After reaction, the FeMn core in Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c shrank, indicating phase evolution.
- **Observations:** Controlled shell thickness and hydrophobic modification preserved core–shell integrity and facilitated desired phase transformations.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and CO Adsorption (FTIR/DRIFTS)
- **Description:** In situ DRIFTS characterized CO adsorption on catalyst surfaces, revealing differences in surface reactivity between hydrophilic and hydrophobic samples.
- **Findings:** On Fe@Mn@0.05Si, CO adsorption produced bands at 2172 and 2117 cm⁻¹ (gaseous CO) and 1700–1200 cm⁻¹ (carbonate species), with persistent carbonate bands indicating strong CO binding and redox activity. In Fe@Mn@0.05Si-c, only gaseous CO bands appeared, signifying poor CO adsorption due to surface chlorine.
- **Observations:** Effective hydrophobic modification (without residual chlorine) is essential for maintaining CO adsorption and active phase formation.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Hydrophobicity (Contact Angle Measurements)
- **Description:** Water-droplet contact angle measurements assessed the hydrophobicity of catalyst surfaces after chlorotrimethylsilane treatment.
- **Findings:** Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalysts exhibited increased contact angles, confirming successful surface modification.
- **Observations:** Enhanced hydrophobicity correlated with improved phase stability and resistance to water-induced oxidation during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch conditions (320 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 53.8%. The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst exhibited a lower CO conversion of 24.3%. Increasing the SiO₂ shell thickness (via higher TEOS dosage) led to a marked decrease in CO conversion, with values dropping from 53.8% (x = 0.05) to 24.3% (x = 2.8).
- **Observations and Trends:** Thicker SiO₂ shells hinder CO diffusion and carburization of internal iron species, reducing the formation of active iron carbide phases and thus lowering CO conversion. Hydrophobic modification further decreases CO conversion due to restricted water ingress and altered phase evolution.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water-gas shift side reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst showed a CO₂ selectivity of 28.9%, while the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst exhibited a significantly lower value of 7.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic surface modification suppresses water-gas shift activity, resulting in reduced CO₂ formation and improved carbon efficiency toward hydrocarbon products.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated on a carbon-atom basis:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity decreased from 21.5% for Fe@Mn@0.2Si to 14.1% for Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic shell inhibits water adsorption and hydrogenation, thereby suppressing methane formation and favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ and Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms. Olefin selectivity is the proportion of olefins among hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Data:** As SiO₂ shell thickness increased, C₃⁺ and olefin selectivity decreased (olefin selectivity dropped from 66.8% for Fe@Mn@0.05Si to 27.1% for Fe@Mn@2.8Si). Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c showed an olefin selectivity of 64.3%, compared to 49.8% for Fe@Mn@0.2Si.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic modification stabilizes iron carbide phases, enhancing C–C coupling and olefin formation, while excessive shell thickness impedes higher hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability reflects the ability to maintain performance over extended operation.
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst maintained stable CO conversion and selectivity over 110 hours of continuous reaction, with no significant deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic shell effectively prevents water-induced oxidation (Fe₃O₄ formation), preserving the active iron carbide phase and ensuring long-term catalytic stability.

*Note: The chain growth factor (α) is discussed qualitatively in relation to product distribution but is not reported with explicit numerical values in the article.*
