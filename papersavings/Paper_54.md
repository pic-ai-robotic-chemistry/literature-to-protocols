## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fe–Mn–K/SiO₂ catalysts (Mn-00, Mn-07, Mn-20) are synthesized via a coprecipitation method combined with spray drying to ensure controlled composition and reproducibility. The following detailed procedure provides step-by-step guidance extracted from the study:

#### Catalyst Synthesis via Coprecipitation and Spray Drying

1. • Precursor Mixing / Coprecipitation  
   - Prepare a solution of ferric sulfate (derived from the complete oxidation of FeSO₄•7H₂O) and manganese sulfate (MnSO₄•6H₂O) in deionized water maintained at 353 ± 1 K, ensuring the desired Fe/Mn weight ratio (100/x).  
   - Add NH₄OH continuously to adjust and maintain the pH at 9.0 ± 0.1, thereby promoting the formation of iron and manganese hydroxide precipitates.  
   - Observe the onset of precipitate formation, an essential step for achieving a uniform catalyst precursor.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   - After the initial coprecipitation, wash the precipitate thoroughly with deionized water until sulfate ions are undetectable (using BaCl₂ solution as a confirmatory test).  
   - Next, introduce silica sol concurrently with K₂CO₃ solution to achieve the target Fe/K/SiO₂ weight ratio of 100/4/20. This deposition step ensures the even incorporation of silica and potassium into the precipitated matrix.

3. • Drying  
   - Re-slurry the modified precipitate and subject the mixture to a spray drying process using a spray dryer. This step is critical for obtaining a uniform, free-flowing powder with controlled particle morphology, enhancing drying efficiency compared to conventional techniques.

4. • Calcination  
   - Calcine the spray-dried catalyst precursor at 773 K for 5 hours in a calcination furnace. This thermal treatment stabilizes the catalyst structure and promotes the formation of a hematite-based solid solution, vital for subsequent reduction and carburization during reaction.

The precise control of temperature, pH, reagent concentrations, and rigorous washing ensures reproducibility and optimal integration of Fe, Mn, and promoter species—factors that are confirmed by complementary QA responses and are crucial for superior catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the steps for preparing the catalyst precursors for the Fe–Mn–K/SiO₂ system with three formulations (Mn-00, Mn-07, and Mn-20). The procedure is organized into three key operations: weighing of solid reagents, liquid transfer, and mixing. Although the text does not specify exact masses or equipment precision, the formulations are defined through the target Fe/Mn weight ratios (100/0, 100/7, and 100/20), and critical conditions (pH 9.0 ± 0.1 and a solution temperature of 353 ± 1 K) are strictly maintained for reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

Each catalyst formulation is prepared by calculating the appropriate amounts of the solid reagents based on the desired Fe/Mn ratios.

##### 3.1.1.1 Mn-00  
- **FeSO₄•7H₂O (oxidized to Fe₂(SO₄)₃)**: Weighed to achieve a Fe/Mn ratio of 100:0.  
(Note: No manganese sulfate is added for Mn-00.)

##### 3.1.1.2 Mn-07  
- **FeSO₄•7H₂O (oxidized to Fe₂(SO₄)₃)**: Weighed to achieve a Fe/Mn ratio of 100:7.  
- **MnSO₄·6H₂O**: Weighed in an amount corresponding to the moderate manganese level.

##### 3.1.1.3 Mn-20  
- **FeSO₄•7H₂O (oxidized to Fe₂(SO₄)₃)**: Weighed to attain a Fe/Mn ratio of 100:20.  
- **MnSO₄·6H₂O**: Weighed in an amount corresponding to the high manganese level.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

For all catalyst variants, liquid reagents are transferred to ensure controlled precipitation.

##### 3.1.2.1 Mn-00  
- **Deionized Water**: Poured into a precipitation vessel pre-heated to 353 ± 1 K.  
- **NH₄OH Solution**: Added continuously to maintain a pH of 9.0 ± 0.1.

##### 3.1.2.2 Mn-07  
- **Deionized Water**: Poured into the pre-heated precipitation vessel at 353 ± 1 K.  
- **NH₄OH Solution**: Introduced at a controlled rate to adjust and hold the pH at 9.0 ± 0.1.

##### 3.1.2.3 Mn-20  
- **Deionized Water**: Poured into the precipitation vessel maintained at 353 ± 1 K.  
- **NH₄OH Solution**: Gradually added to sustain the target pH of 9.0 ± 0.1.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

Following the addition of both solids and liquids, each formulation is mixed to form a uniform precipitate.

##### 3.1.3.1 Mn-00  
- **Precipitate Mixture**: Mixed under controlled conditions to ensure uniform pH and consistent distribution of the Fe precursor.

##### 3.1.3.2 Mn-07  
- **Precipitate Mixture**: Thoroughly stirred to promote homogeneous dispersion of the moderate manganese content along with the Fe precursor.

##### 3.1.3.3 Mn-20  
- **Precipitate Mixture**: Mixed efficiently to ensure complete integration, laying the foundation for subsequent addition of silica sol and K₂CO₃, thus guaranteeing a homogeneous precursor blend.

This systematic grouping and documentation of operations ensure a reproducible synthetic protocol that directly impacts catalyst structure and subsequent Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe–Mn–K/SiO₂ (Mn-00, Mn-07, Mn-20)

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: FeSO₄•7H₂O (to be oxidized into ferric sulfate), MnSO₄•6H₂O (amount adjusted to achieve the desired Mn level: 0, 7, or 20 wt%), deionized water, and NH₄OH.  
- **Conditions**: The deionized water is preheated and maintained at 353 ± 1 K. A precise pH of 9.0 ± 0.1 is ensured using a calibrated pH meter and controlled dispensing of NH₄OH.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve the calculated amounts of FeSO₄•7H₂O and, if applicable based on the target catalyst, MnSO₄•6H₂O in deionized water. For each variant, maintain the Fe/Mn weight ratio at 100/x, where x corresponds to the manganese content.
  - Introduce the solution gradually into the precipitation vessel while keeping the temperature steady.  
  - Simultaneously add NH₄OH to adjust the pH to 9.0 ± 0.1, promoting the formation of uniform metal hydroxide precursors.
  - Stir the mixture for approximately 1 hour to ensure complete reaction and homogeneity.  
- **Key Steps**: Accurate reagent dosing, temperature control, and pH adjustment are critical for reproducibility.

##### Step [2]: Purification  
- **Process**:  
  - Wash the precipitate with deionized water maintained at about 323 K.  
  - Carry out several wash cycles until sulfate ions are undetectable by performing a BaCl₂ test.
- **Key Steps**: Thorough washing is essential to remove sulfate contaminants that could interfere with later stages.

##### Step [3]: Catalyst Assembly, Drying, and Calcination  
- **Reagents**: Add silica sol and a K₂CO₃ solution to the washed precipitate to achieve a Fe/K/SiO₂ ratio of 100/4/20.  
- **Conditions**: Reslurry the mixture uniformly; subsequent spray drying controls droplet size and particle morphology.  
- **Process**:  
  - Combine the precipitate with silica sol and K₂CO₃ solution with thorough mixing.  
  - Spray dry the resulting slurry under controlled parameters to form consistent particles.  
  - Dry the powder at 393 K for 6 hours before calcining in a furnace at 773 K for 5 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform mixing before spray drying and precise calcination conditions ensure optimal catalyst structure.

##### Step [4]: Activation (If Applicable)  
- **Process**: Optionally, reduce the calcined catalyst under a controlled H₂ flow and temperature profile to convert the iron oxides into active iron carbides, which is especially effective for the Mn-07 variant.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor reduction levels closely for reproducible catalytic performance.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 BET Surface Area Analysis  
- **Operational Description**: The surface area and pore structure of the catalysts are evaluated by N₂ physisorption using the BET method. Catalyst samples are first degassed to remove moisture and adsorbed impurities.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2500 system.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Degassing is performed at 393 K for 6 hours; measurements are taken at the normal boiling point of N₂ (77 K).  
- **Test Parameters**: Standard N₂ physisorption parameters—although specific scan ranges or step sizes are not detailed, the degassing condition ensures reproducible surface area determination.  
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample preparation via degassing is critical for accurate BET measurements.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)  
- **Operational Description**: This method assesses the catalyst’s reduction behavior. After loading the catalyst into a quartz flow reactor, H₂ is used to reduce iron oxides and monitor the reduction profile.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Conventional quartz flow reactor equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: A gas mixture of 5% H₂/95% Ar is used at a flow rate of 50 mL/min; temperature is ramped from 373 K to 1073 K at 6 K/min with a 30-minute hold at 1073 K. An in-line liquid-nitrogen trap is employed to remove water or CO₂ byproducts.  
- **Test Parameters**: Heating rate of 6 K/min and controlled gas flow ensure reproducibility in reduction peak detection.  
- **Key Notes**: Consistent catalyst loading and temperature control are essential.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)  
- **Operational Description**: CO‑TPR examines both reduction and carburization behavior by using CO as the reducing agent, which complements the H₂‑TPR data.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Conducted using a conventional atmospheric quartz flow reactor similar to H₂‑TPR.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Utilizes a gas mixture of 5% CO/95% He at 50 mL/min, with temperature increased from 373 K to 773 K at 6 K/min.  
- **Test Parameters**: Temperature ramping and gas composition are standardized for comparative analysis.  
- **Key Notes**: The method provides insights into carburization processes influenced by manganese content.

### 4.4 CO₂ Temperature‑Programmed Desorption (CO₂‑TPD)  
- **Operational Description**: CO₂‑TPD determines the surface basicity of the catalysts. A 200‐mg catalyst is pre-loaded and subjected to CO₂ adsorption and subsequent desorption.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Performed in the same quartz flow reactor setup.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: The catalyst is heated from 373 K to 773 K at 6 K/min, held at 773 K for 60 minutes, cooled to 373 K, followed by CO₂ adsorption at 373 K for 30 minutes and a 60-minute purge.  
- **Test Parameters**: A controlled ramp rate and fixed adsorption/purge durations ensure accurate basicity profiling.  
- **Key Notes**: This technique reveals increased basicity with moderate manganese incorporation.

### 4.5 Mössbauer Spectroscopy  
- **Operational Description**: This method characterizes phase composition, detecting hematite particle size and superparamagnetic Fe³⁺ species.  
- **Instruments and Models**: MR 351 constant‐acceleration Mössbauer spectrometer with a 25 mCi ⁵⁷Co in Pd matrix as the source.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements are performed at room temperature over 512 channels; isomer shifts are referenced to metallic Fe (α-Fe).  
- **Test Parameters**: Nonlinear least square fitting is employed to resolve singlets, doublets, and sextets in the spectra.  
- **Key Notes**: The resolution may be limited in distinguishing subtle differences among Fe³⁺ environments.

### 4.6 X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)  
- **Operational Description**: XPS is used to analyze the surface composition and manganese enrichment, particularly after calcination.  
- **Instruments and Models**: PHI Quantera SXM spectrometer using Al Kα radiation.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: The spectrometer is set to a resolution of 0.5 eV, with peak positions corrected by fixing the C 1s binding energy at 284.8 eV.  
- **Test Parameters**: While detailed instrument calibration parameters are not provided, the method effectively captures surface elemental distributions.  
- **Key Notes**: XPS results provide crucial information regarding surface modifications due to manganese presence.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is conducted as a pre-reaction treatment, focusing on controlled reduction and carburization to expose the active iron carbide phases. The activation protocol is uniformly applied across all catalyst formulations (Mn-00, Mn-07, and Mn-20) regardless of manganese loading.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for promoting reduction and the subsequent carburization processes, which transform iron oxides into the active carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at a temperature of 553 K.
- **Observations:** This temperature ensures efficient reduction without promoting undesirable sintering, thereby maintaining optimal catalyst dispersion.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The activation pressure influences the reduction kinetics and gas–solid interactions during the pre-reaction treatment.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is carried out at 0.10 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at this controlled pressure facilitates consistent activation, enabling reproducibility in catalyst performance without leading to agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas is pivotal for achieving the delicate balance between reduction and carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with a hydrogen-to-carbon monoxide molar ratio of 1.20 is employed.
- **Observations:** This gas composition optimizes the formation of active iron carbide phases. The presence of CO assists in carburizing the metallic iron while H₂ drives the reduction process.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity defines the flow of activation gas per unit catalyst weight, ensuring thorough exposure of the catalyst surface to the reactive gas phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A gas hourly space velocity of 1000 h⁻¹ is maintained during activation.
- **Observations:** This parameter provides a balance that avoids under- or over-exposure, contributing to a uniform reduction across the catalyst bed.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is essential to achieve complete reduction and carburization without over-processing.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under the specified activation conditions for a total of 24 hours.
- **Observations:** The 24-hour activation period is sufficient to form the necessary active phases while ensuring catalyst stability and reproducibility, particularly evident in the improved performance of the Mn-07 catalyst formulation.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter for activating syngas and forming the active iron carbide phase. In this study, the Fischer–Tropsch reaction was conducted at 523 K. This temperature is optimal for promoting the reduction of iron oxides and facilitating the carburization process. Enhanced formation and stabilization of active phases were observed under these conditions, with CO conversion monitored over time to ensure catalyst stability.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure directly influences syngas concentration and reaction kinetics. The experiments were performed at a pressure of 1.5 MPa. This pressure supports increased reactant concentrations and improved overall kinetics, contributing to favorable hydrocarbon chain growth. Additionally, the 1.5 MPa condition aids in maintaining catalyst stability, as noted by the improved performance trends, particularly in catalysts with manganese modifications.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio is essential for controlling hydrocarbon selectivity. A feed ratio of 1.2 was employed in all catalytic tests. This balanced ratio was found to suppress the formation of light hydrocarbons such as CH₄ while enhancing the production of heavier hydrocarbons (C₁₂⁺). The specific ratio also plays a role in facilitating CO chemisorption, crucial for effective chain growth during synthesis.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
Space-time velocity, often expressed as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), determines the contact time between reactants and the catalyst. In these experiments, a GHSV of 2000 h⁻¹ was maintained. This parameter supports efficient reactant conversion, ensuring adequate exposure and contact, which in turn leads to improved CO conversion and consistent catalyst performance.

#### 5.2.5 Reactor Configuration  
The reaction was carried out in a continuous slurry tank reactor with a working volume of 1 dm³. This configuration, which includes a mixture of liquid paraffin and 20.0 g of catalyst, provides a controlled environment for thorough mixing and consistent reaction conditions. The reactor setup and the defined conditions collectively enable high reproducibility and reliable performance evaluation in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** The textural properties of the Fe–Mn–K/SiO₂ catalysts were evaluated by N₂ physisorption using a Micromeritics ASAP 2500 system. BET surface area, pore volume, and average pore size were determined after degassing the samples at 393 K for 6 hours. These parameters are critical for understanding catalyst dispersion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:**  
  - BET surface area (m²/g): Mn-00 = 192, Mn-07 = 195, Mn-20 = 196  
  - Pore volume (cm³/g): Mn-00 = 0.38, Mn-07 = 0.37, Mn-20 = 0.32  
  - Average pore size (nm): Mn-00 = 8.67, Mn-07 = 8.33, Mn-20 = 7.36  
- **Observations:** The addition of SiO₂ significantly enhances dispersion and surface area. Differences in BET surface area among the catalysts are minor and within experimental error (±3%), indicating that manganese content does not substantially affect textural properties.

#### 6.1.2 Crystal Phase and Crystallite Size
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to determine the phase composition and crystallite size of iron species. This technique is sensitive to the local environment of iron atoms and is essential for identifying active phases in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:**  
  - Fresh catalysts exhibited a central doublet, assigned to superparamagnetic Fe³⁺ ions in non-cubic sites of α-Fe₂O₃ (hematite), with crystallite diameters <13.5 nm.
  - Increasing manganese content led to a decrease in α-Fe₂O₃ particle size, indicating improved dispersion.
- **Observations:** Moderate manganese addition (Mn-07) results in the smallest crystallite size, which is beneficial for catalytic activity due to increased surface area of active phases.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Carburization Behavior
- **Description:** H₂-TPR and CO-TPR were conducted in quartz flow reactors with thermal conductivity detection to assess the reduction and carburization behavior of the catalysts. These properties are directly linked to the formation of active iron carbide phases.
- **Findings:**  
  - For Mn-00, the reduction of α-Fe₂O₃ to FeO occurs at 688 K, with subsequent FeO-to-Fe reduction peaks at 937 K and 1026 K.
  - Mn-07 shows larger CO-TPR peak areas, indicating facilitated reduction and carburization, while Mn-20 exhibits suppressed reduction and smaller TPR profile areas.
  - Mössbauer spectroscopy after reduction (553 K, H₂/CO = 1.2, 0.1 MPa, 24 h) revealed that Mn-07 has the highest amount of iron carbides (χ-Fe₅C₂), whereas Mn-20 has the least.
- **Observations:** Moderate manganese addition (Mn-07) optimizes reduction and iron carbide formation, correlating with higher FTS activity. Excessive manganese (Mn-20) leads to surface enrichment that suppresses these processes.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Basicity
- **Description:** CO₂-TPD was used to quantify surface basicity, a key factor influencing CO adsorption and hydrocarbon selectivity. The technique measures CO₂ desorption from basic sites as the sample is heated.
- **Findings:**  
  - Mn-07 exhibits the largest total TPD area, with peak 2 (medium-strength basic sites) shifting to higher temperature and increasing in intensity.
  - Mn-20 shows a decrease in total TPD area and a shift of peak 3 (stronger basic sites) to lower temperature, indicating reduced basicity.
- **Observations:** Surface basicity is maximized at moderate manganese levels (Mn-07), enhancing CO dissociation and selectivity to heavier hydrocarbons. Excess manganese reduces effective potassium content and overall basicity.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Composition and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed to analyze surface elemental composition and the distribution of Mn, Fe, K, and Si. This provides insight into promoter enrichment and its impact on catalytic properties.
- **Findings:**  
  - The Mn/Fe atomic ratio at the surface is significantly higher than in the bulk, confirming manganese surface enrichment after calcination.
  - Excessive manganese (Mn-20) reduces the effective potassium content on the surface.
- **Observations:** Surface enrichment of manganese enhances basicity and alters adsorption properties up to an optimal level, but excessive enrichment suppresses iron reduction and carburization, negatively impacting catalyst performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 523 K, 1.5 MPa, 2000 h⁻¹, and H₂/CO = 1.2, steady-state CO conversion values were:
  - Mn-00: 41.7% (at 203 h), 45.9% (at 513 h)
  - Mn-07: 48.1% (at 198 h), 51.2% (at 510 h)
  - Mn-20: 8.9% (at 199 h), 9.4% (at 510 h)
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate manganese addition (Mn-07) significantly enhances CO conversion compared to the undoped catalyst (Mn-00), while excessive manganese (Mn-20) sharply reduces activity. This trend is attributed to optimal iron carbide formation and improved reduction/carburization in Mn-07, with Mn-20 suffering from surface Mn enrichment that suppresses iron reduction.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** 
  - Mn-00: 9.6 wt%
  - Mn-07: 9.1–9.5 wt%
  - Mn-20: 4.4–4.7 wt%
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing manganese content suppresses methane formation, with Mn-20 exhibiting the lowest CH₄ selectivity. This suppression is linked to enhanced surface basicity and altered adsorption behavior.

#### 6.2.3 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Selectivity to C₁₂⁺ (a subset of C₃⁺) increases with manganese content:
  - Mn-00: 10.1–13.7 wt%
  - Mn-07: 20.0–24.1 wt%
  - Mn-20: 43.4–43.7 wt%
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese doping shifts product distribution toward heavier hydrocarbons, with Mn-20 showing the highest selectivity for C₁₂⁺ products.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, with values from 0 (only CH₄) to 1 (very long chains):
  - Calculated from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - Mn-00: 0.62–0.69
  - Mn-07: 0.60–0.82
  - Mn-20: 0.57–0.90
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher manganese content increases α, indicating a greater tendency for long-chain hydrocarbon formation. The highest α values correlate with increased iron carbide content, especially in Mn-07.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is the decline in CO conversion over time, typically expressed as % h⁻¹:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit numerical deactivation rates are not provided, Mn-07 demonstrates the highest stability (lowest deactivation), Mn-00 is intermediate, and Mn-20 deactivates most rapidly.
- **Observations and Trends:** The improved stability of Mn-07 is attributed to optimal iron carbide formation and balanced surface basicity, whereas excessive manganese in Mn-20 leads to rapid deactivation.

#### 6.2.6 Induction Period
- **Description:** The induction period is the time required to reach steady-state CO conversion after catalyst activation.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - Mn-00: ~400 h
  - Mn-07: ~150 h
  - Mn-20: nearly eliminated
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese addition accelerates catalyst activation, but excessive Mn (Mn-20) shortens the induction period at the expense of steady-state activity.

**Summary:** Moderate manganese promotion (Mn-07) optimizes CO conversion, selectivity toward heavier hydrocarbons, chain growth probability, and catalyst stability, while excessive manganese (Mn-20) suppresses activity despite further increases in selectivity for long-chain products.
