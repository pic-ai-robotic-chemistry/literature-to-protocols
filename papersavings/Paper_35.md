## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines the synthesis of the CoMn catalyst via a co‑precipitation process with an integrated precipitation–deposition step, followed by drying and calcination. The procedure is reported with precise control over parameters to ensure reproducibility.

#### • Co‑precipitation and Precipitation–Deposition Method  
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: Dissolve cobalt nitrate (Co(NO₃)₂•6H₂O) and manganese nitrate (Mn(NO₃)₂, 50 wt% aqueous solution) in deionized water, maintaining a Co/Mn molar ratio of 2/1 in a 2 M mixed salt solution.  
   - Process: Add the mixed salt solution dropwise into deionized water containing the precipitant (either sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) or ammonium carbonate ((NH₄)₂CO₃)).  
   - Conditions: Maintain a pH of 8.0 ± 0.1 and a temperature of 30 ± 1 °C during the addition.
   - Observation: Controlled addition ensures uniform precipitate formation, critical for subsequent steps.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Integration: Immediately following precursor mixing, a precipitation–deposition step occurs before drying.  
   - Procedure: Continue stirring the suspension for enhanced deposition of the support precursor; this step is essential for tailoring the catalyst morphology.
  
3. Drying  
   - Conditions: The precipitate is centrifuged, washed with deionized water (seven times), and then dried at 100 °C for 10 h.
  
4. Calcination  
   - Conditions: Calcine the dried catalyst in a muffle furnace at 330 °C for 3 h under static air to achieve the CoₓMn₃₋ₓO₄ precursor structure.  
   - Outcome: Under subsequent syngas exposure, the precursor transforms into Co₂C nanoprisms, forming the active phase for Fischer–Tropsch to olefins (FTO).

### Comparative Analysis  
- Advantages: The co‑precipitation method, including the integrated precipitation–deposition step, offers a uniform distribution of metal precursors and is less complex and scalable compared to traditional impregnation methods.  
- Drawbacks: It requires precise control of pH and temperature to avoid incomplete precipitation.

This detailed protocol ensures experimental reproducibility with exact numerical values and conditions, facilitating replication by other researchers.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases present in the CoMn catalyst and their evolution under syngas were determined by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer (Cu Kα, 1.54056 Å). Quantitative Rietveld refinement was performed against the PDF4-2015 database, with phase identification and lattice parameter refinement for Co₂C, MnO, and CoxMn₁−xO.
- **Findings:** The as-prepared catalyst consists primarily of CoxMn₁−xO and a minor fraction of metallic Co. Upon exposure to syngas, XRD patterns reveal a progressive transformation: metallic Co is rapidly converted to Co₂C, while CoxMn₁−xO segregates into Co₂C and MnO. After 2 hours on stream, nearly all metallic Co is converted, and with increasing reaction time, the fractions of Co₂C and MnO increase at the expense of CoxMn₁−xO. The Co₂C phase is identified as the active component, with its content correlating with catalytic performance.
- **Observations:** The formation and growth of Co₂C under reaction conditions are directly linked to enhanced selectivity for lower olefins in the Fischer–Tropsch process. The presence of MnO as a segregated phase is also confirmed.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Facet Exposure
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were employed to examine particle morphology and exposed crystal facets. Samples were dispersed in ethanol and imaged at 200 kV.
- **Findings:** TEM images show the emergence of parallelepiped-shaped (quadrangular) Co₂C nanoprisms, alongside sphere-like nanoparticles. HRTEM reveals lattice fringes corresponding to the (101), (−101), and (020) planes of Co₂C, confirming the exposure of these facets. The evolution from irregular to well-defined nanoprisms occurs with increasing time on stream.
- **Observations:** The specific nanoprism morphology and exposed facets are associated with high catalytic selectivity for lower olefins and low methane formation, highlighting the importance of controlled synthesis and phase evolution.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Spatial Distribution
- **Description:** Elemental analysis was conducted using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) for spatial mapping and inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis for bulk quantification.

- **Observations:** The spatial segregation of Co and Mn supports the XRD findings of phase separation. Residual sodium, as quantified by ICP, is shown to promote Co₂C formation, while manganese aids in nanoprism development, both critical for optimal catalytic performance.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (250 °C, 1 bar, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 2,000 ml h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹), the CoMn catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 31.8%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The formation of Co₂C nanoprisms, promoted by residual sodium and manganese, is directly linked to this high conversion. The conversion remains stable over extended operation, indicating robust catalytic activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO}_2\,\text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 1 bar and H₂/CO = 2, CO₂ selectivity was reported as 47.3%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The relatively high CO₂ selectivity is characteristic of the Co₂C phase, which favors the water-gas shift reaction alongside Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4\,\text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity was 5.0% at H₂/CO = 2, decreasing to 2.4% at H₂/CO = 0.5.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lowering the H₂/CO ratio suppresses methane formation. The presence of Co₂C nanoprisms, stabilized by Na and Mn, is crucial for maintaining low methane selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–₄= (Lower Olefin) Selectivity and Olefin/Paraffin Ratio
- **Description:** Lower olefin selectivity is the percentage of converted CO forming C₂–C₄ olefins. The olefin/paraffin ratio indicates the preference for unsaturated over saturated hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** At 250 °C, 1 bar, H₂/CO = 2, the selectivity for C₂–₄= was 60.8% (CO₂-free basis). Reducing H₂/CO to 0.5 increased the C₂–₄ olefin/paraffin ratio to 51.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibits exceptional selectivity for lower olefins, with propylene (C₃=) as the dominant product. Lower H₂/CO ratios further enhance olefin selectivity and suppress paraffin formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, represents the probability of chain growth:
\[
\text{ASF:}\quad W_n = n(1-\alpha)^2\alpha^{n-1}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** The α value decreases rapidly within the first 4 h of reaction and then stabilizes, corresponding to a shift from long-chain hydrocarbons to lower olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** The decrease in α is associated with the transformation of metallic Co to Co₂C, resulting in a product distribution favoring lower olefins and deviating from the classical ASF pattern.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over time.
- **Experimental Data:** The catalyst maintained stable CO conversion and product selectivity with no significant deactivation over 600 h under optimized conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The structural stability of Co₂C nanoprisms, promoted by Na and Mn, underpins the catalyst’s long-term performance and industrial viability.
