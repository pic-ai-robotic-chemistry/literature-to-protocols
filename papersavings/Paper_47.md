## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is achieved using a high-temperature liquid‑phase method in a four‑neck flask, ensuring a controlled inert environment (N₂ or NH₃) during the reaction. This method emphasizes reproducibility through strict temperature control, precise reagent injection, and defined ramp rates.

#### High-Temperature Liquid‑Phase Synthesis: Fe5C₂ Catalyst

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Reagents: Fe(CO)₅, along with solvents and stabilizers such as Octadecylamine and hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB).  
   • Equipment: Four‑neck flask equipped for stirring and gas flow management.  
   • Conditions: The reaction is initiated under an inert atmosphere (N₂ for Fe5C₂) with the mixture heated to 180 °C.  
   • Observation: Injection of Fe(CO)₅ results in a distinct color change from orange to black, signaling precursor decomposition and Fe nanocrystals nucleation.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • If applicable, a support precursor is added dropwise immediately after nucleation.  
   • Stirring is maintained for 1 h to ensure uniform deposition on the forming nanoparticle surfaces.

3. **Drying**  
   • The system undergoes solvent removal, typically through controlled drying conditions prior to calcination.

4. **Calcination and Post‑treatment**  
   • Temperature Ramp: The mixture is ramped at 10 °C/min and heated to the target temperature—350 °C for Fe5C₂ (or 300 °C for pure Fe and 260 °C for Fe2C, the latter with a 2‑hour hold).  
   • Holding Period: A critical 10‑minute hold (except for the extended period for Fe2C) ensures phase transformation and carbide formation.  
   • Subsequent Washing: Nanoparticles are washed with ethanol and hexane to remove residual chemicals.  
   • Storage: The catalysts are immediately transferred to an Ar‑filled glovebox to prevent oxidation.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural purity of the Fe-based catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD), including both ex situ and in situ measurements with a Rigaku D/Max-2400 diffractometer and synchrotron beam line 14B. These analyses are critical for confirming the formation of targeted iron and iron-carbide phases and monitoring phase evolution under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that the synthesized nanoparticles are single-phase, with Fe, Fe₅C₂, Fe₇C₃, and Fe₂C each displaying distinct diffraction peaks matching their respective standard patterns. In situ XRD under syngas flow demonstrated the transformation of α‑Fe (peak at 35.3° two-theta) into Fe₅C₂ (peak at 34.8°) as temperature increased above 220 °C, evidencing carburization during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Observations:** The absence of peaks corresponding to Fe oxides or other impurities confirms high phase purity. The real-time monitoring of α‑Fe to Fe₅C₂ transformation highlights the dynamic structural evolution relevant to catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Local Coordination and Electronic Structure
- **Description:** X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XANES/EXAFS) was employed to probe the local atomic environment and electronic state of iron species, providing insight into Fe–C bonding and oxidation states.
- **Findings:** XANES spectra indicated that iron-carbide particles possess lower oxidation states compared to metallic Fe. EXAFS analysis identified Fe–C coordination shells at approximately 2.0 Å and an expansion of the first Fe–Fe shell from 2.46 Å (α‑Fe) to about 2.60 Å in carbides, consistent with carbon incorporation.
- **Observations:** No features attributable to Fe oxides or metallic Fe were detected, corroborating the formation of pure-phase iron carbides.

#### 6.1.3 Phase Identification and Crystallinity (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was utilized to further distinguish iron-carbide phases and assess crystallinity.
- **Findings:** The spectra displayed sextet peaks characteristic of Fe₅C₂, Fe₂C, and Fe₇C₃, with weak doublet peaks in Fe₅C₂ indicating superior crystallinity relative to Fe₂C and Fe₇C₃. No signals from Fe oxides or metallic Fe were observed.
- **Observations:** These results reinforce the XRD findings, confirming the successful synthesis of pure-phase iron carbides.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size

- **Observations:** The uniformity in particle size across all samples suggests effective control over synthesis conditions and supports the reproducibility of the method.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Composition and Chemical State (XPS)

- **Findings:** The Fe 2p region in XPS spectra enabled differentiation between oxidized iron species and iron carbide (FeCx), confirming the chemical state of iron in the catalysts.
- **Observations:** The data support the presence of FeCx and the absence of significant surface oxidation, consistent with the inert storage and handling protocols.

- **Observations:** The oxidative deactivation is attributed to reaction with H₂O and CO₂, underscoring the importance of phase stability for sustained catalyst performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) reaction. It is typically calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (270 °C, 30 bar, 32% CO/64% H₂/4% Ar), initial CO conversion values were: Fe5C2 – 35%, Fe2C – 29.5%, Fe7C3 – 22.5%, and α‑Fe – 15.2%. For Fe5C2, the CO conversion decreased sharply within the first 6 hours, stabilizing at 27%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe5C2 exhibited the highest initial and stabilized CO conversion, indicating superior intrinsic activity. α‑Fe showed a lower initial conversion but increased upon in situ carburization to Fe5C2, highlighting the importance of phase evolution.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift and oxidation reactions:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe2C displayed the highest CO₂ selectivity at 22.7%. Fe7C3 and Fe5C2 also produced CO₂, but at lower selectivities.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ formation was most pronounced on Fe2C, distinguishing it from Fe5C2 and Fe7C3, which favored hydrocarbon production. The higher CO₂ selectivity for Fe2C is attributed to its greater susceptibility to oxidation and water–gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, a less desirable product in FTS:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe2C and Fe7C3 exhibited high methane selectivities of 20.1% and 19.5%, respectively. Fe5C2 and α‑Fe produced less methane.
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane formation was not detected at low temperatures and commenced only above 190 °C, indicating that C–C coupling is kinetically favored over methane formation on these catalysts.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms, indicative of chain growth:
\[
\text{C₃⁺ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}} + F_{\text{CH₄,out}} + 2F_{\text{C₂H₂,out}} + 2F_{\text{C₂H₄,out}} + 2F_{\text{C₂H₆,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe5C2 and α‑Fe produced significant C₃⁺ hydrocarbons, with Fe5C2 showing higher C₅⁺ selectivity (49%).
- **Observations and Trends:** The appearance of C₄ and C₅⁺ products at elevated temperatures and over time underscores the superior chain growth capability of Fe5C2 and α‑Fe.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination, influencing hydrocarbon length. Higher α values favor longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values (at 270 °C, 30 bar) were:  
  - Fe: 1.1 (1 h), 1.7 (30 h)  
  - Fe5C2: 2.3 (1 h), 1.8 (30 h)  
  - Fe7C3: 1.6 (1 h), 1.7 (30 h)  
  - Fe2C: 1.8 (1 h), 1.0 (30 h)
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe5C2 consistently exhibited the highest α factor, correlating with its enhanced selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons. The α factor for α‑Fe increased with time, reflecting its transformation to Fe5C2.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate reflects the decline in CO conversion over time, indicating catalyst stability:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe5C2, CO conversion dropped from 35% to 27% within the first 6 hours, then stabilized.
- **Observations and Trends:** The initial rapid deactivation of Fe5C2 is attributed to surface oxidation (e.g., by water), but the catalyst maintains stable performance thereafter, demonstrating good long-term stability relative to other phases.
