## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis for the xAM/Fe system is centered on achieving precise promoter-to-Fe loadings by integrating either a precipitation–deposition technique or an incipient wetness impregnation method. Both approaches ensure accurate adjustment of variables such as pH, temperature, and promoter concentration to produce a reproducible Fe₂O₃-based catalyst that, under reaction conditions, transforms into the desired active phases (Fe₅C₂ and Fe₃O₄).

#### Precipitation–Deposition Method
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Reagents: Dissolve iron nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O) in deionized water along with the alkali metal nitrate (e.g., KNO₃) corresponding to promoter ratios (0, 0.001, or 0.05).  
   • pH Control: Add 6.7M ammonia solution dropwise under continuous stirring with a pH meter until the pH reaches exactly 9.5.  
   • Equipment: Magnetic stirrer and pH meter ensure homogeneity.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • The addition of ammonia leads to controlled precipitation, forming a uniform suspension that is aged for 2 hours at room temperature.  
   • Observations: No specific color change is noted; however, the steady formation of a precipitate confirms successful nucleation.

3. **Drying**  
   • Conditions: The catalyst is dried in a muffle furnace at 100 °C for 12 hours to secure uniform dispersion and prevent promoter segregation.  
   • Equipment: Muffle furnace is used to precisely control drying temperature.

4. **Calcination**  
   • Conditions: Post-drying, the sample is calcined at 400 °C for 5 hours in a muffle furnace, facilitating the formation of the α‑Fe₂O₃ support structure with integrated promoter species.  

A comparative evaluation reveals that while the precipitation–deposition method offers simplicity and uniformity through controlled pH and aging, the impregnation method—although providing enhanced control over promoter distribution—requires more stringent volume and dispersion management. Both methods yield catalysts that, after an in situ pre‐reduction at 400 °C for 2 hours under hydrogen, deliver consistent performance in CO₂ hydrogenation reactions.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: xAM/Fe (e.g., 0.001Cs/Fe)
  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Iron nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O) in a calculated amount dissolved in deionized water.  
  • Alkali metal nitrate (e.g., CsNO₃) corresponding to the desired atomic ratio (x = 0.001 or 0.05).

- **Conditions**:  
  • Reaction medium: Aqueous; maintain solution pH at 9.5 by dropwise addition of ammonia under continuous stirring.

  1. Dissolve the iron nitrate in a defined volume of deionized water.  
  2. Add the alkali metal nitrate to the solution and stir to achieve a uniform precursor mixture.  
  3. Gradually introduce ammonia until the pH reaches 9.5 and age the suspension for 2 hours.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensure complete dissolution and uniform pH adjustment to facilitate controlled precipitation.

- **Key Steps**:

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for initiating the reduction of the Fe₂O₃ precursor, setting the catalyst on a controlled pathway toward forming the active phases.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The in situ pre‐reduction is performed at 400 °C.  
- **Observations:** This temperature is sufficiently high to promote the partial reduction of Fe₂O₃ into Fe₃O₄, thereby priming the catalyst for subsequent conversion into active carbide phases under CO₂ hydrogenation conditions.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure enhances the reaction kinetics by increasing the collision frequency of hydrogen molecules with the catalyst surface, facilitating more efficient reduction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is conducted under 15 bar.  
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure accelerates the reduction process without leading to excessive sintering, ensuring a uniform transformation of the catalyst material.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation defines the reducing environment necessary for converting the iron oxide precursor into its partially reduced form.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A 1:1 H₂/N₂ mixture is utilized, where hydrogen comprises 50 vol.% of the mixture, accompanied by a controlled flow rate of 12 mL/min.  
- **Observations:** The hydrogen provides the reducing power required to initiate the transformation, while nitrogen acts as an inert diluent that moderates the reaction rate and minimizes the risk of over-reduction or sintering.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time and Flow Details
- **Definition:** The activation time, along with the gas flow (or space-time velocity), ensures that the catalyst is uniformly exposed to the reducing atmosphere across the entire catalyst bed.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The pre‐reduction step is maintained for 2 hours with the specified gas flow, ensuring that all catalyst particles experience consistent treatment.  
- **Observations:** This duration is optimal for achieving a thorough reduction of Fe₂O₃ into Fe₃O₄, which is a precursor state for the reaction-induced formation of the active iron carbide phase (Fe₅C₂).

Overall, these conditions—400 °C, 15 bar, a 1:1 H₂/N₂ mixture at 12 mL/min for 2 hours—collectively contribute to the reproducible activation of Fischer–Tropsch catalysts while preserving the catalyst’s structural integrity and minimizing sintering, thus promoting reliable catalytic performance in subsequent CO₂ hydrogenation reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was performed at a controlled temperature of 300 °C. This temperature is critical for effective syngas activation and promoting the appropriate phase transformation of iron from Fe₃O₄ towards the active iron carbide (Fe₅C₂) phase during the reaction. Under these conditions, the catalysts maintained notable stability over prolonged operation, with minimal signs of rapid deactivation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The reaction was conducted at a pressure of 15 bar. This elevated pressure increases the concentration of syngas components, which is essential for driving the formation of higher hydrocarbons. Experimental observations indicate that this pressure enhances CO₂ conversion efficiency and steers the hydrocarbon distribution away from undesired methane formation towards C₂⁺ products.

#### 5.2.3 Gas Composition and H₂/CO Ratio  
The feed gas employed consisted of H₂/CO₂/N₂ in a volumetric ratio of 3/1/0.3. Importantly, the H₂/CO ratio during reaction conditions is maintained at 3:1. This specific composition plays a pivotal role in governing the selectivity of the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It not only optimizes CO₂ hydrogenation but also balances the competitive formation of methane versus higher hydrocarbons. The controlled ratio contributes to stabilizing the formation of the desired iron carbide phase and moderates the CO consumption dynamics throughout the reaction.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The process was operated at a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 1160 mL/(gcat·h). This parameter defines the contact time between the syngas and the catalyst, thereby influencing the overall CO₂ conversion and product selectivity. A GHSV of this magnitude was found to be effective in achieving reproducible catalytic performance while also providing insight into the kinetics of the reaction. Combined, these parameters ensure that the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions are rigorously defined and reproducible, allowing for confident replication and further studies on catalyst behavior under similar experimental settings.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases present in the Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), selected area electron diffraction (SAED), and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques collectively reveal the transformation of iron phases during catalyst activation and reaction.
- **Findings:** As-synthesized catalysts are stabilized in the hematite phase (α‑Fe₂O₃), as confirmed by XRD and TEM. Upon in situ activation and after 90 hours on stream at 300 °C, XRD patterns of spent catalysts display reflexes characteristic of Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) and Fe₅C₂ (iron carbide), confirming the conversion of Fe₂O₃ into these active phases. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantifies the Fe₅C₂ fraction, which ranges from 5.3% to 8.7% in 0AM/Fe and 0.001AM/Fe catalysts, increasing up to ~25% with higher K loading. No metallic Fe is detected after reaction.
- **Observations:** The fraction of Fe₅C₂ increases with higher alkali metal promoter loading, especially for K, indicating that promoter concentration is a key factor in phase evolution. The persistence of Fe₃O₄ alongside Fe₅C₂ is consistently observed in spent catalysts.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Surface Electronic Structure
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze the surface elemental composition and electronic states of iron, with a focus on the effects of alkali metal promoters.
- **Findings:** The Fe 2p₃/₂ peak for FexCy (iron carbide) species appears at 706.8–707.5 eV. The presence of alkali promoters shifts this peak to lower binding energies, indicating electron donation from the promoter to Fe. This shift is more pronounced with increasing promoter loading, particularly for Na and K. The Allen scale electronegativity of the promoter correlates with these electronic modifications.
- **Observations:** The electronic effects induced by alkali promoters, as evidenced by XPS, are directly linked to changes in catalytic activity and selectivity, with greater electron donation favoring the formation of higher hydrocarbons over methane.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Phase Distribution
- **Description:** TEM and SAED were used to assess catalyst morphology, particle dispersion, and phase distribution before and after reaction.
- **Findings:** TEM images of as-synthesized catalysts show uniform particles in the α‑Fe₂O₃ phase with no observable segregation of promoter species. After reaction, TEM and SAED confirm the presence of crystalline Fe₃O₄ and Fe₅C₂ phases. 
- **Observations:** The absence of promoter segregation and the clear identification of active phases support the effectiveness of the synthesis and activation protocols.

#### 6.1.4 Bulk and Surface Composition Analysis
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) and atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) were used to determine the bulk Fe and alkali metal promoter contents.
- **Findings:** ICP-OES analysis confirms the intended Fe and promoter loadings in the as-prepared catalysts, with AAS used for Cs quantification.
- **Observations:** Accurate control of promoter concentration is achieved, enabling systematic investigation of promoter effects on catalyst structure and performance.

#### 6.1.5 Reduction and Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) and temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) were conducted to evaluate reduction behavior and CO₂ adsorption properties.
- **Findings:** TPR profiles show that alkali metal promoters shift reduction peaks to higher temperatures, indicating slower reduction kinetics. CO₂-TPD reveals increased strength and concentration of basic sites with higher promoter loading, especially for K, Rb, and Cs.
- **Observations:** Enhanced basicity and modified reduction behavior are associated with improved CO₂ activation and selectivity toward higher hydrocarbons.

#### 6.1.6 Transient Kinetic Analysis
- **Description:** Temporal Analysis of Products (TAP) pulse experiments were performed to probe adsorption and desorption kinetics of CO₂, CO, H₂, and C₂H₄.
- **Findings:** CO₂ exhibits reversible adsorption on all catalysts. C₂H₄ shows reversible adsorption on 0AM/Fe and 0.001Li/Fe, but weak adsorption on Na- and K-promoted catalysts. H₂/D₂ exchange experiments provide insight into hydrogen activation.
- **Observations:** The kinetic data elucidate the elementary steps governing product selectivity and the influence of promoter type and loading on surface reactivity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ consumed relative to the amount fed into the reactor, calculated as:
  \[
  X(\mathrm{CO}_2) = 1 - \frac{n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}^{\text{out}}}{n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}^{\text{in}}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (300°C, 15 bar, H₂/CO₂/N₂ = 3/1/0.3), unpromoted Fe catalysts (0AM/Fe) exhibit higher initial CO₂ conversion rates in the first catalyst segment compared to alkali-promoted variants. However, K-, Rb-, and Cs-promoted catalysts maintain higher CO₂ consumption rates downstream, indicating improved efficiency at higher conversions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of alkali metal promoters, especially K, Rb, and Cs, moderates the intrinsic activity but enhances overall CO₂ utilization along the catalyst bed. Increasing promoter loading (x = 0.001 to 0.05) further augments CO₂ adsorption and conversion capacity.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO₂ forming methane, defined as:
  \[
  S(\mathrm{CH}_4) = \frac{n_{\mathrm{CH}_4}^{\text{out}}}{n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}^{\text{in}} - n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}^{\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity is highest for 0AM/Fe and decreases markedly with alkali metal promotion. The order of CH₄ formation rate in the first segment is: 0AM/Fe > Li/Fe, Na/Fe > K/Fe, Rb/Fe, Cs/Fe. For K, Rb, and Cs-promoted catalysts, the CH₄/CO selectivity ratio at zero CO₂ conversion drops to ≤0.05, compared to 0.75 for unpromoted Fe.
- **Observations and Trends:** Stronger alkali promoters suppress direct CO₂ hydrogenation to CH₄, shifting product distribution toward higher hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.3 C₂⁺ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** C₂⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO₂ forming hydrocarbons with two or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  S(\mathrm{C}_2^+) = \frac{\sum n_{\mathrm{C}_2^+}^{\text{out}}}{n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}^{\text{in}} - n_{\mathrm{CO}_2}^{\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The formation rate and selectivity of C₂⁺ hydrocarbons increase with the use of K, Rb, and Cs promoters, especially at higher CO₂ conversions. Segmental rate analysis confirms that these promoters favor C₂⁺ production over CH₄.
- **Observations and Trends:** The shift toward C₂⁺ hydrocarbons is most pronounced for catalysts with higher promoter loadings, correlating with increased basicity and CO₂ adsorption capacity.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination:
  \[
  \alpha = \frac{\text{moles of } C_{n+1}}{\text{moles of } C_n}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values include 3.37 × 10² for 0.001Na/Fe and 1.64 × 10¹ for 0.001K/Fe (noting the article’s specific definition). Higher α values are associated with increased selectivity toward long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts with higher basicity and weaker ethylene adsorption, as imparted by K, Rb, and Cs promoters, exhibit elevated α factors, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring the decline in CO₂ conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{X_{\text{initial}} - X_{\text{final}}}{\text{reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Stability tests (40–90 h on stream) reveal that catalysts with higher promoter loadings (e.g., 0.05K/Fe) maintain more stable CO₂ conversion, attributed to the transformation of Fe₃O₄ into iron carbides (Fe₅C₂). In contrast, low-promoter or unpromoted catalysts show faster deactivation due to incomplete phase transformation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced stability correlates with the extent of iron carbide formation, which is promoted by higher alkali metal content.

#### 6.2.6 Correlation with Promoter Electronegativity
- **Description:** The Allen scale electronegativity difference (ΔEN) between Fe and the alkali metal promoter serves as a performance descriptor.
- **Experimental Data:** As ΔEN increases, rates of CO₂ conversion to CH₄ and C₂⁺ decrease, while CO₂ adsorption and dissociation rates increase.
- **Observations and Trends:** This correlation provides a mechanistic basis for rational catalyst design, linking electronic promoter effects to activity and selectivity.
