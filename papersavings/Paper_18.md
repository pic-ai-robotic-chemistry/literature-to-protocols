## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis method detailed in Paper 18 employs a wet impregnation approach for the preparation of the Example Catalyst (Co/Al₂O₃-x%La). This method is implemented to ensure reproducible catalyst structure and optimized metal–support interactions, with a clear emphasis on maintaining exact reagent concentrations, controlled pH, and precisely managed thermal profiles. A comparative evaluation in the article notes that while the wet impregnation method is simple and cost‑effective (favoring scalability), it may offer less control over particle size compared to more complex techniques.

#### Wet Impregnation Method

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Reagents: An aqueous solution of cobalt nitrate [Co(NO₃)₂] and lanthanum nitrate in ratios corresponding to dopant concentrations (x = 0, 1, 5, 10).  
  – Conditions: The pH is maintained near neutral using a pH meter and titration setup, ensuring precise reagent concentrations and uniform mixing with the catalyst support (Al₂O₃, pre‑treated by prior calcination).  
  – Equipment: pH meter, magnetic stirrer.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Process: Following the initial mixing, the precipitating agent (NH₄OH) is added dropwise to initiate the precipitation–deposition step, securing a uniform deposition of the active metals on the support.  
  – Observation: A distinct color change and gradual precipitate formation confirm the effective deposition of the metal precursors.  
  – Equipment: Magnetic stirrer and titration setup for controlled addition.

3. • Drying  
  – Conditions: The impregnated support is dried at 80°C overnight (or at 100°C for 6 hours, as specified in alternative examples) to remove excess solvent without degrading the deposited layer.  
  – Equipment: Drying oven.

4. • Calcination  
  – Conditions: Calcination is performed at 400°C for 3 hours (or alternative optimized profiles such as 450°C for 5 hours), which enhances the metal–support interaction and consolidates the catalyst framework.  
  – Atmosphere: The process is conducted under ambient air unless otherwise specified.  
  – Equipment: Calcination furnace.

5. • Post‑treatment (Reduction)  
  – Process: A reduction step is executed under a controlled H₂/CO atmosphere, where precise control over temperature and gas flow ensures the desired catalyst structure and activity.  
  – Equipment: Flow controllers and a reduction reactor ensure that the active phase is reduced efficiently while minimizing oxidation.

This detailed, step‑by‑step protocol—with emphasis on exact numerical values and reproducible conditions—facilitates direct replication and further comparative studies of Fischer–Tropsch catalyst synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Co/Al₂O₃-x%La Catalyst

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • Cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (e.g., 10.0 g)  
  • Aluminum nitrate nonahydrate (e.g., 15.0 g)  
  • Lanthanum nitrate (dosed to achieve x = 0, 1, 5, or 10 wt% depending on the target formulation)  
  • 50 mL deionized water  
  • 1 M NaOH solution for pH adjustment  
- **Conditions**:  
  Temperature maintained at 25°C; stirring at 300 RPM; pH adjusted to 9 using dropwise addition of NaOH.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve cobalt nitrate and aluminum nitrate in deionized water to obtain a clear homogeneous solution.  
  2. Introduce the lanthanum nitrate solution slowly to achieve the desired dopant level while continuously stirring.  
  3. Gradually add the 1 M NaOH solution dropwise until the pH reaches 9. Continue stirring for an additional 30 minutes to ensure complete precipitation of the hydroxides.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Ensure the uniform dissolution of metal nitrates and precise pH control to promote a consistent precursor formation.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**:  
  Utilize the synthesized precursor slurry along with pre-treated Al₂O₃ support.  
- **Conditions**:  
  Employ a wet impregnation method ensuring intimate contact between the precursors and the support.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Mix the precursor solution with the Al₂O₃ support thoroughly under constant stirring for 45 minutes to promote uniform dispersion of the active metal phase.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Monitor the mixing process to avoid any inhomogeneity in metal distribution.

##### Step [3]: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Purification**:  
  Centrifuge the slurry at 3000 RPM for 10 minutes and wash the precipitate three times with deionized water to remove residual ions.  
- **Drying**:  
  Place the washed material in a vacuum oven at 120°C for 12 hours to eliminate moisture.  
- **Calcination**:  
  Heat in air with a ramp rate of 5°C/min to a final temperature of 600°C, holding for 4 hours to enhance metal-support interactions.

##### Step [4]: Activation
- **Process**:  
  Subject the calcined catalyst to a reduction step in a H₂/CO flow (maintaining 50 mL/min) by ramping the temperature from room temperature to 300°C over 2 hours.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Monitor gas flow and temperature profiles carefully to ensure complete reduction and avoid agglomeration.

This detailed workflow ensures that all critical parameters—from precursor dissolution through to final activation—are controlled, enabling reproducibility and optimization of Fischer–Tropsch catalytic performance.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural features of the Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD). This technique identifies the presence and dispersion of active metal phases, which are critical for catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis revealed distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic cobalt (Co), cobalt oxides (CoO, Co₃O₄), and, in the case of iron-based catalysts, iron carbides (Fe₅C₂). The positions and intensities of these peaks, recorded over a range of 2θ angles, confirmed the formation of well-defined crystalline phases. The average crystallite size was calculated using the Scherrer equation, with values indicating that higher dopant levels (e.g., La at x = 10) led to increased particle size and reduced dispersion.
- **Observations:** Higher calcination temperatures and longer durations resulted in sharper, more intense peaks, signifying improved crystallinity. ### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the efficiency of the catalyst in transforming carbon monoxide into products. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** CO conversion was determined for catalysts with varying promoter (La) content (x = 0, 1, 5, 10) under controlled temperature, pressure, GHSV, and H₂/CO ratios. Gas chromatography was used to analyze inlet and outlet gas compositions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The data reveal that CO conversion increases with the introduction of the promoter, reaching an optimum at intermediate La content (x = 5), after which further increases in promoter level do not yield significant improvements. This trend underscores the importance of promoter optimization for maximizing conversion efficiency.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the fraction of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift and oxidation side reactions. It is defined as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity was measured for each catalyst composition by quantifying CO₂ in the product stream via gas chromatography.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing promoter content modulates CO₂ selectivity, with the lowest values observed at optimal promoter levels (x = 5). This suggests that appropriate promoter loading suppresses undesired CO₂ formation, favoring hydrocarbon synthesis.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity indicates the proportion of methane among the products, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane yields were determined for all catalyst variants under identical reaction conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** CH₄ selectivity decreases as promoter content increases, with the lowest methane formation at x = 5. This demonstrates that promoter addition effectively suppresses methane production, enhancing the selectivity toward longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The selectivity toward C₃⁺ hydrocarbons was quantified for each catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** C₃⁺ selectivity increases with promoter addition, peaking at x = 5, indicating enhanced chain growth and a favorable product distribution for liquid fuel applications.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution. Values range from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (maximum chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** α values were calculated from product distributions for each catalyst composition.
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor increases with promoter content up to x = 5, correlating with higher C₃⁺ selectivity and lower methane formation. This demonstrates that optimal promoter loading enhances the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time, expressed as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate (h}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Time-on-stream measurements tracked CO conversion decline for each catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts with optimal promoter content (x = 5) exhibited the lowest deactivation rates, indicating superior stability. Both under- and over-promoted catalysts showed faster deactivation, highlighting the critical role of promoter optimization in maintaining long-term activity.
