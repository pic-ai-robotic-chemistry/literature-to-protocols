## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fischer–Tropsch catalysts—FePₓ/SiO₂ and NiPₓ/SiO₂—were synthesized using a two‑step incipient wetness impregnation technique. This method is favored for achieving a uniform distribution of metal and phosphorus species on the pre‑calcined silica support (calcined at 773 K for 3 h). The process is outlined as follows:

#### Two‑Step Incipient Wetness Impregnation

1. **Precursor Mixing**
   - • The silica support is initially impregnated with an aqueous solution of (NH₄)₂HPO₄.
   - • No specific concentration is provided; however, this step uniformly deposits phosphorus on the support.
   - • The impregnated support is then dried in a controlled drying oven at 393 K for 8 hours.

2. **Secondary Impregnation**
   - • The phosphorus-coated support is subsequently impregnated with a metal nitrate solution (Fe(NO₃)₃ for iron-based and Ni(NO₃)₂ for nickel-based catalysts).
   - • Following this, the support is dried again at 393 K for another 8 hours to remove excess moisture and ensure even distribution.

3. **Calcination**
   - • The dried, doubly-impregnated material is calcined in a furnace at 773 K for 4 hours.
   - • This high-temperature treatment supports the transformation of the impregnated species into metal phosphate precursors.

4. **Reduction via TPR**
   - • The calcined sample undergoes temperature‑programmed reduction (TPR) in a 10% H₂/Ar atmosphere.
   - • The reduction is performed by first ramping from room temperature to 623 K at 5 K/min, then from 623 K to 973 K at 1 K/min, with a 2-hour hold at 973 K.
   - • Post‑reduction, samples are passivated in a 1% O₂/Ar flow for 4 hours to stabilize the phases before Fischer–Tropsch operation.

### Comparative Analysis

This method offers advantages in terms of catalyst uniformity and reproducibility, which are critical for controlling phase compositions such as Fe₂P, Fe₃P, metallic Fe (transforming to Fe₅C₂), and various Ni phosphides (Ni₂P, Ni₃P, Ni₁₂P₅). The two‑step impregnation minimizes the risk of inhomogeneous metal dispersion compared to deposition–precipitation approaches, which were not specifically employed here. However, the complexity of sequential drying and precise temperature control during calcination and TPR may pose challenges for scalability.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature and Heating Ramp
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for the reduction of metal phosphate precursors into active phosphide phases and, in the case of iron catalysts, for initiating the transformation of metallic Fe into the active iron carbide phase (Fe₅C₂).
- **Experimental Conditions:** The temperature-programmed reduction involves a two-step heating process. Initially, the precursors are heated from room temperature to 623 K at a rate of 5 K/min. This is followed by a slower ramp of 1 K/min from 623 K to a final temperature of 973 K, at which the catalyst is maintained.
- **Observations:** The controlled ramping facilitates uniform reduction and minimizes sintering, ensuring the proper development of the desired phosphide phases.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The choice of activation gas is essential as it determines the efficiency of the reduction. A H₂-rich atmosphere provides the necessary active atomic hydrogen for effective precursor reduction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** During the reduction step, pure H₂ is employed to ensure complete conversion of the metal phosphate precursors. Following reduction, a gentle passivation in a 1% O₂/Ar mixture is applied; however, the passivation step is distinctly separate from the primary activation process.
- **Observations:** The reducing conditions achieved with pure H₂ help remove oxygen-containing species and support the formation of active catalytic phases.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process is vital to guarantee complete phase transformation while controlling catalyst sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** At the final temperature of 973 K, the catalyst is held for 2 hours under flowing H₂.
- **Observations:** This extended dwell period allows sufficient time for phase transformation in both FePₓ/SiO₂ and NiPₓ/SiO₂ systems, ensuring that the active phases are fully developed without detrimental over-sintering effects.

*Note:* Although a gas hourly space velocity of 5000 h⁻¹ is specified for the subsequent reaction phase in the fixed-bed reactor, no explicit activation pressure or space-time velocity parameters are reported during the reduction step.

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction section details the conditions under which the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was evaluated, ensuring reproducibility and clarity for subsequent experiments.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter for syngas activation and catalytic performance. In the current study, the reactor was maintained at a constant temperature of 553 K during the CO hydrogenation tests. Prior to the reaction, the catalyst was pre-conditioned by cooling to 553 K in a H₂ flow before switching the gas feed to syngas. This controlled temperature promotes the effective dissociation of H₂ and CO, supporting the transformation of metallic Fe into the active iron carbide phase (Fe₅C₂) and ensuring the stable performance of the phosphide phases.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Maintaining a fixed reaction pressure is essential for managing syngas concentration and product selectivity. The experiments were conducted in a fixed-bed reactor at a pressure of 5.0 MPa. This pressure level not only fosters enhanced reaction kinetics by increasing the reactant concentration but also influences the balance between hydrocarbon and oxygenate formation. The design of the reactor facilitates continuous control of the 5.0 MPa pressure, thus ensuring uniform operating conditions throughout the experiment.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The molar feed ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide directly affects the distribution of catalytic products. A H₂/CO ratio of 2 was employed, which is pivotal for modulating the reaction pathway. In the case of FePₓ/SiO₂ catalysts, this ratio promotes the formation of oxygenates with methanol as the major product, while NiPₓ/SiO₂ catalysts yield primarily methanol. The selected ratio helps balance the hydrogenation and CO dissociation steps, thereby fine-tuning the overall selectivity.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is used to quantify the exposure of the catalyst to the reactant flow. A GHSV value of 5000 h⁻¹ was maintained throughout the catalytic evaluations. This high flow rate minimizes the residence time of syngas over the catalyst, which can influence CO conversion and product distribution. Consistent application of these parameters ensures that the catalytic tests are both reproducible and reflective of optimal Fischer–Tropsch operating conditions.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Stability
- **Description:** Phase identification and stability were assessed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a PANalytical X'Pert-Pro diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. XRD provides insight into the crystalline phases present in both fresh and spent catalysts, crucial for understanding active site formation and catalyst durability under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:**  
  - For FePₓ/SiO₂ catalysts, XRD patterns reveal that FeP₁/₂/SiO₂ contains an almost pure Fe₂P phase with minor Fe₃P, both of which remain stable after CO hydrogenation. In FeP₁/₄/SiO₂, metallic Fe, Fe₂P, and Fe₃P are present in the fresh catalyst; under syngas, most metallic Fe transforms into iron carbide (Fe₅C₂), while Fe₂P and Fe₃P persist. FeP₁/₈/SiO₂ shows increased metallic Fe and Fe₃P as the main phosphide after reaction.
  - For NiPₓ/SiO₂, NiP₁/₂/SiO₂ exhibits Ni₂P and Ni₁₂P₅ as dominant phases, with a characteristic broad peak at 47° (overlapping Ni₂P at 47.4° and Ni₁₂P₅ at 46.9°). Increasing the P:Ni ratio to 1:4 and 1:8 leads to the formation of Ni₃P. XRD patterns remain unchanged after reaction, indicating phase stability.
- **Observations:** The transformation of metallic Fe to Fe₅C₂ under syngas is linked to enhanced hydrocarbon formation, while the stability of phosphide phases supports sustained oxygenate production. In Ni-based catalysts, phase transitions with varying P:Ni ratios modulate catalytic selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Reduction Behavior (TPR Analysis)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was conducted on a Micromeritics Autochem 2910 to elucidate the reduction pathways of metal phosphate precursors, with H₂ consumption monitored by a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Findings:**  
  - For FePₓ/SiO₂, three reduction stages are observed: Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ at 650 K, Fe₃O₄ to FeO at 750 K, and FeO to Fe at 870 K. Iron phosphate reduction proceeds via Fe₃(P₂O₇)₂ and Fe₂P₂O₇ intermediates before forming elemental Fe and P.
  - For NiPₓ/SiO₂, the first reduction peak at 610 K corresponds to nickel species, while a higher temperature peak (850–925 K) is attributed to PO-bond reduction and phosphide formation. Lower P:Ni ratios shift the PO-bond reduction peak to lower temperatures (by up to 75 K), indicating facilitated reduction.
- **Observations:** The reduction profiles demonstrate that phosphorus content influences reduction ease and phase evolution, with lower P favoring metallic phase formation and subsequent carbide generation in Fe systems.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity (BET/BJH Analysis)
- **Description:** Textural properties were determined by nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms using the BET method for surface area and the BJH method for pore size distribution.
- **Findings:**  
  - Surface areas (m²/g): FeP₁/₂/SiO₂: 120; FeP₁/₄/SiO₂: 124; FeP₁/₈/SiO₂: 133; NiP₁/₂/SiO₂: 134; NiP₁/₄/SiO₂: 147; NiP₁/₈/SiO₂: 149. The SiO₂ support is 180 m²/g.

- **Observations:** The incorporation of metal phosphides reduces both surface area and pore diameter relative to the support, likely due to partial pore blockage. This may impact reactant diffusion and accessibility of active sites.

#### 6.1.4 CO Adsorption Properties (CO-TPD)
- **Description:** CO temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD) was used to probe CO adsorption strength and surface reactivity, with desorbed species monitored by mass spectrometry.
- **Findings:**  
  - For FePₓ/SiO₂, desorption peaks at 366 K (weakly adsorbed CO) and 859 K (strongly adsorbed CO) are observed. For NiPₓ/SiO₂, peaks appear at 372 K and 722–756 K, with the high-temperature peak shifting higher as P:Ni decreases.
  - CO₂ formation during high-temperature desorption indicates CO disproportionation (2CO → CO₂ + C) on the catalyst surface.
- **Observations:** Strong CO adsorption and the occurrence of CO disproportionation are more pronounced in Fe-based catalysts, correlating with their higher hydrocarbon selectivity. In Ni-based systems, increased phosphorus content weakens CO adsorption, favoring methanol production.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{\sum (n_i \times M_i)}{M_{\text{CO}}} \times 100\%
  \]
  where \( n_i \) is the number of carbon atoms in product \( i \), \( M_i \) is the mole number of product \( i \), and \( M_{\text{CO}} \) is the total moles of CO in the feed.
- **Experimental Data:** Under syngas conditions (553 K, 5.0 MPa, H₂:CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 h⁻¹), the CO conversion values are:
  - FeP₁/₂/SiO₂: 2.0%
  - FeP₁/₄/SiO₂: 6.5%
  - FeP₁/₈/SiO₂: 11.5%
  - Fe/SiO₂: 19.2%
  - NiP₁/₂/SiO₂: 1.3%
  - NiP₁/₄/SiO₂: 2.5%
  - NiP₁/₈/SiO₂: 4.6%
  - Ni/SiO₂: 14.5%
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower phosphorus content (lower P:metal ratio) leads to higher CO conversion for both Fe- and Ni-based catalysts, attributed to increased metallic sites that enhance CO dissociation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity

  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{n_{\text{CO}_2} \times M_{\text{CO}_2}}{\sum (n_i \times M_i)} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**

  - NiP₁/₈/SiO₂: 4.0%
  - Ni/SiO₂: 11.5%
- **Observations and Trends:** For Fe-based catalysts, CO₂ selectivity increases as phosphorus content decreases. Ni-based catalysts show lower CO₂ selectivity overall, with a notable increase for pure Ni.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{X_0 - X_t}{X_0 \times t} \times 100\% \quad (\text{per hour})
  \]
  where \( X_0 \) and \( X_t \) are initial and final CO conversions, and \( t \) is the time interval (typically 24 h).
- **Experimental Data:** Fe-based catalysts exhibit higher deactivation rates than Ni-based ones, due to phase transformation (e.g., Fe to Fe₅C₂) and increased hydrocarbon formation. Ni-based phosphides, especially at higher P:Ni ratios, show greater stability.
- **Observations and Trends:** Proper control of phosphorus content and reduction conditions is critical for minimizing deactivation and maintaining performance.

---
**Summary:**  
Catalyst performance is strongly influenced by phosphorus content, which modulates CO conversion, product selectivity, and stability. Fe-based catalysts with lower P:Fe ratios favor hydrocarbon chain growth and higher CO conversion but also higher deactivation rates. Ni-based phosphides are more selective for methanol and exhibit superior stability, with methanation suppressed at higher P:Ni ratios. These trends underscore the importance of phase composition and electronic modification in tailoring Fischer–Tropsch catalyst performance.
