## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ and its Mn‑promoted variant is based on a well‑defined series of steps that ensure reproducibility and controlled catalyst properties. Below is a detailed description of the experimental procedures extracted from the article:

#### Step 1: Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation
• The process begins by mixing an iron–aluminium alloy powder (50:50 by weight) with an 8 mol L⁻¹ KOH solution in a flask under constant stirring.  
• The mixture is heated to 70°C so that aluminium is effectively dissolved from the alloy.  
• After dissolution, the mixture undergoes an extensive washing procedure—deionized water is used ten times followed by ethanol being applied seven times—to remove K⁺ and AlO₂⁻ residues.

#### Step 2: Precipitation–Deposition (and Promoter Incorporation)
• For the Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ variant, a Mn(NO₃)₂ solution is introduced to the washed Raney iron precursor to achieve a promoter loading of 10 wt%.  
• The controlled dropwise addition of the promoter solution is carried out under vigorous stirring, ensuring uniform deposition before proceeding.

#### Step 3: Drying
• The precursor (with or without the Mn promoter) is then dried in a vacuum oven under an argon atmosphere at room temperature for 12 hours.  
• This step ensures complete removal of residual solvents while maintaining an inert environment to prevent premature oxidation.

#### Step 4: Post‑treatment: Passivation and Carburization
• Following drying, the material is passivated by exposure to a 1% O₂ in He atmosphere at room temperature for 20 hours using a dedicated setup.  
• Final in situ carburization is performed in a stainless‑steel tubular fixed‑bed reactor. The temperature is ramped at 1°C/min until reaching 350°C, where the material is held for 6 hours under a controlled syngas flow (H₂/CO/He or H₂/CO/Ar). This controlled heating transforms the precursor into phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ without introducing secondary phases.

A comparative analysis of these steps highlights advantages such as targeted phase formation and uniform promoter distribution, while also addressing potential complexities in washing and precise atmosphere control. These evidently contribute to catalyst reproducibility and performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction involves two sequential stages—passivation followed by in situ carburization—to convert the Raney iron precursor into phase‐pure χ‑Fe₅C₂. This section details the precise activation parameters as reported in the experimental study.

#### Activation Temperature and Ramp Rate
During the carburization step, the catalyst is heated to 350 °C. A controlled ramp rate of 0.5 °C per minute (with some interpretation allowing up to 1 °C/min in comparable runs) is applied to ensure uniform heating and avoid rapid temperature fluctuations that may induce unwanted phase formations. The controlled ramping facilitates a gradual transformation, thereby promoting high metal dispersion and minimizing sintering effects.

#### Activation Time
The activation procedure is divided into two time-critical steps. Initially, the catalyst undergoes passivation at room temperature for 20 hours under a 1% O₂ in He atmosphere. This step creates a protective oxide layer on the catalyst surface to prevent uncontrolled oxidation before carburization. Following passivation, the catalyst is subjected to an extended carburization process, maintaining the temperature at 350 °C for a dwell period of 6 hours. This prolonged heating allows sufficient time for carbon to diffuse into the iron and complete the phase transformation.

#### Activation Gas Composition
The gas composition is critical in both steps. For passivation, a dilute oxygen stream in helium (1% O₂ in He) is used to form a controlled oxide layer. In the carburization phase, the catalyst is exposed to a syngas mixture typically comprising H₂, CO, and an inert diluent (He or Ar). The controlled syngas environment is essential to ensure an optimal H₂/CO ratio, which drives the full conversion of the precursor into the desired χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase.

#### Activation Pressure and Gas Flow
All activation steps are performed under ambient pressure. During carburization, a high space-time velocity—reported as approximately 75,000 mL/(g_cat·h)—ensures uniform exposure to the reactive gas mixture, further promoting consistent catalyst activation.

These optimized activation conditions collectively contribute to a phase-pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalyst with enhanced performance, characterized by high CO conversion and favorable selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis as it governs the syngas activation and overall catalyst performance. In the described experiments, the temperature was carefully controlled within a range of 250–320 °C. This range was selected because the catalyst exhibited stable activity over extended time-on-stream (up to 200 hours), ensuring a sustained CO conversion and high yield of targeted C₂–C₁₀ linear α-olefins. Notably, higher temperatures within this range promoted an exponential increase in CO conversion and hydrocarbon formation rates.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Reaction pressure plays a significant role by affecting both the concentration of syngas and product selectivity. The experiments were conducted at pressures of 2.3 MPa and 2.5 MPa. These conditions were chosen to optimize syngas utilization while simultaneously suppressing undesired carbon deposition. Specifically, operating at a pressure of 2.5 MPa was found to improve the catalyst’s stability and to help fine-tune the hydrocarbon product distribution, which is essential for maintaining an operable olefin-to-paraffin ratio.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The H₂/CO ratio used in the Fischer–Tropsch tests directly influences the product distribution. In these experiments, two ratios were employed: 1.5 and 2.0. A ratio of 1.5 is indicative of commercial syngas feed and was observed to balance the formation of light olefins while minimizing excessive methane production. Conversely, a ratio of 2.0 tends to favor higher methane selectivity. The deliberate selection of these ratios allows for a clear evaluation of their impact on catalyst performance and process efficiency.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
Space-time velocity (SV) is defined as the volumetric flow rate of reactants per unit mass of catalyst, expressed in mL/(g_cat·h). The reported experiments utilized SVs ranging from 5,000 to 60,000 mL/(g_cat·h). Higher space velocities were associated with enhanced CO conversion, although careful optimization was required to ensure that the shorter residence time did not adversely impact the formation of desired light olefins.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and structural evolution of the catalysts were determined using in situ X-ray Diffraction (XRD) with Rietveld refinement (Fullprof software), complemented by high-resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HR-TEM) and Selected-Area Electron Diffraction (SAED).
- **Findings:** In situ XRD patterns reveal that the transformation from passivated Raney iron to phase-pure χ-Fe₅C₂ initiates at 300°C and is completed after 6 hours at 350°C under a syngas atmosphere. Rietveld refinement confirms the exclusive presence of the χ-Fe₅C₂ phase, with no detectable secondary phases. The refined lattice parameters and space group are consistent with published data for χ-Fe₅C₂. Scherrer analysis of the XRD data yields an average crystallite size of approximately 17 nm.
- **Observations:** The high phase purity and well-defined crystallite size are directly linked to the optimized carburization protocol, which employs a high H₂/CO ratio and controlled temperature ramping. The absence of impurity phases is critical for achieving high catalytic activity and stability.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Lattice Structure
- **Description:** Morphological features and lattice structure were examined by HR-TEM and SAED, providing direct visualization of the catalyst’s nanostructure and crystallinity.
- **Findings:** HR-TEM images show that the phase-pure χ-Fe₅C₂ grains exhibit a characteristic lattice spacing of about 2.7 Å along the (311) direction. The initial passivated Raney iron consists of crystalline iron particles surrounded by an amorphous oxide layer, which is fully converted to χ-Fe₅C₂ upon carburization. SAED patterns confirm the single-phase nature of the product.
- **Observations:** The uniform and well-ordered lattice structure, together with the absence of significant amorphous carbon deposition, supports the catalyst’s high activity and resistance to deactivation.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Promoter Distribution
- **Description:** The elemental composition and spatial distribution of the Mn promoter were assessed using scanning transmission electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDS).
- **Findings:** STEM-EDS mapping demonstrates a homogeneous distribution of Mn (10 wt%) on the χ-Fe₅C₂ surface, both before and after extended Fischer–Tropsch operation (up to 160 hours). No evidence of Mn migration or agglomeration is observed.
- **Observations:** The stable and uniform Mn dispersion is essential for suppressing carbon deposition and maintaining catalyst performance over time.

#### 6.1.4 Phase Stability under Reaction Conditions
- **Description:** In situ Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to monitor the phase composition and stability of the catalyst during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra confirm that only the χ-Fe₅C₂ (Hägg carbide) phase is present during and after prolonged reaction, with no detectable transformation to other iron phases.
- **Observations:** The exclusive presence and stability of χ-Fe₅C₂ under reaction conditions underpin the catalyst’s long-term activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Deposition Assessment
- **Description:** The extent of carbon deposition was evaluated by HR-TEM imaging of spent catalysts.
- **Findings:** HR-TEM reveals minimal amorphous carbon on the surface of phase-pure χ-Fe₅C₂, in contrast to significant carbon buildup observed on phase-impure samples.
- **Observations:** The suppression of carbon deposition, particularly in Mn-promoted catalysts, is directly correlated with enhanced catalyst stability and resistance to deactivation.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The χ‐Fe₅C₂ catalyst achieves a CO conversion of 27.8% at 250 °C and 2.3 MPa, while the Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ (10 wt% Mn) shows 16.0% under identical conditions. Increasing the pressure to 3.0 MPa and adjusting the space velocity (SV) to 5,000 ml g_cat⁻¹ h⁻¹ raises the CO conversion for Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ to 46.1%. Across all tested conditions, CO conversion values range from 16.0% to 46.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher temperatures and pressures generally enhance CO conversion, consistent with kinetic expectations. The addition of Mn slightly reduces conversion at lower temperatures due to partial site blocking but enables higher conversion at optimized conditions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 250 °C and 2.3 MPa, χ‐Fe₅C₂ exhibits a CO₂ selectivity of 11.2%, while Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ achieves 9.3%. Under various conditions, CO₂ selectivity for Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ remains low (9–11%), significantly outperforming reference catalysts (37–47%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese promotion effectively suppresses CO₂ formation, enhancing carbon efficiency toward hydrocarbon products.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity is consistently lower than for higher hydrocarbons and increases with temperature. The (100) surface of χ‐Fe₅C₂ demonstrates lower CH₄ selectivity compared to other iron carbides.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both catalyst composition and reaction temperature influence CH₄ selectivity, with Mn promotion favoring lower methane yields.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ catalyst achieves nearly 50% carbon-based selectivity toward C₂–C₁₀ light olefins, with C₃⁺ selectivity reflecting this high efficiency.
- **Observations and Trends:** Product distributions align with the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, and Mn promotion increases selectivity toward valuable light olefins.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination, derived from the ASF distribution:
  \[
  \ln\left(\frac{W_n}{n}\right) = n \ln \alpha + \ln\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{\alpha}\right)
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with n carbon atoms.
- **Experimental Data:** The α factor is higher for Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂, correlating with increased olefin yields. The value decreases with rising temperature, indicating a shift toward shorter hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced α values with Mn promotion support improved chain growth and olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over extended time-on-stream.
- **Experimental Data:** Both χ‐Fe₅C₂ and Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ maintain stable CO conversion and selectivity for up to 200 h at 320–325 °C and 2.3 MPa, with no significant deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalysts exhibit excellent long-term stability, attributed to phase purity and resistance to carbon deposition, especially with Mn promotion.
