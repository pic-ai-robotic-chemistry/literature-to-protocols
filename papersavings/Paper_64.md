# Effects of Zn, Cu, and K promoters on the structure and on the reduction, carburization, and catalytic behavior of iron-based Fischer–Tropsch synthesis catalysts

## 1. Materials

### 1.1 Reagents

#### 1.1.1 Metal Precursors
- **Iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O)**: High purity (99.9+%), used in a 3.0 M solution for co-precipitation to form the active Fe₂O₃ phase, supplying Fe for the catalyst.

- **Deionized water**: High purity, used as the solvent for preparing aqueous impregnation solutions for both Cu(NO₃)₂ and K₂CO₃, ensuring minimal impurities that might affect catalyst performance.

## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the two‑step synthesis procedure utilized for the Fe₂O₃–Zn–K–Cu Fischer–Tropsch catalyst. The method integrates a co‑precipitation step to form a homogeneous oxide precursor, followed by an impregnation step to introduce the promoters, ensuring reproducibility and scalability.

#### Co‑precipitation Method

• Step 1 – Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
 - Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ (3.0 M) and Zn(NO₃)₂ (1.4 M) are simultaneously mixed in water.  
 - Precipitating agent: (NH₄)₂CO₃ is added while a pH meter monitors the reaction, maintaining a controlled pH of 7.0 ± 0.1.  
 - Advantages: This step offers a homogeneous formation of porous Fe–Zn oxyhydroxide powders, crucial for subsequent phase evolution.  
 - Observations: The gradual formation of a precipitate indicates successful nucleation of the oxide precursor.

• Step 2 – Precipitation–Deposition  
 - Following precursor mixing, potential precipitation–deposition is employed to further stabilize the structure before drying.

• Step 3 – Drying  
 - Conditions: The precipitated powders are dried in ambient air at 393 K overnight.  
 - Outcome: Removal of moisture and stabilization of the precursor morphology prior to calcination.

• Step 4 – Calcination  
 - Process: Dried powders are calcined in flowing dry air at temperatures ranging from 623 K to 673 K.  
 - Function: This thermal treatment promotes the crystallization of Fe₂O₃ and the formation of ZnFe₂O₄, thereby enhancing structural integrity and inhibiting sintering.

#### Impregnation Method

• Step 5 – Promoter Introduction via Impregnation  
 - Reagents: Aqueous solutions of K₂CO₃ and Cu(NO₃)₂, both at 0.16 M, are used to impregnate the calcined powder.  
 - Purpose: The impregnation step ensures a uniform distribution of Cu and K, which are essential for accelerating reduction and improving catalytic selectivity.  
 - Advantages: Compared to a single‑step approach, the two‑step method permits precise control over promoter dispersion and reaction kinetics.

• Step 6 – Post‑impregnation Drying and Thermal Treatment  
 - Drying: The impregnated catalyst is again dried at 393 K in ambient air to remove excess solvent.  
 - Final Treatment: A further calcination at 673 K for 4 hours decomposes the nitrate precursors and finalizes the promoter activation.

This detailed methodology, with controlled reagent concentrations, pH regulation, and systematic thermal treatments, enhances reproducibility and facilitates scaling for practical Fischer–Tropsch synthesis applications.

##### 3.1.1.2 Catalyst B

This controlled addition guarantees that the nucleation and growth of the precursor powders occur under reproducible conditions.

Overall, these systematically documented procedures—detailing reagent identities, precise amounts, transfer methods, and mixing conditions—collectively enhance the reproducibility of the catalyst synthesis process as required for a rigorous Fischer–Tropsch experimental setup.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₂O₃–Zn–K–Cu Fischer–Tropsch Catalyst

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation – Co-precipitation of Fe–Zn Oxyhydroxide  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Fe(NO₃)₃ solution (3.0 M) and Zn(NO₃)₂ solution (1.4 M) mixed to achieve Zn/Fe ratios between 0 and 0.4.  
  • (NH₄)₂CO₃ added as the precipitating agent.  
  • Deionized water as the solvent.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Reaction temperature: 353 K (maintained via a controlled liquid pump introducing the nitrate mixture).  
  • pH controlled at 7.0 ± 0.1 using an Omega PHB-62 pH meter, with (NH₄)₂CO₃ added at a constant rate.  
- **Process**:  
  1. In a stirred flask, introduce Fe(NO₃)₃ and Zn(NO₃)₂ solutions into deionized water.  
  2. Simultaneously add (NH₄)₂CO₃ while monitoring pH to maintain 7.0 ± 0.1.  
  3. Allow precipitation to proceed, then wash the precipitate five times (using approximately 1000 cm³ water per gram of powder) to remove soluble impurities.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensuring homogeneous mixing and precise pH control is critical for reproducibility.

##### Step [2]: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  • Wash the precipitated powders and dry in ambient air at 393 K overnight to remove moisture.  
- **Calcination**:  
  • Heat the dried powders in flowing dry air at 623 K for 1 hour, converting the precursors into crystalline Fe₂O₃ and ZnFe₂O₄ phases.

##### Step [3]: Catalyst Assembly – Promoter Impregnation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Aqueous solutions of K₂CO₃ and Cu(NO₃)₂, each prepared at 0.16 M to achieve target atomic ratios of K/Fe (up to 0.04) and Cu/Fe (up to 0.02).  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Impregnate the calcined powders at ambient conditions; subsequent drying at 393 K and thermal treatment at 673 K for 4 hours in dry air.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Impregnate with K₂CO₃ and/or Cu(NO₃)₂ to ensure even distribution of promoters.  
  2. Dry the impregnated samples at 393 K in ambient air.  
  3. Calcine again at 673 K to fully decompose nitrate species, forming CuO while preserving residual K₂CO₃.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Maintain the sequential order from precursor formation to promoter addition to preserve catalyst structure.  
  • Verify phase transitions with in situ XRD and BET surface area analysis.  
  • Final pellet pressing (443 MPa) and sieving (100–180 µm) ensure consistent particle sizing for reproducible catalytic performance.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**:  
  XRD analysis was conducted to identify the crystalline phases present in the catalyst. The procedure involved the preparation of the catalyst by repeated washing of the precipitated powders with deionized water, followed by drying at 393 K overnight and a treatment in flowing dry air at 623 K for 1 hour. The resultant sample was then analyzed to detect phases such as hematite (Fe₂O₃) and ZnFe₂O₄.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  D‑5000 Siemens diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Cu Kα radiation was used (λ = 1.5406 Å). Although the specific scan range was not detailed, the analysis was carried out under ambient conditions after the prescribed thermal treatments.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Standard sample mounting and alignment procedures were followed to ensure accurate diffraction pattern acquisition.
- **Key Notes**:  
  Proper sample preparation and alignment are critical to avoid measurement errors in phase identification.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**:  
  The H₂‑TPR method was applied to assess the reduction behavior of the catalyst. Initially, 0.2 g of the sample was loaded into a quartz cell. A pretreatment in 20% O₂ in Ar was performed by heating the sample to 673 K at a ramping rate of 0.33 K/s and holding for 900 seconds, then cooling to ambient temperature under Ar. The gas was then switched to 20% H₂ in Ar, and the temperature was increased to 1000 K at a ramping rate of 0.167 K/s.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  Analysis was conducted using a mass spectrometer from Leybold Inficon Instruments Co., Inc.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Gas atmosphere: first 20% O₂ in Ar during pretreatment, then 20% H₂ in Ar; gas flow rate of 0.268 mol/h.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Heating rates of 0.33 K/s (oxidative step) and 0.167 K/s (reductive step) with precise temperature ramping.
- **Key Notes**:  
  Ensuring uniform catalyst loading and accurate temperature control is essential for reliable reduction profiles.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**:  
  CO‑TPR follows a similar procedure to H₂‑TPR but employs a 20% CO in Ar gas mixture instead. The sample is heated to 1000 K at a ramping rate of 0.167 K/s to examine the reduction behavior using CO as the reductant.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  Same instrumental setup as H₂‑TPR.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Gas mixture: 20% CO in Ar; identical temperature profile as H₂‑TPR.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Heating and gas flow rate parameters consistent with the H₂‑TPR method.
- **Key Notes**:  
  CO‑TPR provides complementary reduction kinetics data to that obtained by H₂‑TPR.

### 4.4 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**:  
  BET analysis was employed to determine the surface area of the catalyst, which is crucial for understanding the effect of the Zn textural promoter.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  Autosorb 6 automated system.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Samples were evacuated at 393 K for 3 hours prior to N₂ physisorption measurements conducted at N₂’s boiling point.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Data acquisition followed the BET method, with surface areas reported in the range of approximately 53–96 m²/g depending on Zn content.
- **Key Notes**:  
  Proper degassing ensures reliable adsorption data for accurate BET surface area calculations.

### 4.5 In‑situ Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**:  
  In‑situ IR spectroscopy was used to monitor CO₂ adsorption and the formation of carbonyl species on the catalyst. After reduction in the IR cell, the feed was switched from H₂ to a CO₂ + H₂ mixture, allowing the study of surface species evolution.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  In‑situ IR cell equipped for temperature-controlled measurements.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Experiments were conducted at around 300 °C under controlled gas mixtures.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Monitoring focused on characteristic IR bands – around 2115 cm⁻¹ for CO₂ and near 2000 cm⁻¹ for carbonyl species.
- **Key Notes**:  
  The technique provides valuable insight into surface reaction mechanisms, particularly CO₂ dissociation on metallic sites.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical in driving the reduction and carburization of iron oxide precursors, ensuring the exposure of active sites by transforming Fe₂O₃ into Fe₃O₄ and subsequently forming iron carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is heated following a two-stage temperature ramp: first from 298 K to 423 K at 0.167 K/s, and then from 423 K to the activation temperature of 543 K at 0.017 K/s. Once 543 K is reached, the catalyst is held for 1 hour.
- **Observations:** This controlled heating creates a balance between achieving complete reduction and minimizing sintering, resulting in the formation of smaller, highly dispersed active crystallites.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the interaction between the synthesis gas and the catalyst, affecting the kinetics of reduction and carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is conducted under a pressure of 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at this moderate pressure replicates realistic operating conditions while ensuring efficient gas–catalyst contact without promoting excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition used during activation is essential for facilitating both reduction and carburization, thus shaping the final active phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to synthesis gas composed of approximately 62% H₂, 31% CO, and 7% N₂.
- **Observations:** This specific gas mixture effectively promotes the sequential reduction of Fe₂O₃ and formation of iron carbide phases, aiding in the development of a high concentration of active sites.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space–Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space–time velocity governs the rate at which the activation gas passes through the catalyst bed, ensuring uniform exposure.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Although an explicit numerical value is not provided, the gas flow is carefully controlled with an electronic mass-flow controller. Additionally, the catalyst is diluted with quartz granules to guarantee uniform distribution of the gas.
- **Observations:** This controlled flow prevents local overheating and ensures homogeneous activation across the catalyst bed.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is crucial to ensure complete transformation of the precursor phases into their active forms without incurring sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst remains at 543 K under synthesis gas for 1 hour.
- **Observations:** This specific time frame is sufficient to achieve full reduction and carburization, yielding a catalyst with excellent active site dispersion and stability for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter for facilitating the reduction and carburization of the Fe₂O₃–Zn–K–Cu catalyst. In this study, the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was performed at a fixed reactor temperature of 493 K. This temperature was carefully maintained using a three-zone furnace equipped with type-K movable thermocouples. The chosen temperature promotes optimal syngas activation, enhances CO conversion rates, and supports the formation of active carbide phases. Experimental observations indicate that under these conditions the catalyst exhibited steady-state performance without significant deactivation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Maintaining a high reaction pressure is essential for increasing gas solubility and shifting the equilibrium toward heavier hydrocarbon production. The experiments were conducted at a pressure of 3.16 MPa using a fixed-bed tubular reactor designed for precision control. This elevated pressure not only enhanced the overall reaction rates but also influenced product selectivity by favoring the formation of C₅⁺ hydrocarbons over lighter fractions. Continuous monitoring ensured that the reactor maintained a consistent pressure, which is critical for reproducibility and enhanced hydrocarbon yield.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio directly impacts both the catalyst activation process and the hydrocarbon distribution. A ratio of 2 was used throughout the catalytic evaluation, achieved by blending a synthesis gas mixture containing 62% H₂, 31% CO, and 7% N₂. This ratio facilitates efficient reduction of iron oxides and promotes the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons while minimizing excessive methane production. Flow rates were regulated using electronic mass-flow controllers to preserve the target ratio, ensuring consistent gas composition throughout the reaction.  
   
Collectively, these reaction conditions—493 K, 3.16 MPa, and an H₂/CO ratio of 2—create an optimized environment for reproducible Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, enhancing both catalyst activation and hydrocarbon formation in the fixed-bed reactor system.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases present in the Fe₂O₃–Zn–K–Cu Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for understanding the active phase evolution and the role of promoters.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that at Zn/Fe atomic ratios below 0.2, rhombohedral hematite (Fe₂O₃) with a corundum-type structure is the dominant phase. As the Zn/Fe ratio increases, a cubic spinel-type ZnFe₂O₄ phase emerges, coexisting with Fe₂O₃ at intermediate ratios. At the highest Zn/Fe ratio (0.4), ZnFe₂O₄ becomes the sole detectable phase. Minor phases such as CuO and ZnO are also present after promoter impregnation and calcination.
- **Observations:** The formation of ZnFe₂O₄ at higher Zn loadings inhibits sintering and provides a matrix for Fe₂O₃ crystallites, supporting enhanced textural stability.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Textural Properties
- **Description:** BET surface area analysis and N₂ physisorption were employed to assess the textural properties, which directly influence catalyst dispersion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area increases monotonically with Zn content, ranging from 53 m²/g (Zn/Fe = 0) to 96 m²/g (Zn/Fe = 0.4). This trend demonstrates the textural promotion effect of Zn, attributed to the formation of ZnFe₂O₄, which inhibits particle growth and sintering.
- **Observations:** Higher surface areas correlate with improved catalytic performance up to an optimal Zn/Fe ratio (~0.1), beyond which further increases in Zn content do not enhance activity due to the formation of unreducible ZnFe₂O₄.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction and Carburization Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) with H₂ or CO was used to evaluate the reduction and carburization kinetics, which are critical for activating the catalyst.
- **Findings:** TPR profiles show that Fe₂O₃ reduces to Fe₃O₄ at approximately 543 K, with further reduction and carburization to Fe carbides (Fe₂.₅C, Fe₃C) occurring between 543–723 K. The presence of Cu lowers the reduction temperature of Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ by about 140 K in H₂ and by 50 K in CO, due to enhanced H₂ dissociation and oxygen removal.
- **Observations:** The combined addition of Cu and K accelerates reduction and carburization, leading to smaller, more active carbide crystallites.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to analyze particle morphology and size distribution, which affect catalyst dispersion and reactivity.
- **Findings:** TEM images indicate that the average particle size varies with calcination temperature, from 10 nm (CZ500) to 113 nm (CZ1000). Zn promotion results in smaller, more uniformly dispersed crystallites, as confirmed by both TEM and BET-derived estimates.
- **Observations:** Uniform particle size and high dispersion are attributed to controlled synthesis conditions and the textural stabilization provided by ZnFe₂O₄.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition and Promoter Distribution
- **Description:** Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and chemical analysis were used to confirm the elemental composition and distribution of promoters on the catalyst surface.
- **Findings:** The catalysts maintain the intended atomic ratios of Zn/Fe (0–0.4), Cu/Fe (0–0.02), and K/Fe (0–0.04) after synthesis. EDX mapping demonstrates uniform dispersion of Fe, Zn, Cu, and K, with promoters residing mainly at the Fe oxide crystallite surfaces.
- **Observations:** The homogeneous distribution of promoters is crucial for modulating reduction behavior and catalytic selectivity.

#### 6.1.6 Chemical State of Promoters and Residual Phases
- **Description:** X-ray absorption spectroscopy and XRD were used to identify the chemical state of promoters and residual phases after calcination.
- **Findings:** After impregnation and calcination, K remains as K₂CO₃ and Cu is present as CuO. These phases are stable under the applied conditions and are confirmed by both XRD and X-ray absorption spectroscopy.
- **Observations:** The presence of K₂CO₃ and CuO is essential for promoting CO activation and enhancing the carburization process during catalyst activation.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst, typically calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet molar flow rates of CO, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (493 K, 3.16 MPa, H₂/CO = 2), Fe₂O₃–K–Cu catalysts exhibited the following CO conversion rates (mol-CO/h·g-at.-Fe) at similar conversion levels (16–18%):
  - Zn/Fe = 0: 1.52
  - Zn/Fe = 0.1: 2.40
  - Zn/Fe = 0.4: 2.63
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Zn/Fe ratio enhances CO conversion up to Zn/Fe ≈ 0.1, attributed to increased surface area and improved textural stability. Further Zn addition yields marginal gains, likely due to the formation of unreducible ZnFe₂O₄, which limits active Fe sites.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of secondary water–gas shift activity:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 16–18% CO conversion, CO₂ selectivity for Fe₂O₃–K–Cu catalysts was:
  - Zn/Fe = 0: 12.3%
  - Zn/Fe = 0.1: 12.3%
  - Zn/Fe = 0.4: 7.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of K significantly increases CO₂ selectivity due to enhanced water–gas shift activity, while higher Zn/Fe ratios reduce CO₂ selectivity, reflecting a balance between textural promotion and active phase dilution.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, reported on a CO₂-free basis:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{\sum F_{\text{hydrocarbons, out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe₂O₃–Zn–Cu catalysts, CH₄ selectivity increased from 4.8% (no Cu, no K) to 10.2% (Cu only). The addition of K suppressed this increase, maintaining lower CH₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Cu promotes CH₄ formation, but K counteracts this effect, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity denotes the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, also reported on a CO₂-free basis.
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivity exceeded 80% for most promoted catalysts, with the highest values (85–88%) observed for Fe₂O₃–Zn–K–Cu at optimal promoter ratios (K/Fe = 0.02, Cu/Fe = 0.01).
- **Observations and Trends:** K increases C₅⁺ selectivity and olefin content, while Cu alone decreases it. The combination of K and Cu maintains high C₅⁺ selectivity, supporting the formation of heavier hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, influencing product distribution. It is derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the study notes that K increases chain growth selectivity (higher α), Cu decreases it, and their combination balances the effect, maintaining high selectivity for long-chain products.
- **Observations and Trends:** Variations in Zn/Fe ratio did not significantly affect α, while K and Cu exert opposing influences that can be tuned for optimal product distribution.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is inferred from the maintenance of steady-state CO conversion and selectivity over time.
- **Experimental Data:** No significant deactivation was observed under the reported conditions, particularly for catalysts with optimal Zn content, which exhibited stable performance due to inhibited sintering and preserved surface area.
- **Observations and Trends:** The textural promotion by Zn and the balanced use of K and Cu promoters contribute to sustained catalyst activity and selectivity during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
