## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis strategy for CoCZ700 is divided into two essential stages: the preparation of the ceria–zirconia (CZ) support via co‑precipitation and the subsequent catalyst impregnation using a wet impregnation method with strong electrostatic adsorption.

#### Co‑precipitation: CZ Support Preparation
1. • Precursor Mixing  
   – Aqueous solutions of Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O and ZrOCl₂·8H₂O are combined.  
   – The supporting reagents are quantitatively managed to ensure controlled Ce and Zr ion concentrations.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   – A 30% NH₃ solution is added dropwise until the pH reaches 9, leading to the formation of the CZ precipitate.  
   – A pH meter is used for monitoring the pH to enhance reproducibility.
3. • Aging  
   – The precipitate is aged for 24 h at room temperature to promote complete precursor conversion.
4. • Drying  
   – Post-filtration and washing, the precipitate is dried in a convection oven at 110 °C overnight.
5. • Calcination  
   – The dried material is calcined in air at 700 °C for 4 h, yielding a support with an intermediate particle size of approximately 19–20 nm (denoted as CZ700).

#### Wet Impregnation: Catalyst Impregnation
1. • Precursor Mixing  
   – Cobalt acetate is dissolved in a solution of 30 wt% ammonia.  
   – This solution is mixed with 2 g of the pre-prepared CZ support.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   – The cobalt precursor solution is stirred with the CZ support for 2 h to ensure strong electrostatic adsorption and uniform deposition of cobalt.
3. • Drying  
   – The impregnated support is dried at 110 °C overnight to remove moisture.
4. • Calcination  
   – The dried material is calcined at 350 °C for 4 h to convert the cobalt precursor into a uniform Co₃O₄ phase.
5. • Reduction  
   – Finally, the catalyst is reduced in flowing H₂ at 500 °C for 4 h to generate the active metallic phase.

### Comparative Analysis
The co‑precipitation and wet impregnation methods offer clear advantages regarding reproducibility and scalability. While the co‑precipitation approach permits fine control over CZ particle size through established pH and calcination protocols, the wet impregnation method—benefiting from strong electrostatic adsorption—ensures a uniform Co₃O₄ phase formation. Equipment requirements, including pH meters, convection ovens, and controlled atmosphere furnaces, are critical for both methods, ensuring that other researchers can replicate these procedures with high fidelity.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases of the ceria–zirconia (CZ) support and cobalt species were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D2 Phaser diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. The Scherrer equation was applied to estimate crystallite sizes.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis of the CZ supports revealed a transition from monoclinic to cubic and tetragonal phases as the calcination temperature increased above 700 °C. For CoCZ700, the support is predominantly in the cubic/tetragonal phase. The cobalt oxide phase (Co₃O₄) is uniformly formed after calcination at 350 °C, with particle sizes in the 7–9 nm range before reduction. After reduction at 500 °C, the cobalt particle size in CoCZ700 remains stable, with nearly identical distributions before and after reduction, indicating strong resistance to sintering.
- **Observations:** The crystal phase of the support does not directly influence catalytic performance, as both cubic/tetragonal and exclusively tetragonal supports yield high activity. The stability of cobalt particle size in CoCZ700 is attributed to optimal metal–support interaction.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Particle Size
- **Description:** BET N₂ physisorption was used to measure the surface area and estimate the particle size of the CZ supports. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provided direct visualization of particle morphology and size distribution.
- **Findings:** The CZ700 support exhibits a BET surface area of 48 m²/g and an average particle size of 19 nm. TEM images confirm this particle size and show that, in CoCZ700, cobalt nanoparticles are well dispersed with no large agglomerates, both before and after reduction. In contrast, supports calcined at lower (CZ500) or higher (CZ900) temperatures result in significant cobalt agglomeration after reduction.
- **Observations:** The intermediate particle size of CZ700 is optimal for stabilizing cobalt nanoparticles, leading to enhanced dispersion and resistance to sintering under reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Metal–Support Interaction
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) were employed to probe the reducibility of cobalt species and the strength of metal–support interactions.
- **Findings:** The TPR profile of CoCZ700 displays a low-temperature feature (α) corresponding to partial surface reduction of ceria, and high-temperature features (γ and δ) associated with strongly interacting cobalt species and reduction at the Co–O–Ce interface. XANES confirms that the reduction of cobalt in CoCZ700 is shifted to higher temperatures, indicating strong metal–support interaction.
- **Observations:** These features are direct evidence of robust metal–support interaction, which is linked to improved catalytic activity and stability.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Distribution and Morphology
- **Description:** Scanning transmission electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDX) was used to map the spatial distribution of cobalt, cerium, and zirconium.
- **Findings:** STEM-EDX mapping shows homogeneous dispersion of cobalt on the CZ700 support, with no large agglomerates after reduction. In contrast, other supports show significant cobalt aggregation.
- **Observations:** The uniform elemental distribution in CoCZ700 is critical for maintaining high catalytic performance and stability.

#### 6.1.5 Oxygen Vacancy Formation and Interfacial Dynamics
- **Description:** In situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and near-ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (NAP-XPS) were used to monitor oxygen vacancy formation and redox dynamics at the metal–support interface.
- **Findings:** FTIR detects the Ce³⁺ band at 2,115 cm⁻¹, indicating oxygen vacancy formation during H₂ reduction. NAP-XPS shows that Ce⁴⁺ is reduced to Ce³⁺ before metallic cobalt appears, and that oxygen vacancies form readily at low temperatures in CoCZ700. Upon CO₂ exposure, these vacancies are refilled, demonstrating dynamic reverse oxygen spillover.
- **Observations:** The ability to form and heal oxygen vacancies at low temperature is a key factor in the superior catalytic performance of CoCZ700, highlighting the importance of interfacial redox processes.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO₂ at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** At 225 °C, the CoCZ700 catalyst achieves a CO₂ conversion that is 5.8 times higher than CoCZ500 and 2.9 times higher than CoCZ1000 under identical conditions (atmospheric pressure, 5% CO₂/20% H₂/He, 50 mL/min total flow). The reaction temperature range tested was 200–325 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest conversion is observed for CoCZ700, attributed to its intermediate CZ particle size (~19–20 nm) and optimized metal–support interaction. Both smaller (CZ500) and larger (CZ900/1000) support particles result in significantly lower conversion due to either excessive sintering or reduced surface area.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO₂ that is transformed into methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CoCZ700 exhibits near-complete selectivity for methane, with CH₄ selectivity values close to 100% across the tested temperature range. This indicates that almost all converted CO₂ is reduced to methane, with negligible formation of CO or higher hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior CH₄ selectivity of CoCZ700 is consistent with its high activity and is not matched by catalysts with other CZ calcination temperatures.

#### 6.2.3 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of hydrocarbon chain growth during CO₂ hydrogenation. It is defined as the probability that a growing hydrocarbon chain will add another carbon atom rather than terminate. Values close to zero indicate exclusive methane formation.
- **Experimental Data:** The α factor for CoCZ700 is reported to be very low, signifying negligible formation of long-chain hydrocarbons and confirming the catalyst’s high selectivity for methane.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low α value aligns with the observed product distribution, where methane is the dominant product and higher hydrocarbons are not detected in significant quantities.

#### 6.2.4 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring the maintenance of CO₂ conversion and resistance to cobalt nanoparticle sintering during prolonged reaction at elevated temperatures.
- **Experimental Data:** CoCZ700 maintains its initial cobalt particle size and dispersion after reduction and extended reaction at 300 °C, as confirmed by TEM and STEM-EDX analyses. No significant deactivation or loss of activity is observed during stability tests.
- **Observations and Trends:** The enhanced stability of CoCZ700 is attributed to the optimal metal–support interaction provided by the intermediate CZ particle size, which prevents cobalt agglomeration and preserves active sites. In contrast, CoCZ500 and CoCZ900 show marked sintering and reduced stability.

#### 6.2.5 Additional Performance Indicators
- **Methane Yield:** CoCZ700 delivers the highest methane yield among the tested catalysts, directly reflecting its superior CO₂ conversion and CH₄ selectivity.
- **Specific Activity:** The article notes that the specific activity (mol CO₂ converted per mol Co per second) is maximized for CoCZ700, correlating with its optimized support properties and metal dispersion.

**Summary:** The CoCZ700 catalyst, featuring a CZ support calcined at 700 °C, demonstrates outstanding CO₂ conversion, near-complete methane selectivity, minimal chain growth (low α factor), and exceptional stability under reaction conditions. These results underscore the critical role of support particle size and metal–support interaction in governing catalytic performance for CO₂ hydrogenation.
