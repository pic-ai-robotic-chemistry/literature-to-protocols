## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis methods reported in the article involve two distinct protocols: one for MIL-101(Fe)‑derived Fe@C core–shell catalysts and another for the unsupported-Fe catalyst.

#### MIL‑101(Fe)‑derived Fe@C Catalysts

• Precursor Mixing:  
 – Reagents: 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 ml nitric acid (0.074 mmol), and 70 ml DMF.  
 – Procedure: Mix under stirring for 2 hours.  
 – Equipment: Teflon‑lined steel autoclave ensures controlled conditions for hydrothermal synthesis.  

• Hydrothermal Synthesis:  
 – Conditions: 120°C for 48 hours.  
 – Role: DMF acts as a solvent and nitric acid as the mineralizer, aiding in the formation of MIL‑101(Fe).

• Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation Followed by Precipitation–Deposition:  
 – The MOF is prepared and then directly subjected to controlled post‑processing.  

• Drying & Calcination Via Pyrolysis:  
 – Pyrolysis is conducted in a tube furnace under a constant flow of 2% C₂H₂/He.  
 – Conditions: Heating rate of 5°C/min to target temperatures of 500°C, 600°C, or 700°C held for 1 hour.  
 – Observations: Temperature variation leads to different carbon shell structures (amorphous at 500°C; 3–5 layer graphene at 600°C; graphite at 700°C).

• Post‑processing Treatment – Reduction:  
 – Fe@C‑600 is further reduced in hydrogen (50 ml/min) at 400°C for 24 hours to yield Fe@C‑600‑H₂.

#### Unsupported‑Fe Catalyst by Co‑precipitation

• Precursor Mixing:  
 – Reagents: Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ solution (1 mol/L) combined with ammonia solution (3 mol/L) is added dropwise.  
 – Conditions: Stirred at 80 ± 1°C with pH maintained at 8.2–8.5, aged for 2 hours.  

• Precipitation–Deposition:  
 – This step facilitates uniform deposition of iron hydroxides prior to subsequent processing.

• Drying & Calcination:  
 – Drying: Conducted at 80°C overnight.  
 – Calcination: Performed at 500°C for 5 hours in static air followed by H₂‐treatment (100 ml/min) at 350°C for 24 hours.

A comparative analysis highlights that the hydrothermal and pyrolysis‐based route offers advantages in controlling structure and particle stabilization via the carbon shell, whereas the co‑precipitation method, although simpler, results in unsupported particles with higher aggregation propensity. These detailed and reproducible procedures ensure that experimental parameters such as reagent concentrations, temperatures, durations, and gas flow rates are rigorously maintained for replicability and optimal catalyst performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@C-600

**Step 1: Precursor Preparation – MIL-101(Fe) Synthesis**  
- **Reagents**: 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 mL nitric acid, and 70 mL N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF).  
- **Conditions**: Stir the mixture for 2 hours at room temperature for complete homogeneity. Transfer the solution to a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and perform hydrothermal treatment at 120°C for 48 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Mix all reagents until a uniform solution forms.  
  - Seal and heat in the autoclave to allow crystallization of MIL-101(Fe).  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough stirring and maintain the specified temperature and duration to obtain high crystallinity.

**Step 2: Catalyst Assembly – Pyrolysis to Form Fe@C-600**  
- **Reagents**: Use the dried MIL-101(Fe) precursor (dried at 80°C overnight).  
- **Conditions**: Pyrolyze under a gas flow of 2% C₂H₂ in helium at a heating rate of 5°C/min until reaching 600°C, hold for 1 hour.  
- **Process**:  
  - Place precursor in a tube furnace with controlled gas flow at 50 mL/min.  
  - Conduct pyrolysis to transform the MOF into a carbon-coated iron catalyst featuring a 3–5 layer graphene shell.  
- **Key Steps**: Careful temperature monitoring is crucial for achieving the desired graphene structure and h‑Fe₃C phase.

**Step 3: Activation – Hydrogen Reduction (Fe@C-600-H₂)**  
- **Reagents**: Post-pyrolysis Fe@C-600 catalyst.  
- **Conditions**: Expose to H₂ gas at 50 mL/min, reduce at 400°C for 24 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Transfer the catalyst to a reduction furnace and perform hydrogen treatment to modify the iron phase from h‑Fe₃C towards a‑Fe, impacting catalytic performance.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Unsupported-Fe

**Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Co-precipitation**  
- **Reagents**: Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ (1 mol/L) and ammonia solution (3 mol/L).  
- **Conditions**: Add Fe(NO₃)₃ dropwise under stirring at 80 ± 1°C while adjusting pH to 8.2–8.5.  
- **Process**:  
  - Allow the reactor mixture to age for 2 hours for complete precipitation.  
  - Purify by washing and filtering with distilled water.

**Step 2: Drying & Calcination**  
- **Drying**: Dry the precipitated sample at 120°C for 12 hours.  
- **Calcination**: Heat in static air at 500°C for 5 hours followed by reduction at 350°C for 24 hours under a hydrogen stream (100 mL/min).

**Key Notes and Cautions:**  
- Monitor pH during co-precipitation and maintain precise gas flow rates during both pyrolysis and reduction.  
- Consistent temperature control ensures reproducibility and prevents catalyst degradation, ensuring reliable performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 4. Characterization Methods

- **Characterization Conditions**: Room temperature conditions with the calibration ensuring accurate isomer shift determination.
- **Test Parameters**: Velocity scale and data acquisition were optimized for clear resolution of iron components.
- **Key Notes**: MES is sensitive to subtle variations in iron phases, providing insights that complement XRD findings.

### 4.4 Raman Spectroscopy

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fe@C-600-H₂ is performed via a hydrogen reduction step designed to convert the predominant h‑Fe₃C phase into the desired metallic a‑Fe phase while preserving the integrity of the carbon shell. This process is critical for achieving optimal performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis by ensuring high active site accessibility and stabilizing the catalyst structure.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** Activation temperature is crucial to drive the reduction reaction, enabling the conversion of iron carbide (h‑Fe₃C) to metallic iron (a‑Fe) without damaging the carbon shell.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is conducted at 400 °C.
- **Observations:** The chosen temperature of 400 °C is optimal; it is high enough to achieve complete reduction and transformation of the iron phase while avoiding excessive sintering or degradation of the supporting graphene shell.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The reducing gas facilitates the chemical transformation of the catalyst’s iron species. In this case, hydrogen is used as the active reducing agent.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Hydrogen (H₂) is employed during this step, with the reduction process maintained under a continuous flow.
- **Observations:** The use of hydrogen not only ensures effective reduction but also helps maintain a constant reducing atmosphere, which is essential for transforming h‑Fe₃C into a‑Fe without oxidation or incomplete reduction.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Activation time ensures that the reduction reaction is allowed to proceed to completion, transforming the catalyst phases adequately.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The hydrogen reduction treatment is performed for a duration of 24 hours.
- **Observations:** The 24-hour period ensures complete phase conversion. This prolonged exposure facilitates the formation of the active a‑Fe phase, promoting improved catalytic performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis by preserving particle dispersion and minimizing aggregation.

This carefully controlled activation process—leveraging a 400 °C temperature, a continuous hydrogen flow at 50 ml/min, and a 24-hour treatment duration—ensures the effective conversion of Fe@C-600 into Fe@C-600-H₂, thereby delivering a catalyst with improved activity, stability, and selectivity for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer-Tropsch synthesis was conducted at a reaction temperature of 300 °C. This temperature is critical for effective syngas activation, promoting both the decomposition of reactive species and the formation of active iron phases (h‑Fe₃C and a‑Fe). At 300 °C, the catalytic system demonstrated optimal performance, with catalysts such as Fe@C-600 maintaining high CO conversion rates and enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon (C₅⁺) selectivity, while also ensuring good stability during extended testing periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The experiments were carried out under a pressure of 2.0 MPa within a fixed-bed reactor. Operating at elevated pressure increases the concentration of the syngas feed, thereby favoring enhanced reaction kinetics and improved CO conversion. In this controlled setup, the higher pressure contributed to a favorable distribution of hydrocarbons, especially promoting the production of long-chain products over light gases.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
An H₂/CO ratio of 1.0 was maintained in the syngas feed. This specific ratio is instrumental in simulating conditions relevant to coal- or biomass-derived syngas, ensuring that the catalyst’s performance can be directly correlated with product distribution in terms of hydrocarbon chain length. Under these conditions, the observed performance included a desirable balance where higher conversion did not come at the expense of an excessive methane formation, particularly for catalysts with a well-formed graphene shell.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) was set at 8000 ml/(gcat·h) for the MOF-derived catalysts and 3000 ml/(gcat·h) for the unsupported-Fe catalyst. The WHSV values are essential for determining the contact time between the syngas and the catalyst, directly impacting CO conversion and the selectivity toward long-chain hydrocarbons. The precise control over the WHSV was confirmed to contribute to optimal catalyst performance by balancing conversion efficiency with catalyst stability, facilitating reproducibility across multiple experimental runs.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition of the catalysts was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases relevant to Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-700 exhibited diffraction lines corresponding to cementite (h‑Fe₃C, JCPDS 35‑0772), while Fe@C-600-H₂ showed dominant peaks for α-Fe (JCPDS 06–0696). The presence and intensity of these lines confirmed the formation and relative abundance of each phase.
- **Observations:** The dominance of h‑Fe₃C in Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-700 is associated with enhanced selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons, whereas α-Fe in Fe@C-600-H₂ correlates with higher methane selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Iron Speciation and Valence States
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES) was employed to distinguish iron species and their valence states, providing quantitative phase analysis.
- **Findings:** MES indicated that h‑Fe₃C was the predominant phase in Fe@C-500 (68.2%), Fe@C-600 (91.2%), and Fe@C-700 (95.5%), with minor contributions from α-Fe, Fe²⁺, and superparamagnetic Fe⁰. The isomer shift, quadrupole splitting, and hyperfine field parameters enabled differentiation of these species.
- **Observations:** The increasing content of h‑Fe₃C with higher pyrolysis temperature supports its role as an active phase for C₅⁺ selectivity, while the presence of α-Fe is linked to increased methane formation.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were assessed by N₂ adsorption–desorption (BET) at −196 °C, providing insights into surface area, pore volume, and average pore size, which influence catalyst dispersion and accessibility.
- **Findings:** MIL-101(Fe) exhibited a high surface area (1632 m²/g), which decreased after pyrolysis: Fe@C-500 (135 m²/g, 0.23 cm³/g, 3.3 nm), Fe@C-600 (280 m²/g, 0.27 cm³/g, 3.8 nm), Fe@C-700 (165 m²/g, 0.28 cm³/g, 3.8 nm), Fe@C-600-H₂ (291 m²/g, 0.28 cm³/g, 3.7 nm), and unsupported-Fe (15.9 m²/g, 0.04 cm³/g).
- **Observations:** The preservation of mesoporosity and higher surface area in MOF-derived catalysts facilitates better iron dispersion and reactant diffusion, correlating with improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphological features and particle size distributions were examined by SEM and TEM, with HRTEM providing detailed visualization of carbon shells and iron nanoparticles.
- **Findings:** SEM showed MIL-101(Fe) as uniform octahedra (~150 nm). TEM revealed iron nanoparticles encapsulated in carbon shells: Fe@C-500 (8.7 nm), Fe@C-600 (5.9 nm), Fe@C-700 (40 nm), Fe@C-600-H₂ (6.5 nm), and unsupported-Fe (63 nm).
- **Observations:** The smallest and most uniform particles were observed in Fe@C-600, attributed to the confinement effect of the graphene shell, which inhibits aggregation and enhances stability.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Shell Structure and Graphitization
- **Description:** The structure and graphitization degree of carbon shells were characterized by HRTEM, XRD, and Raman spectroscopy, which are critical for catalyst stability and performance.
- **Findings:** HRTEM identified amorphous carbon in Fe@C-500, concentric 3–5 layer graphene shells in Fe@C-600/Fe@C-600-H₂, and thick graphite shells in Fe@C-700. Raman spectra showed D (1346–1350 cm⁻¹), G (1580–1600 cm⁻¹), and 2D (2500–3200 cm⁻¹) bands, with ID/IG ratios of 1.10 (Fe@C-500), 0.79 (Fe@C-600), 0.78 (Fe@C-600-H₂), and 0.91 (Fe@C-700), indicating increasing graphitization at 600°C.
- **Observations:** The formation of a well-defined graphene shell at 600°C provides optimal stabilization and confinement, directly contributing to superior catalytic selectivity and long-term durability.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (300 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.0, WHSV = 8000 ml/g/h), Fe@C-600 exhibited a CO conversion that increased from 65% to 74.5% over time, stabilizing thereafter. Fe@C-600-H₂ achieved a higher CO conversion of 78.7% at 24 h. Unsupported-Fe, tested at a lower WHSV (3000 ml/g/h), showed significantly lower activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe@C-600 catalyst demonstrated a gradual activation period followed by stable performance, while Fe@C-600-H₂, though more active initially, did not maintain superior selectivity. The presence of a graphene shell in Fe@C-600 is critical for sustained high conversion and stability.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-600 maintained a low CH₄ selectivity of 10–12%. In contrast, Fe@C-600-H₂ exhibited a higher CH₄ selectivity, and unsupported-Fe reached 28.7% at 24 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The dominance of h‑Fe₃C in Fe@C-600 suppresses methane formation, favoring long-chain hydrocarbon production. Catalysts with a-Fe or lacking effective carbon shells show increased methane selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ (Long-Chain Hydrocarbon) Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key metric for liquid fuel production.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-600 achieved C₅⁺ selectivity of 57–60% under FTS conditions. This value remained stable over extended operation (up to 184 h). Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe showed lower C₅⁺ selectivity, correlating with higher methane formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high C₅⁺ selectivity of Fe@C-600 is attributed to the h‑Fe₃C phase and the stabilizing effect of the graphene shell, which together promote chain growth and suppress undesired byproducts.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of hydrocarbon chain propagation versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution:
  \[
  \text{ln}(W_n/n) = n \cdot \text{ln}(\alpha) + \text{constant}
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the mass fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not numerically reported, Fe@C-600 (h‑Fe₃C dominated, graphene shell) is described as having a higher α, indicative of enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity, compared to Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of h‑Fe₃C and a well-formed graphene shell in Fe@C-600 correlates with a higher α, supporting efficient chain growth and superior C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by the change in CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is expressed as the percentage decline in CO conversion during the reaction period.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-600 maintained stable CO conversion with negligible deactivation over 120–184 h. In contrast, Fe@C-500, Fe@C-700, and unsupported-Fe exhibited deactivation rates of 13.1%, 20.1%, and 47.9%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 effectively prevents nanoparticle aggregation and phase transformation, ensuring long-term stability. Catalysts with amorphous or thick graphite shells, or lacking carbon encapsulation, are prone to rapid deactivation.

---

**Summary:**  
Fe@C-600, characterized by a h‑Fe₃C core and a graphene shell, delivers high CO conversion, low methane selectivity, and superior long-chain hydrocarbon production with outstanding stability under FTS conditions. The structural synergy between the active phase and carbon shell is critical for optimal catalyst performance.
