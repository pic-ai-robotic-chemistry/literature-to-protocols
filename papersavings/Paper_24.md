## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article describes the synthesis of cobalt catalysts via incipient wetness impregnation, a technique chosen for its simplicity and precise control over precursor loading. This method ensures uniform cobalt dispersion by filling the support’s pores with an aqueous cobalt nitrate hexahydrate solution. The following synthesis methods are reported for Co/TiO₂, Co/Nb₂O₅, and Co/α-Al₂O₃ supports.

#### Co/TiO₂ Synthesis
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Pre‑dry 2 g of TiO₂ support at 80 °C for 1–2 h under vacuum to remove moisture and ensure efficient pore filling.  
  – Add a pore‑filling amount of cobalt nitrate hexahydrate solution (prepared in Milli‑Q water) dropwise under continuous stirring.
  – Note: No explicit precipitation–deposition step is reported.
2. • Drying  
  – Dry the impregnated sample at 80 °C for 2 h under vacuum.
3. • Heat Treatment  
  – Conduct heat treatment in a fluidized bed reactor at 250 °C for 2 h under a controlled N₂ flow to decompose the precursor and form cobalt nanoparticles.
4. • Post‑synthesis ROR Treatment  
  – Initial reduction at 350 °C, followed by oxidation (observations at 200 °C and 400 °C, where hollow cobalt oxide particles form), and finally re‑reduction at 220 °C to optimize metal–support interactions.

#### Co/Nb₂O₅ and Co/α-Al₂O₃ Synthesis
1. • Precursor Mixing  
  – Impregnate the support with an aqueous cobalt nitrate solution, using the same pore‑filling strategy.
2. • Drying  
  – Dry the impregnated supports for 12 h at 60 °C in stagnant air to ensure complete solvent removal.
3. • Calcination  
  – Calcine the samples in a fixed‑bed reactor at 350 °C for 2 h under N₂ flow, which helps in forming stable cobalt oxide phases.
4. • Post‑synthesis ROR Treatment  
  – Follow the same reduction–oxidation–reduction sequence as for Co/TiO₂.

This detailed synthesis outline aligns with the experimental QA responses. The procedure highlights the importance of controlled conditions such as specific temperatures, durations, and reactor environments (fluidized versus fixed-bed) to ensure reproducibility and optimal catalyst performance.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the preparation of reagents for the incipient wetness impregnation of cobalt catalysts, organized by catalyst type. The procedures outline solid reagent weighing, liquid transfer, and subsequent mixing to ensure a homogeneous impregnation process. All steps are described with sufficient detail to allow reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

For all catalysts, the preparation begins with pre-drying the support at the specified conditions before weighing the active phase precursor.

##### 3.1.1.1 Co/TiO₂  
- **TiO₂ (P25)**: Weighed 2.00g after pre-drying at 80 °C for 1–2 hours under vacuum.  
- **Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed 0.15g using an analytical balance to achieve a 6 wt% loading.

##### 3.1.1.2 Co/Nb₂O₅  
- **Nb₂O₅**: Weighed 2.00g after pre-drying at 80 °C for 1 hour under vacuum.  
- **Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed 0.15g using an analytical balance for the preset cobalt content.

##### 3.1.1.3 Co/α-Al₂O₃  
- **α-Al₂O₃**: Weighed 2.00g following the same pre-drying protocol.  
- **Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed 0.15g using an analytical balance for uniform comparison across catalysts.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

The liquid precursor solution is prepared in Milli‑Q water and transferred in a controlled manner to ensure complete pore filling of the support.

##### 3.1.2.1 Co/TiO₂  
- **Aqueous Co(NO₃)₂ solution**: Poured dropwise (pore‑filling amount) using a graduated pipette into the pre‑dried TiO₂.

##### 3.1.2.2 Co/Nb₂O₅  
- **Aqueous Co(NO₃)₂ solution**: Added dropwise (pore‑filling amount) under manual control to the pre‑dried Nb₂O₅.

##### 3.1.2.3 Co/α-Al₂O₃  
- **Aqueous Co(NO₃)₂ solution**: Transferred dropwise (pore‑filling amount) using a syringe into the pre‑dried α‑Al₂O₃.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

Homogeneity is achieved by controlled stirring and, in some cases, ultrasonic treatment.

##### 3.1.3.1 Co/TiO₂  
- **Co/TiO₂ precursor mixture**: Mixed at 800 rpm for 30 minutes at room temperature.  
- **Final Co/TiO₂ suspension**: Ultrasonically treated for 15 minutes to ensure uniform dispersion.

##### 3.1.3.2 Co/Nb₂O₅  
- **Co/Nb₂O₅ precursor mixture**: Stirred at 800 rpm for 30 minutes under ambient conditions.  
- **Final Co/Nb₂O₅ suspension**: Mixed manually for 10 minutes to enhance homogeneity.

##### 3.1.3.3 Co/α-Al₂O₃  
- **Co/α-Al₂O₃ precursor mixture**: Stirred at 700 rpm for 25 minutes under ambient air.  
- **Final Co/α-Al₂O₃ suspension**: Ultrasonically treated for 10 minutes, promoting complete distribution of the precursor.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Co/TiO₂

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g cobalt nitrate hexahydrate dissolved in 100 mL Milli‑Q water (targeting a 6 wt% cobalt loading).  
- **Conditions**: Prepare the aqueous solution at room temperature under continuous magnetic stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve the cobalt nitrate in Milli‑Q water ensuring complete dissolution.  
  - Verify the solution’s homogeneity before use.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure reagent purity (≥99%) and accurate weighing to maintain reproducibility.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly via Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
- **Reagents**: Pre-dried TiO₂ support.  
- **Conditions**: Pre-dry 2 g of TiO₂ support at 80 °C for 1 hour under vacuum.  
- **Process**:  
  - Add the cobalt precursor dropwise to the pre-dried support ensuring the solution exactly fills the pore volume.  
  - Stir manually and magnetically to secure uniform dispersion.  
- **Key Steps**: The defined “pore-filling amount” must be carefully matched to the support’s pore volume.

##### Step 3: Drying, Calcination & Purification  
- **Drying**: Dry the impregnated TiO₂ at 80 °C for 2 hours in a fluidized bed reactor.  
- **Calcination**: Heat treat at 250 °C for 2 hours under a 1 L min⁻¹ N₂ flow to prevent premature reduction.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor temperature ramp rates and ensure even gas distribution.

##### Step 4: Activation (ROR Procedure)  
- **Process**:  
  - Reduce initially at 350 °C, then oxidize using 5 vol% O₂ in N₂ for 2 hours (noting key effects at 200 and 400 °C), and finally re‑reduce at 220 °C to optimize metal–support interactions.  
- **Key Steps**: Control gas flow rates and temperature ramp (≈1–2 °C min⁻¹).

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Co/Nb₂O₅ & Co/α-Al₂O₃

##### Precursor Preparation & Assembly  
- Follow similar incipient wetness impregnation with the same cobalt nitrate solution.  
- For both supports, after impregnation, dry for 12 hours at 60 °C in stagnant air.

##### Calcination & Activation  
- **Calcination**: Perform in a fixed-bed reactor at 350 °C for 2 hours with a ramp rate of 3 °C min⁻¹ under N₂ flow (1 L min⁻¹).  
- **Activation**: Apply the same ROR protocol for Co/Nb₂O₅. For Co/α-Al₂O₃, standard drying and calcination are employed, avoiding the additional ROR steps.

These step‐by‐step processes, detailing reagent amounts, temperatures, and controlled atmospheres, ensure reproducibility across different supports while emphasizing critical monitoring points such as pH adjustments during impregnation and precise drying conditions before calcination.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for the Co-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is performed using a reduction–oxidation–reduction (ROR) procedure designed specifically to tailor metal–support interactions and increase the number of accessible cobalt sites.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
The activation process begins with an initial reduction at 350 °C using hydrogen gas. This temperature is critical for converting cobalt oxide into metallic cobalt while preserving the catalyst dispersion. Subsequently, a controlled oxidation step is implemented. When oxidation is carried out at 200 °C, it initiates the Kirkendall effect, leading to the formation of hollow cobalt oxide particles. This effect is essential, as it enhances the exposure of active sites. In contrast, oxidation at 400 °C tends to induce particle growth (with sizes increasing from approximately 10 to 15 nm), which can reduce the effective cobalt surface available for catalysis. The final re-reduction is then performed at 220 °C, regenerating the metallic phase and completing the activation procedure without compromising the intrinsic turnover frequency.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
Hydrogen is employed during both the initial and final reduction steps. Its role is to ensure complete reduction of cobalt oxide to metallic cobalt. The hydrogen-rich environment is indispensable for maximizing the catalyst’s active surface area and for modulating the metal–support interface effectively.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time and Process Integration
Each step in the ROR treatment is carried out over a specified duration—typically, the initial reduction and oxidation steps are maintained for 2 hours. Although the final re-reduction conditions (220 °C) are reported without an explicit duration, the comprehensive experimental protocol ensures reproducibility. Notably, no specific flow rate or space-time velocity is provided for these activation steps, as such parameters are primarily detailed in the reaction conditions for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (20 bar, H₂/CO 2 V/V, GHSV between 1000–13000 h⁻¹).

This carefully sequenced activation procedure—distinguishing synthesis-related treatments from characterization methods—allows for the controlled tuning of metal–support interactions. As a result, catalysts with reducible supports (Co/TiO₂ and Co/Nb₂O₅) exhibit a twofold increase in H₂ uptake, whereas an irreducible support (Co/α-Al₂O₃) shows a decrease due to particle agglomeration.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** This parameter represents the controlled thermal environment at which Fischer–Tropsch synthesis proceeds. The reaction temperature governs syngas activation and influences both conversion rates and product distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The synthesis reactions were carried out at a temperature of 220 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 220 °C provides a consistent thermal milieu that promotes stable catalytic performance across the studied cobalt catalysts (Co/TiO₂, Co/Nb₂O₅, and Co/α-Al₂O₃).

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure directly affects the concentration of syngas components (H₂ and CO), thereby impacting both reaction kinetics and product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Experiments were conducted at a constant pressure of 20 bar within stainless steel reactors integrated into an Avantium Flowrence 16 parallel reactor setup.
- **Observations:** The utilization of 20 bar offers enhanced partial pressures for the reactants, creating reproducible conditions that facilitate reliable catalyst comparisons and promote efficient syngas activation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is pivotal in dictating the hydrocarbon distribution during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It determines the relative availability of hydrogen versus carbon monoxide, thereby influencing the type and distribution of products formed.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A fixed H₂/CO ratio of 2 V/V was maintained during all catalytic tests.
- **Observations:** This constant ratio ensures uniformity across experiments, enabling straightforward assessment of catalyst performance and product selectivity. Variations in this ratio are known to steer the formation of different hydrocarbon chains.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Often expressed as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), this parameter quantifies the volume of synthesis gas passed over a unit mass of catalyst per hour, thereby controlling the reactant contact time.
- **Experimental Conditions:** GHSV values were adjusted within a range from 1000 to 13000 h⁻¹.
- **Observations:** Alterations in GHSV are linked to changes in CO conversion efficiency; lower velocities generally allow for higher conversion due to increased residence time, whereas higher velocities tend to support improved catalyst stability under industrially relevant reaction conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Cobalt Particle Morphology and Size Distribution
- **Description:** The morphology and size distribution of cobalt particles were analyzed using scanning transmission electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDX), employing an FEI Talos F200X equipped with a HAADF detector. This technique provides direct visualization of particle dispersion and allows for statistical analysis of particle diameters.
- **Findings:** For TiO₂-supported catalysts, STEM-EDX mapping revealed well-dispersed cobalt particles with an average size of 11 ± 3.4 nm after reduction (R-TiO₂) and 10 ± 3.8 nm after reduction–oxidation–reduction (RO200R-TiO₂), indicating minimal change in particle size through the ROR process. On Nb₂O₅, the average particle size after ROR was 18 ± 7.9 nm, while on α-Al₂O₃, it was 21 ± 11 nm. At oxidation temperatures of 200 °C and above, hollow cobalt oxide particles were observed, attributed to the Kirkendall effect.
- **Observations:** The formation of hollow structures is reversible upon re-reduction, as confirmed by the restoration of metallic cobalt surface area. The consistent particle size before and after ROR on reducible supports suggests that increased surface accessibility is not due to redispersion but to changes in surface chemistry.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Accessibility and H₂ Uptake
- **Description:** H₂-chemisorption was employed to quantify the accessible metallic cobalt surface area, using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 C. This method measures the amount of hydrogen adsorbed on the catalyst, directly correlating with the number of active surface sites.
- **Findings:** For Co/TiO₂, H₂ uptake increased from 341 to 783 µmol H₂ gCo⁻¹ after ROR treatment. Similarly, Co/Nb₂O₅ showed an increase from 265 to 657 µmol H₂ gCo⁻¹. In contrast, Co/α-Al₂O₃ exhibited a decrease in H₂ uptake from 631 to 499 µmol H₂ gCo⁻¹ after ROR.
- **Observations:** The substantial increase in H₂ uptake for catalysts on reducible supports is attributed to the removal of suboxide overlayers and the modulation of strong metal–support interactions (SMSI), resulting in a higher fraction of accessible metallic cobalt. The decrease on α-Al₂O₃ is linked to particle growth and agglomeration.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Redox Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was used to assess the reducibility of cobalt oxide species and the degree of reduction (DOR). This technique tracks hydrogen consumption as the temperature increases, revealing reduction steps and their corresponding temperatures.
- **Findings:** Pristine Co₃O₄/Nb₂O₅ exhibited a two-step reduction: Co₃O₄ to CoO at 280–300 °C, and CoO to metallic Co at 300–400 °C. After reduction and mild oxidation (≤100 °C), a single sharp reduction peak at 230 °C was observed, indicating easier reduction of CoO. Oxidation at higher temperatures (≥150 °C) restored the two-step profile, consistent with reformation of Co₃O₄. The DOR for reduced samples was 97–98%, decreasing slightly to 84–95% after ROR.
- **Observations:** The redox behavior is highly dependent on the oxidation temperature during ROR, with optimal conditions (e.g., oxidation at 200 °C, re-reduction at 220 °C) maximizing accessible metallic cobalt and catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.4 Crystal Structure and Phase Analysis
- **Description:** X-ray diffraction (XRD) was referenced to confirm the crystal phases present and to support findings from H₂-chemisorption regarding metallic cobalt surface area.
- **Findings:** XRD data corroborated the increase in accessible metallic cobalt after ROR, as the results from H₂-chemisorption and XRD were brought into closer agreement post-treatment.
- **Observations:** The removal of suboxide overlayers and restoration of metallic cobalt phases are critical for enhanced catalyst performance, as indicated by the convergence of XRD and chemisorption data.

#### 6.1.5 In Situ Structural and SMSI Analysis
- **Description:** In situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was utilized to study structural changes and the presence of SMSI in Co/Nb₂O₅ under reducing conditions.
- **Findings:** No overlayers of suboxide species were detected on the metallic cobalt surface after ROR, confirming the reversibility of SMSI and the restoration of active sites.
- **Observations:** The ability to modulate SMSI through controlled redox treatments is central to optimizing catalyst accessibility and performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flows of CO into and out of the reactor, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (220 °C, 20 bar, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV 1000–13,540 h⁻¹), CO conversion values were:
  - Co/TiO₂: 15–34%
  - Co/Nb₂O₅: 17–25%
  - Co/α-Al₂O₃: 8–12%
  After ROR treatment, CO conversion for RO200R-TiO₂ was 19.3%, RO200R-Nb₂O₅ 24.6%, and RO200R-Al₂O₃ 8.98%.
- **Observations and Trends:** ROR treatment nearly doubled CO conversion for reducible supports (TiO₂, Nb₂O₅), correlating with increased H₂ uptake and accessible cobalt surface area. In contrast, α-Al₂O₃ showed decreased conversion post-ROR due to particle agglomeration.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity (C₁ selectivity) values were:
  - RO200R-TiO₂: 7.5%
  - RO200R-Nb₂O₅: 6.7%
  - RO200R-Al₂O₃: 6.2%
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts on reducible supports exhibited slightly higher CH₄ selectivity than α-Al₂O₃, but all values remained low, favoring heavier hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, calculated as:
  \[
  S_{C_5^+} = 1 - S_{C_1–C_4}
  \]
  where \( S_{C_1–C_4} \) is the combined selectivity for C₁–C₄ hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivity was consistently high:
  - All catalysts: 85–89%
- **Observations and Trends:** ROR treatment did not significantly alter C₅⁺ selectivity, which remained high across all supports, indicating a strong preference for long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution. Higher α values correspond to greater selectivity for longer-chain products.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, high C₅⁺ selectivity (85–89%) implies α values typically above 0.9.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts on reducible supports, especially after ROR, favor higher α factors, correlating with increased C₅⁺ selectivity and enhanced CO-dissociation rates at the metal–suboxide interface.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time-on-stream. The deactivation rate is inferred from the decline in conversion during extended operation.
- **Experimental Data:** Activity was stable over 90–100 hours on stream for optimized catalysts, with minimal loss in CO conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** ROR-treated catalysts on reducible supports maintained high activity and stability, while α-Al₂O₃-supported catalysts showed greater deactivation, attributed to particle growth and reduced active site accessibility.

#### 6.2.6 Additional Performance Indicators
- **Cobalt Time Yield (CTY):** Calculated as the amount of CO converted per gram of cobalt per second, normalized using ICP-OES-determined cobalt loading.
- **Turnover Frequency (TOF):** Remained constant for each support, indicating that ROR treatment increases the number of active sites without altering their intrinsic activity.

**Summary:**  
Catalysts supported on reducible oxides (TiO₂, Nb₂O₅) and subjected to ROR treatment exhibit significantly enhanced CO conversion and maintain high selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons, with stable performance over extended operation. These improvements are directly linked to increased accessible cobalt surface area and optimized metal–support interactions, while intrinsic activity per site (TOF) remains unchanged.
