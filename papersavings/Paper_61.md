## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The composite OX‑ZEO catalyst (ZnCrOx/MSAPO) is synthesized using two complementary methods, each optimized to achieve the desired oxide and zeolite properties.

#### • Co‑precipitation Method for ZnCrOx Oxide
1. Precursor Mixing:  
   - Reagents: 14.56 g Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O, 5.6 g Cr(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, and 5.25 g Al(NO₃)₃·9H₂O dissolved in 100 mL distilled water.
   - Equipment: Standard laboratory glassware with stirring.
2. Co‑precipitation:  
   - The mixture is precipitated at 70 °C using (NH₄)₂CO₃.  
   - pH is monitored with a laboratory pH meter.
3. Aging and Precipitation–Deposition:  
   - The reaction mixture is allowed to age for 3 hours at 70 °C under constant stirring.
4. Drying and Calcination:  
   - The precipitate is filtered, washed with distilled water, and dried overnight at 110 °C.
   - Calcination is conducted at 500 °C for 1 hour in air.
5. Post‑Treatment:  
   - The oxide is activated via in situ reduction in H₂ at 310 °C for 2 hours.

#### • Hydrothermal Method for MSAPO Zeolite
1. Precursor Mixing:  
   - Reagents: A mixture of 30% silica sol, 82% AlOOH, 85% phosphoric acid, and triethylamine (TEA) in the molar ratio SiO₂:Al₂O₃:H₃PO₄:TEA:H₂O = 0.48:1:1.6:3:50.
2. Hydrothermal Synthesis:  
   - Conducted in a Teflon‑lined autoclave at temperatures between 180–200 °C for a duration of 12–28 hours.
3. Drying and Calcination:  
   - Following synthesis, the product is dried at 110 °C (overnight) and calcined at 550 °C for 3 hours.

#### • Formation of Composite Catalyst
- The ZnCrOx and MSAPO components are blended in defined mass ratios (commonly 1:1, 1.4, and 0.9). The uniform dispersion achieved during co‑precipitation and hydrothermal synthesis facilitates the formation of bifunctional active sites. 

### Comparative Analysis
- The co‑precipitation method is straightforward, ensuring uniform mixing and reproducibility, though it requires careful pH control. The hydrothermal process, while offering controlled hierarchical pore formation and acidic site distribution, necessitates specialized equipment and extended processing times. This balance allows scalability while maintaining catalyst performance and selectivity.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Composite OX-ZEO Catalyst (ZnCrOx/MSAPO)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – ZnCrOx Oxide Component  
- **Reagents**: 14.56 g Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O, 5.6 g Cr(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, and 5.25 g Al(NO₃)₃·9H₂O dissolved in 100 mL distilled water; (NH₄)₂CO₃ used as precipitant.  
- **Conditions**: Precipitation is carried out at 70 °C with vigorous stirring; the reaction mixture is aged for 3 hours at the same temperature.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve metal nitrates in distilled water to form a homogeneous solution.  
  - Introduce (NH₄)₂CO₃ gradually to initiate controlled precipitation of the metal hydroxides.  
  - Maintain the mixture at 70 °C for 3 hours to complete precipitation and aging.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor pH closely during washing; centrifuge until the supernate attains pH 7.0–7.5.

##### Step 2: Precursor Preparation – MSAPO Zeolite Component  
- **Reagents**: Silica sol, AlOOH, phosphoric acid, and triethylamine (TEA) in appropriate ratios to achieve a SAPO-34 type framework.  
- **Conditions**: Hydrothermal synthesis is conducted in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 180–200 °C for 12–28 hours to develop a hierarchical pore structure.  
- **Process**:  
  - Mix the raw chemicals to ensure a homogenous gel formation.  
  - Load the mixture into the autoclave and maintain the specified temperature range and duration to promote zeolite crystallization.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure precise control of reaction time and temperature to achieve desired acidity and mesoporosity.

##### Step 3: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents & Conditions**: Blend the as-prepared ZnCrOx oxide and MSAPO zeolite components using precise mass ratios (e.g., 1:1, 1.4, 0.9) to form the composite.  
- **Process**:  
  - Weigh each component accurately, then combine them thoroughly in a mechanical mixer.  

##### Step 4: Purification, Drying, & Calcination  
- **Purification**: Centrifuge the blended precursor and wash with distilled water until neutral pH is reached.  
- **Drying**: Dry the purified material overnight at 110 °C in a standard drying oven.  
- **Calcination**: Calcine the dried material at 500 °C for 1 hour to ensure crystallinity and phase formation.

##### Step 5: Activation  
- **Process**: Activate the composite catalyst through in situ reduction under H₂ at 310 °C for 2 hours prior to catalytic testing.  
- **Key Considerations**: Maintain controlled gas flow and temperature to avoid over-reduction, ensuring reproducibility in catalyst performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation step, performed as a pretreatment for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction, involves an in situ reduction process that is critical to forming the functionally bifunctional active sites. This step specifically targets the ZnCrOx component of the composite OX-ZEO catalyst to generate a partially reduced oxide with a spinel structure, creating oxygen vacancies that facilitate CO activation and subsequent C–C coupling.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** Activation temperature is essential for initiating reduction reactions by removing lattice oxygen from the ZnCrOx phase, thereby creating vacancies for enhanced reactivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated through an in situ reduction process conducted at 310 °C in a H₂ atmosphere.
- **Observations:** Operating at 310 °C provides a moderate thermal environment that ensures effective reduction of the ZnCrOx without causing significant sintering, thereby preserving a high dispersion of metal sites and maintaining catalyst stability.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The activation gas serves as the reducing agent needed for the in situ conversion of the fully oxidized ZnCrOx into its partially reduced form. This step is crucial for establishing the active sites integral to the catalyst’s performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** H₂ is employed as the activation gas, directly participating in the reduction process.
- **Observations:** The use of H₂ facilitates the creation of oxygen vacancies in the ZnCrOx structure. A controlled H₂ environment ensures that the reduction is sufficient to activate the catalyst without leading to over-reduction, which could potentially compromise the catalyst’s structural integrity.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Activation duration determines the completeness of the reduction process, ensuring that all catalyst particles are uniformly converted to the active phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The in situ reduction is carried out for 2 hours.
- **Observations:** The 2-hour activation period is optimized to achieve complete and uniform reduction of the active component, thereby ensuring that the catalyst exhibits the desired reactivity in subsequent syngas conversion. This duration is critical for balancing effective activation while preventing deleterious effects such as sintering or excessive structural changes.

Through these carefully controlled activation parameters, the catalyst demonstrates enhanced CO activation capabilities, laying the foundation for efficient Fischer–Tropsch synthesis under the studied reaction conditions.

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction phase for the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation was conducted under well-defined and reproducible conditions to ensure consistent performance. The selected parameters were fully extracted from the article and are critical for validating the catalyst's activity and selectivity.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature, a key driver for syngas activation and overall catalytic performance, was maintained at 400 °C. At this temperature, the catalyst exhibits enhanced activation of CO and H₂, leading to effective conversion of syngas to light olefins. Observations indicate that operating at 400 °C not only promotes the desired C–C coupling reactions but also minimizes side reactions, contributing to the catalyst’s stability over extended periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure

A H₂/CO ratio of 2.5 was applied, serving as a critical factor in controlling the hydrocarbon product distribution. This ratio optimizes the balance between hydrogenation and chain growth reactions, thereby enhancing CO conversion rates and boosting the selectivity towards light olefins. Experimental observations show that deviations from this ratio significantly alter product yields and can increase methane selectivity.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The reactor was operated at a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of approximately 6800 ml/h·g_cat. This parameter defines the residence time of the reactants in the reactor. A higher GHSV reduces the contact time, favoring the formation of light olefins over excessive methane production while maintaining an overall CO conversion of around 17%. The chosen space velocity also contributes to maintaining steady catalyst performance over prolonged testing periods.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalyst components were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and (scanning) transmission electron microscopy [(S)TEM].
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of ZnCrOx, measured on a PANalytical X'pert PPR diffractometer (CuKα, λ = 1.5418 Å, 40 mA, 40 kV, 2θ = 5–80º), confirmed a typical spinel structure by matching PDF #22-1107. For MSAPO, XRD and electron diffraction patterns revealed the characteristic CHA (SAPO-34) zeolite structure. High-resolution (S)TEM images further visualized the atomic arrangement of ZnCrOx and the hierarchical pore structure of MSAPO, including macro-, meso-, and micropores.
- **Observations:** The preservation of the spinel phase in ZnCrOx and the hierarchical CHA structure in MSAPO are essential for bifunctional catalysis, enabling efficient CO activation and subsequent C–C coupling.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption-desorption (BET) analysis at 77 K was used to quantify surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution. Samples were degassed at 300 °C for 6 h prior to measurement.
- **Findings:** The MSAPO zeolite exhibited a hierarchical pore structure, as confirmed by both TEM imaging and BET analysis. Reported parameters include BET specific surface area (m²/g), micropore area (m²/g), mesopore area (m²/g), micropore volume (cc/g), total pore volume (cc/g), and average mesopore diameter (nm).
- **Observations:** The hierarchical porosity of MSAPO facilitates rapid diffusion of reactants and intermediates, which is critical for the high selectivity and efficiency observed in olefin production.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Surface States
- **Description:** In situ near ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (NAP-XPS) was employed to probe the elemental composition and surface states of the catalyst under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** NAP-XPS spectra (acquired at 735 eV photon energy) showed changes in Zn 3d binding energy and the appearance of surface C 1s signals upon exposure to H₂ and CO, indicating partial reduction of ZnCrOx and the formation of oxygen vacancies. The C 1s signal at 284.7 eV confirmed the presence of surface carbon species derived from CO activation.
- **Observations:** The formation of oxygen vacancies and surface *C species on ZnCrOx under syngas is directly linked to the catalyst’s ability to activate CO and facilitate C–C coupling.

#### 6.1.4 Acidity Profile
- **Description:** NH₃ temperature-programmed desorption (NH₃-TPD) was used to assess the acidity of the MSAPO zeolite, focusing on medium strength acidic sites.
- **Findings:** NH₃-TPD (Micromeritics AutoChem 2910, TCD detector) revealed desorption peaks between 350–410 °C, corresponding to medium strength acid sites. MSAPO with a peak at 350 °C exhibited an olefin/paraffin ratio of 4.7, while commercial SAPO-34 (peak at 394 °C) showed a ratio of 0.9.
- **Observations:** The presence and optimal tuning of medium strength acidity in MSAPO are crucial for maximizing olefin selectivity and suppressing paraffin formation.

#### 6.1.5 Detection of Reaction Intermediates
- **Description:** In situ SVUV-PIMS and NAP-XPS were utilized to monitor reaction intermediates and surface species during syngas conversion.
- **Findings:** SVUV-PIMS detected ketene (CH₂CO, m/z = 42.01), propene, methanol, and methoxyl species, while NAP-XPS tracked the evolution of surface *C species. These intermediates were observed under reaction conditions, supporting the proposed mechanism of CO activation and C–C coupling.
- **Observations:** The direct detection of ketene and surface carbon species provides mechanistic insight into the bifunctional catalytic process, confirming the spatial separation of CO activation (ZnCrOx) and olefin formation (MSAPO).




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flows of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (400 °C, 2.5 MPa, H₂/CO = 2.5, space velocity ~6800 ml/h·g_cat), the composite ZnCrOx/MSAPO catalyst achieved a CO conversion of approximately 17%. Adjusting the ZnCrOx/MSAPO mass ratio (e.g., 1.4, 1.0, 0.9) modulates conversion, with higher oxide content favoring increased CO conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal performance is observed when the oxide and zeolite components are balanced, enabling efficient CO activation and intermediate transport. Higher ZnCrOx content increases conversion, while excessive zeolite may reduce it.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The composite catalyst exhibits low methane selectivity, as low as 2–3% at 17% CO conversion. In contrast, ZnCrOx alone yields a much higher CH₄ selectivity of 53%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The bifunctional design of the composite catalyst effectively suppresses methane formation, favoring higher-value hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.3 C₂–C₄ Hydrocarbon Selectivity and Distribution
- **Description:** Selectivity to C₂–C₄ hydrocarbons is defined as the fraction of converted CO forming C₂–C₄ products:
  \[
  \text{C}_2\text{–C}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
  The distribution between olefins and paraffins is further characterized by the olefin/paraffin ratio.
- **Experimental Data:** The composite catalyst achieves up to 94% selectivity for C₂–C₄ hydrocarbons, with approximately 80% as light olefins (C₂=–C₄=) and 14% as paraffins. The olefin/paraffin ratio reaches 4.7 with optimized MSAPO, compared to 0.9 for commercial SAPO-34.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hierarchical pore structure and medium-strength acidity in MSAPO enhance olefin selectivity and suppress overhydrogenation to paraffins.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory (ASF) model, reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
  - Higher α values favor longer-chain hydrocarbons; lower values favor light olefins.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the observed C₂–C₄ selectivity (up to 94%) far exceeds the ASF model’s theoretical maximum (58%), indicating effective suppression of chain growth beyond C₄.
- **Observations and Trends:** The bifunctional catalyst design enables product distributions unattainable by conventional FTS, with high selectivity for light olefins.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over time. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate (h}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The composite catalyst maintains stable performance for over 110 hours with negligible deactivation. In extended tests, stability is preserved for up to 650 hours.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst demonstrates excellent long-term stability, with reproducible performance across multiple batches and minimal loss in activity.
