### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe (Pure Iron Catalyst)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, ammonia solution (as the precipitating agent), and 50 mL deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Room temperature; maintain a solution pH of approximately 8.0 using controlled dropwise addition of ammonia.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in 50 mL deionized water under constant stirring until a homogeneous solution is obtained.
  - Slowly add ammonia solution while monitoring the pH until it reaches 8.0, resulting in the formation of an iron hydroxide precipitate.
  - Continue stirring for 60 minutes to ensure complete precipitation of the precursor.
- **Key Steps**: Emphasize the controlled pH adjustment and steady reagent addition to ensure reproducibility.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Reagents**: The as-formed iron hydroxide precipitate.
- **Conditions**: Ambient laboratory conditions with subsequent centrifugation.
- **Process**:
  - Centrifuge the reaction mixture at approximately 4000 rpm for 10 minutes.
  - Wash the precipitate thoroughly with distilled water and, subsequently, with ethanol to remove nitrate residues and trace impurities.
- **Key Steps**: Repeated washing ensures the complete removal of unwanted ions, critical for catalyst purity.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the purified precipitate in ambient air at 343 K for about 4 hours.
- **Calcination**:
  - Calcine the dried material in a furnace at 573 K for 6 hours under air, using a controlled temperature ramp to avoid thermal shock.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform drying and precise heating are essential to achieve the desired hematite phase prior to activation.

##### Step 4: Activation & Passivation
- **Conditions**: Activation is executed at 723 K for 1 hour under one of the following atmospheres: pure H₂, CO/Ar mixture, or H₂/CO syngas.
- **Process**:
  - Expose the calcined precursor to the chosen reducing/carburizing atmosphere to transform iron oxide into metallic iron and form iron carbide phases (θ‐Fe₃C or χ‐Fe₂.₅C depending on the gas composition).
  - Immediately passivate the activated catalyst using a dilute O₂/He mixture (1 vol% O₂) at room temperature for 1 hour to stabilize the active surface.
- **Key Steps**: Strict control of activation temperature and gas flow, along with immediate passivation, is critical for ensuring reproducibility and maintaining catalyst integrity.

This workflow, from raw precursor dissolution to final passivation, provides clear, replicable steps essential for synthesizing the 100Fe catalyst as described in the article.


## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to identify the crystalline phases of the catalyst, including metallic Fe, magnetite, cementite (θ‑Fe₃C), and Hägg carbide (χ‑Fe₂.₅C). - **Instruments and Models**: Seifert 3000 XRD diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed using Cu Kα radiation in Bragg–Brentano θ/2θ geometry.
- **Test Parameters**: 2θ range of 10°–80°, step size of 0.05°, and a scanning time of 2 seconds per step.

### 4.2 Temperature‑Programmed Surface Reaction with H₂ (TPSR‑H₂)
- **Operational Description**: Approximately 40 mg of catalyst is pretreated in H₂, CO, CO/Ar (5% CO), or H₂/CO (2:1) at 723 K for 1 hour, then flushed with Ar at 723 K for 15 minutes. After cooling to room temperature, a 10% H₂/Ar mixture is passed while ramping the temperature.
- **Instruments and Models**: U‑shaped quartz reactor coupled with appropriate gas flow control.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Temperature ramp from 298 K to 1123 K at 10 K/min; gas flow maintained at 50–100 ml/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Continuous monitoring of methane evolution and surface reaction profiles.
- **Key Notes**: Ensures removal of weakly adsorbed species prior to reaction analysis.

### 4.3 Temperature‑Programmed Desorption with Argon (TPD‑Ar)
- **Operational Description**: Following identical pretreatment, the catalyst is subjected to an Ar flow to monitor desorption of adsorbed species.
- **Instruments and Models**: Same reactor setup as TPSR‑H₂.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Temperature ramp from 298 K to 1123 K at 10 K/min under Ar flow.
- **Test Parameters**: Gas flow between 50 and 100 ml/min.
- **Key Notes**: Differentiates between hydrogen evolution and CO desorption behaviors.

### 4.5 Raman Spectroscopy

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pre‐reaction step performed on the 100Fe catalyst to transform the hematite precursor into the active phases for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential for driving the reduction of iron oxide (hematite) to intermediate phases and ultimately to metallic iron, followed by carburization to form the active carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 723 K.
- **Observations:** This elevated temperature facilitates the sequential reduction–carburization process. Under pure H₂, complete reduction to metallic iron is observed, while exposure to CO-containing atmospheres leads to carbide formation.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Control of activation pressure is critical for maintaining a uniform activation atmosphere and influencing the reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is conducted under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Operating at atmospheric pressure simplifies the experimental setup and ensures reproducibility across different activation treatments.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation determines the extent of reduction versus carburization of the catalyst. Different gas environments yield distinct iron phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Three gas environments are employed: pure hydrogen (H₂), a CO/Ar mixture (e.g., 5% CO in Ar), and a syngas mixture with a H₂/CO ratio of 2:1.
- **Observations:** Pure H₂ produces metallic iron, whereas a CO or CO/Ar mixture promotes the formation of cementite (θ‐Fe₃C). The syngas environment favors the generation of Hägg carbide (χ‑Fe₂.₅C), which correlates with enhanced Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is set to ensure complete phase transformation without incurring detrimental sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is performed for 1 hour.
- **Observations:** This time interval is optimized to enable consistent catalyst conversion from the calcined hematite precursor to the desired active metallic and carbide phases while maintaining high catalyst stability.

The activation procedures are executed in a fixed‐bed microreactor outfitted with precise temperature control and monitored using a K-type thermocouple. These rigorous activation conditions are implemented exclusively as a pre‐reaction treatment to accurately tailor the catalyst’s phase composition, thereby optimizing its performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The evolution of crystal phases during catalyst synthesis and activation was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD). This technique enables identification of iron oxide, metallic iron, and iron carbide phases, which are critical for Fischer–Tropsch (FT) activity.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the calcined precursor confirm the presence of α-Fe₂O₃ (hematite). Upon activation at 723 K under different atmospheres, distinct phase transformations occur: pure H₂ treatment yields metallic Fe (Fe⁰), CO pretreatment leads to θ-Fe₃C (cementite), and H₂/CO (syngas) pretreatment results in χ-Fe₂.₅C (Hägg carbide). The assignment of θ-Fe₃C is based on characteristic Bragg angles at 78.0° and 70.1° 2θ (JCPDS 76-1877), while χ-Fe₂.₅C is identified by the absence of these peaks (JCPDS 36-1248). XRD measurements were performed in the 10°–80° 2θ range, with a scan step of 0.05° and 2 seconds per step.
- **Observations:** The formation of specific iron carbide phases is directly linked to the activation atmosphere, with χ-Fe₂.₅C associated with higher FT activity due to its stabilization of catalytically relevant surface carbon species.

#### 6.1.2 Iron Species Distribution by Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to quantify the distribution and relative concentration of iron species after various activation treatments, providing insight into the magnetic and electronic environments of iron.
- **Findings:** Post-reaction spectra reveal multiple components: three sextets (assigned to χ-Fe₂.₅C) and a doublet, with an additional sextet (θ-Fe₃C) present in CO-pretreated samples. The relative spectral areas indicate that hydrogen and syngas pretreatments predominantly yield χ-Fe₂.₅C and superparamagnetic ferric oxides, while CO pretreatment also produces θ-Fe₃C.
- **Observations:** The abundance of χ-Fe₂.₅C correlates with enhanced FT performance, while θ-Fe₃C formation is linked to lower activity and stabilization of less reactive surface carbon.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Carbon Species and Graphitic Deposits (Raman Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy was used to detect and characterize carbonaceous deposits and graphitic species formed during activation and reaction, which can influence catalyst performance.
- **Findings:** Calcined samples display peaks characteristic of α-Fe₂O₃. After CO or syngas pretreatment and subsequent TPSR-H₂, two prominent bands at ~1300 cm⁻¹ (D-band, disordered graphite) and ~1600 cm⁻¹ (G-band, ordered graphite) are observed. The intensity of these bands is higher following syngas activation, indicating greater formation of graphitic carbon.
- **Observations:** These graphitic species are stable under FT conditions and moderate concentrations act as spectators, but higher concentrations may block active sites and reduce catalytic efficiency.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Reaction Profiles (TPSR-H₂ and TPD-Ar)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed surface reaction with H₂ (TPSR-H₂) and temperature-programmed desorption with Ar (TPD-Ar) were conducted to monitor methane evolution, CO desorption, and water formation, elucidating the nature of surface carbon species and their reactivity.
- **Findings:** TPSR-H₂ profiles show methane evolution at ~770 K (C*α species), 870–950 K (C*β species), and ~1000 K (hydrogenation of iron carbides). Water formation at ~680 K is observed after diluted CO pretreatment, indicating iron oxide reduction. TPD-Ar experiments reveal only H₂ and CO desorption, with no methane formation.
- **Observations:** The presence and temperature of methane evolution peaks correlate with the type of iron carbide phase and surface carbon species, directly impacting FT activity and selectivity.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For the 100Fe catalyst, CO conversion after 10,000 min on stream under standard Fischer–Tropsch conditions (573 K, 1.01 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 0.0023 L g⁻¹ s⁻¹) varied with activation pretreatment:
  - H₂ pretreatment: 23%
  - CO pretreatment: 37%
  - H₂/CO (syngas) pretreatment: 74%
- **Observations and Trends:** Syngas pretreatment yielded the highest CO conversion, attributed to the direct formation of active iron carbide phases (notably χ‐Fe₂.₅C). CO pretreatment resulted in moderate conversion, while H₂ pretreatment led to the lowest activity, correlating with the formation of less active metallic Fe⁰.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For 100Fe:
  - H₂ pretreatment: 15.0%
  - CO pretreatment: 14.8%
  - H₂/CO pretreatment: 24.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity increased notably with H₂/CO activation, suggesting enhanced water–gas shift activity under these conditions. The formation of more active carbide phases is linked to this trend.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For 100Fe:
  - H₂ pretreatment: 19.4%
  - CO pretreatment: 15.8%
  - H₂/CO pretreatment: 15.9%
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane selectivity was highest after H₂ pretreatment, decreasing with CO and H₂/CO activation. This decrease is associated with the stabilization of carbide phases (θ‐Fe₃C and χ‐Fe₂.₅C), which favor longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–C₅ and C₆⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity to C₂–C₅ and C₆⁺ hydrocarbons indicates the catalyst’s ability to promote chain growth beyond methane. C₃⁺ selectivity can be inferred from these values.
- **Experimental Data:** For 100Fe (H₂/CO pretreatment):
  - C₂–C₅: 62.3%
  - C₆–C₁₂: 21.8%
  - C₁₃⁺: 0%
- **Observations and Trends:** H₂/CO activation led to the highest selectivity for C₂–C₅ and C₆–C₁₂ fractions, reflecting enhanced chain growth. CO and H₂ pretreatments resulted in lower selectivity to heavier hydrocarbons.

- **Experimental Data:** For 100Fe:
  - H₂ pretreatment: 0.57
  - CO pretreatment: 0.58
  - H₂/CO pretreatment: 0.58
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor remained relatively constant across activation conditions, indicating similar chain growth probabilities, though overall hydrocarbon distribution favored longer chains with H₂/CO activation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended time-on-stream (up to 10,000 min). Deactivation rate is inferred from the decline in conversion.
- **Experimental Data:** For 100Fe, CO conversion was stable after an initial reconstruction period (~1,000 min), with minimal deactivation observed over the test duration, especially after H₂/CO pretreatment.
- **Observations and Trends:** The most stable performance was observed for catalysts activated with H₂/CO, correlating with the formation of robust carbide phases. H₂-activated catalysts showed lower initial activity and more pronounced deactivation.
