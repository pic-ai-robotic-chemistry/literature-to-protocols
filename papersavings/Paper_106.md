## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the co‑precipitation synthesis method used for the Fe–Zr catalyst series, emphasizing reproducibility by reporting exact reagents, reaction conditions, and equipment. The method is advantageous for its simplicity and cost‑effectiveness, though it requires precise control of pH and temperature.

#### Co‑precipitation Method for Fe–Zr Catalyst Series

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  - Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (active phase precursor) and ZrOCl₂·8H₂O (promoter precursor) are mixed in an aqueous medium.  
  - Reaction medium: An aqueous ammonia solution (14 wt%) is used, with the mixed precursor solution added dropwise.  
  - Conditions: Temperature maintained at 16 °C; the addition continues until the pH reaches 11, as monitored by a calibrated pH meter.  
  - Observations: A distinct color change and formation of a solid precipitate indicate successful co‑precipitation.

2. • (Precipitation–Deposition)  
  - Although this step is defined as a part of the general experimental procedure, it is not explicitly applied in this synthesis. The process proceeds directly after precursor mixing.

3. • Washing  
  - The precipitate is washed with deionized water until the filtrate reaches a neutral pH of 7, ensuring thorough removal of residual ammonia and impurities.

4. • Impregnation  
  - The washed precipitate is impregnated with an aqueous K₂CO₃ solution to introduce potassium, using a Fe/K molar ratio of 100:4. This step enhances catalytic activity by modifying the electronic properties of the catalyst.

5. • Drying  
  - Drying is carried out at 120 °C overnight, which effectively removes moisture from the catalyst precursor.

6. • Calcination  
  - The dried material is calcined at 600 °C for 5 hours, promoting crystallization and the formation of the active α‑Fe₂O₃ and supportive ZrO₂ phases.

7. • Post‑processing  
  - Finally, the calcined samples are crushed and sieved to a 20–40 mesh particle size to ensure uniformity for subsequent catalytic evaluation.

This detailed procedure, with exact numerical values and conditions, ensures that the synthesis is reproducible and provides clear insight into the advantages and limitations of the co‑precipitation method.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation step is critical for transforming the precursor phases into the active forms required for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The pre‐reaction activation is performed under well‐defined conditions to ensure the proper reduction and carburization of the Fe–Zr catalyst series.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential for initiating the reduction of α‑Fe₂O₃ to form more active phases, such as iron carbide, while controlling the reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is heated gradually from ambient temperature to 300 °C at a controlled ramp rate (approximately 5 °C/min) and then maintained at 300 °C for 1 hour.
- **Observations:** This temperature profile facilitates the transformation of surface hematite to active phases and ensures that the bulk oxide undergoes a controlled reduction, thereby preserving catalyst integrity.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The pressure during activation influences gas–catalyst interactions and reduction kinetics necessary for effective pre-reaction treatment.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is carried out at a moderate pressure of 0.5 MPa when employing the CO-containing activation gas.
- **Observations:** This pressure condition is chosen to optimize the reduction process while avoiding excessive catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas determines the reducing environment necessary for the gradual conversion of iron oxides to active phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A reducing gas mixture consisting of 20% CO diluted in N₂ is utilized during the activation process.
- **Observations:** The presence of CO facilitates partial reduction and the formation of active carbide phases without promoting excessive sintering due to the moderating influence of N₂.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity controls the contact intensity between the activation gas and the catalyst, ensuring uniform gas exposure.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of approximately 5000 mL/(gcat·h) is maintained.
- **Observations:** This parameter is critical for achieving consistent reduction across the catalyst particles.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation ensures complete phase transformation without causing unwanted sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is held at 300 °C under the stated conditions for 1 hour.
- **Observations:** This duration is optimal to achieve the desired active phases while maintaining catalyst stability and performance over extended reaction periods.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter that drives the activation of syngas and the reduction of iron oxides to active catalytic species. In this study, the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was conducted at a fixed temperature of 270 °C. This temperature is selected to balance the kinetics of hydrocarbon formation with the minimization of catalyst sintering and carbon deposition. At 270 °C, the catalysts achieved optimal performance with CO conversions and product selectivity observed over extended runs, ensuring reproducibility in multiple experiments.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Operating at a pressure of 1.0 MPa, the reaction setup ensured an enhanced concentration of syngas in the fixed-bed reactor. This pressure level is critical as it increases the contact frequency between reactants and the catalyst surface, contributing to higher conversion efficiencies. The controlled use of 1.0 MPa also supports the formation of specific hydrocarbon chain lengths by shifting the equilibrium towards chain growth reactions without causing excessive deactivation due to overpressurization.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The feed syngas in these experiments maintained an H₂/CO molar ratio of 2. This ratio is pivotal, as it influences both the catalyst’s hydrogenation activity and the distribution of hydrocarbon products. While a high H₂/CO ratio can promote methane formation, under these conditions the impact on overall product distribution remained minimal. This careful setting aids in preserving the balance between hydrogenation and chain-growth processes, which is crucial when analyzing performance trends across different catalyst formulations.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
A weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) of 3 NL_H₂=CO per gram catalyst was applied, ensuring consistent reactant exposure across the catalyst bed. This rate was optimized to provide sufficient contact time for effective syngas conversion, while avoiding overly rapid flow that could compromise contact efficiency. The precise control of WHSV is integral for achieving reproducible results, as even minor variations can significantly influence conversion rates and product selectivity.



## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the Fe–Zr catalyst series were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), with phase identification based on JCPDS standards. The impact of Fe³⁺ substitution in the ZrO₂ lattice was also assessed by monitoring d-spacing variations.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that Zr0 and Zrp samples exhibited single phases of α‑Fe₂O₃ (JCPDS no. 33-0664) and ZrO₂ (JCPDS no. 89-9096), respectively. Mixed-phase samples (Zr0.2, Zr1, Zr2) displayed both α‑Fe₂O₃ and ZrO₂ reflections. The d-spacing of the ZrO₂ (111) plane decreased with increasing Fe³⁺ incorporation: from 0.2959 nm (Zrp) to 0.2938 nm (Zr0.2), 0.2929 nm (Zr1), and 0.2919 nm (Zr2), indicating lattice contraction due to substitution of smaller Fe³⁺ ions for Zr⁴⁺.
- **Observations:** The co-precipitation synthesis promoted high phase purity and intimate mixing, while Fe³⁺ substitution in ZrO₂ led to structural defects and enhanced interaction between the two oxides.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were determined by BET N₂ adsorption/desorption, including surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution. The influence of Zr content on mesoporous structure formation was evaluated.
- **Findings:** Surface area increased markedly with Zr addition: 13.59 m²/g (Zr0), 93.35 m²/g (Zr1), and 108.91 m²/g (Zr2). Pore volume rose from 0.10 cm³/g (Zr0) to 0.22 cm³/g (Zr1) and 0.20 cm³/g (Zr2). Average pore size expanded from 3.34 nm (Zr0) to 9.68 nm (Zr1) and 7.82 nm (Zr2). Isotherm profiles transitioned from type III (Zr0, Zr0.2, Zr1) to type IV (Zr2, Zrp), with corresponding changes in hysteresis loops indicating enhanced mesoporosity.
- **Observations:** ZrO₂ addition inhibited Fe₂O₃ crystal growth, resulting in smaller particles and the development of uniform mesopores, which are beneficial for reactant diffusion and catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Surface Chemistry
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) with H₂ was used to probe the reducibility of surface and bulk α‑Fe₂O₃ species. Surface chemical states and defect structures were further examined by XPS and Raman spectroscopy.
- **Findings:** TPR profiles showed three main reduction peaks: (a) below 500 °C (surface α‑Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄), (b) ~600 °C (Fe₃O₄ to FeO), and (c) ~750 °C (FeO to α‑Fe). The a1/a2 peak area ratio increased with Zr content, indicating a higher proportion of easily reducible surface species. XPS revealed shifts in Fe 2p and Zr 3d binding energies, consistent with Fe³⁺ substitution and increased oxygen vacancies. Raman spectra showed band shifts and defect-related features, confirming structural modifications due to Zr incorporation.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface reducibility and defect formation, as evidenced by TPR, XPS, and Raman, are linked to improved catalyst activation and performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** SEM and TEM analyses provided insights into particle morphology, size distribution, and crystallinity. XRD Scherrer calculations were used to estimate crystallite sizes.
- **Findings:** SEM images demonstrated a reduction in average particle size from ~130 nm (Zr0) to ~25 nm (Zr0.2, Zr1, Zr2), with Zrp at ~30 nm. TEM of Zr1 confirmed high crystallinity and clear lattice fringes for both α‑Fe₂O₃ (0.27 nm, (104) plane) and ZrO₂ (0.29 nm, (111) plane). XRD-derived crystallite sizes decreased with increasing Zr content (e.g., Fe₂O₃: 35.9 nm in Zr0 to 2.7 nm in Zr2).
- **Observations:** The synthesis method enabled uniform, nanoscale particles with high crystallinity and polycrystalline features, supporting enhanced dispersion and catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition and Surface Environment
- **Description:** XPS and Raman spectroscopy were employed to determine elemental composition, oxidation states, and surface defect structures.
- **Findings:** XPS analysis showed surface enrichment of Zr and well-defined Fe³⁺ and Zr⁴⁺ states. Raman spectra identified characteristic vibrational bands of α‑Fe₂O₃ and ZrO₂, with shifts indicating defect formation and altered surface environments due to Zr incorporation.
- **Observations:** The combination of XPS and Raman confirmed the presence of defect-rich, mixed-oxide surfaces, which are favorable for Fischer–Tropsch catalysis.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})} - M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO})}}{M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})}} \times 100\%
  \]
  where \( M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})} \) and \( M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO})} \) are the inlet and outlet carbon numbers of CO, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (270 °C, 1.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, WHSV = 3 NL_H₂=CO g⁻¹ Fe h⁻¹), the Zr1 catalyst (Zr/Fe = 1:1) achieved a CO conversion of 82.1%. Other catalysts exhibited: Zr0.2 at 90.7%, Zr0 at 70.2%, Zr2 at 53.2%, and Zrp at 1.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion peaks at moderate Zr content (Zr0.2 and Zr1), attributed to enhanced α-Fe₂O₃ dispersion and mesoporosity. Excessive Zr (Zr2, Zrp) significantly reduces conversion, likely due to active site blockage and diminished Fe activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO}_2)}}{M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})} - M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO})}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity increases with Zr content up to Zr1 (48.2%), then decreases for Zr2 (19.3%) and Zrp (1.0%). Zr0 and Zr0.2 show lower values (5.3% and intermediate, respectively).
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate Zr promotes the water-gas shift, while excessive Zr reduces CO₂ formation, likely due to decreased bulk Fe₃O₄.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CH}_4)}}{M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})} - M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO})}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity is determined by gas chromatography; while specific values are not detailed for each catalyst, the study notes that potassium promotion and optimal Zr content suppress methane formation, favoring lower olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower CH₄ selectivity is observed for Zr1, correlating with higher olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \sum_{n=1}^{4} S_{C_n}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivity (as a proxy for C₃⁺) is lowest for Zr1 (3.0%) and highest for Zrp (95.4%), indicating that moderate Zr addition favors light olefins over long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Zr/Fe ratio initially decreases C₃⁺ selectivity, reaching a minimum at Zr1, then increases sharply with further Zr addition.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution. Higher α values favor longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** The presence of Zr, especially at Zr1, lowers the α factor, promoting the formation of lower olefins. Specific α values are not numerically detailed but are inferred from product distributions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal Zr/Fe ratios reduce α, enhancing selectivity toward C₂–C₄ olefins.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \ (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 100 hours, Zr1 and Zr0.2 maintain stable CO conversion, while Zr2 deactivates rapidly after 50 hours and Zrp loses activity within 11 hours.
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate Zr content (Zr0.2, Zr1) confers superior stability, attributed to suppression of h‑Fe₃C formation and carbon deposition.

---
**Summary:** The Fe–Zr catalyst series demonstrates that a balanced Zr/Fe ratio (notably Zr1) optimizes CO conversion, selectivity toward lower olefins, and long-term stability. Excessive Zr content impairs performance, underscoring the importance of compositional tuning for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
