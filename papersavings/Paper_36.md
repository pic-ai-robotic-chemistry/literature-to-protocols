## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis methods employed for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, focusing on the sequential co‑impregnation method for Co-MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂. The catalyst synthesis involves combining cobalt and manganese precursors with a carbon source (glucose) to create a carbon‑mediated interface that enhances catalytic performance. Advantages of this method include fine control over precursor dispersion, improved nano-interface formation, and scalability, while limitations involve increased synthesis complexity and equipment dependency.

#### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: Dissolve 1.48 g Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (cobalt precursor) and Mn(CH₃COO)₂·4H₂O (in stoichiometric amounts to achieve a Mn/Co molar ratio of 0.2) in deionized water.  
   - Carbon Source: Incorporate glucose to facilitate carbon mediation, which is critical for generating oxygen vacancies in Co₃O₄.  
   - Equipment: Use a pre‑calcined SiO₂ support and a pH meter to ensure proper solution conditions.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Procedure: After mixing, add the support precursor dropwise under continuous stirring to promote even deposition.  
   - Observation: Note any color changes or precipitate formation as indicators of successful deposition.

3. • Drying  
   - Conditions: Remove solvent via rotary evaporation (100 rpm, 60°C) followed by drying at 80°C for 10 hours, ensuring uniform moisture elimination and precursor distribution.

4. • Pyrolysis  
   - Procedure: Heat the dried precursor to 400°C at a ramp of 5°C/min in an inert argon (Ar) flow.  
   - Duration: Maintain 400°C for 5 hours to carbonize glucose, forming a stable carbon matrix.

5. • Calcination  
   - Conditions: Post‐pyrolysis, calcine in static air at 400°C for 3 hours.  
   - Impact: Oxidizes the precursor, converting metal species into Co₃O₄ while further establishing the Co–MnOx nano‑interface.

The method’s reproducibility is ensured by documenting precise numerical values and controlled conditions at each step, as well as by using specialized equipment like the rotary evaporator and tubular furnace for accurate temperature regulation.

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

- **Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed 1.48g using a calibrated analytical balance.

- **Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed 1.48g.

##### 3.1.1.3 Co‐MnOx/SiO₂
- **Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed 1.48g.
- **Mn(CH₃COO)₂·4H₂O**: Weighed in a stoichiometric amount (approximately 0.25g) to achieve a Mn/Co molar ratio of 0.2.

##### 3.1.1.4 Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂
- **Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed 1.48g.
- **Mn(CH₃COO)₂·4H₂O**: Weighed at approximately 0.25g for the target Mn/Co ratio.
- **Glucose**: Weighed 0.4g to introduce the carbon mediator, ensuring the formation of oxygen vacancies and defective Co₃O₄.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Co/SiO₂
- **Deionized water**: Poured 20mL using a graduated cylinder to dissolve the cobalt precursor.

##### 3.1.2.2 Co‐(C)/SiO₂
- **Deionized water**: Poured 20mL using a graduated cylinder to create a homogeneous aqueous solution containing Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and glucose.

##### 3.1.2.3 Co‐MnOx/SiO₂
- **Deionized water**: Poured 20mL using a graduated cylinder to dissolve both Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and Mn(CH₃COO)₂·4H₂O.

##### 3.1.2.4 Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂
- **Deionized water**: Poured 20mL using a graduated cylinder to fully solubilize Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O, Mn(CH₃COO)₂·4H₂O, and glucose.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Co/SiO₂
- **Impregnation mixture**: Mixed at 400 rpm for 90 minutes at 60°C to ensure complete dissolution and uniform precursor distribution.

##### 3.1.3.2 Co‐(C)/SiO₂
- **Impregnation mixture**: Stirred at 400 rpm for 90 minutes at 60°C under controlled temperature to facilitate even integration of the carbon source.

##### 3.1.3.3 Co‐MnOx/SiO₂
- **Impregnation mixture**: Stirred at 400 rpm for 90 minutes at 60°C to achieve a homogeneous distribution of both the cobalt and manganese precursors.

##### 3.1.3.4 Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂
- **Impregnation mixture**: Mixed at 400 rpm for 90 minutes at 60°C, ensuring the complete incorporation of metal salts, the manganese promoter, and the carbon source to form a uniform and reproducible catalyst precursor.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X-ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was used to confirm the cubic Co₃O₄ phase and crystallinity. Samples were degassed under vacuum at 300 °C for 10 hours before measurement.
- **Instruments and Models**: Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with monochromatic Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. In situ experiments used a BL14B1 beamline (λ = 0.6887 Å) heating samples from 40 to 400 °C at 5 °C/min under a 10% H₂/Ar flow.
- **Test Parameters**: Scanning speed of 0.5°/min over a 2θ range of 10°–80°.
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment is critical to avoid error.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature-Programmed Reduction (H₂-TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Assesses catalyst reduction steps (Co₃O₄ → CoO → metallic Co) using a micro fixed-bed reactor.
- **Instruments and Models**: Fixed-bed reactor coupled with a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Catalyst pretreated in pure Ar at 150 °C for 2 hours, then reduced in 10% H₂/Ar from 40 to 400 °C at 5 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Consistent ramping essential for reproducibility.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform loading and precise temperature control are emphasized.

### 4.3 TEM & HAADF-STEM with EDS
- **Operational Description**: Determines nanoparticle size and dispersion of Co and MnOx.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Tecnai G2 F20 TEM equipped with a Gatan Tridiem 863 P filter and EDX detector operating at 200 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Standard sample dispersion and mounting; calibration done via measuring ~300 particles.
- **Key Notes**: Accurate particle sizing confirms dispersion differences.

### 4.4 H₂ Chemisorption
- **Operational Description**: Quantifies surface metallic Co sites.
- **Characterization Conditions**: 100 mg sample reduced in pure H₂ (50 mL/min) at 400 °C for 5 hours; measurement conducted at 150 °C after cooling.
- **Key Notes**: Critical for assessing catalyst dispersion.

### 4.5 In Situ DRIFTS
- **Operational Description**: Monitors CO adsorption and hydrogenation behavior.
- **Characterization Conditions**: 50 mg sample pretreated in H₂ (20 mL/min) at 400 °C for 5 hours, then cooled; baseline recorded in Ar (20 mL/min). Exposed to 30% CO in Ar with stepwise heating from 40 to 220 °C at 5 °C/min, spectra collected every 10 °C (4 cm⁻¹ resolution, 64 scans).
- **Key Notes**: Stepwise collection allows detailed observation of surface species.

### 4.6 X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: Evaluates Co chemical states and oxidation ratios.
- **Instruments and Models**: Axis Ultra spectrometer with an Al monochromatic X-ray source.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements at room temperature in ultrahigh vacuum (~5×10⁻⁹ torr); binding energies calibrated to the C1s peak (284.8 eV) after catalyst pretreatment in 10% H₂/Ar at 400 °C.
- **Key Notes**: Inert transfer minimizes contamination and ensures data reproducibility.

### 4.7 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: Determines surface area and porosity via N₂ adsorption/desorption.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Analysis based on standard isotherms with the BJH method at a relative pressure of 0.99.
- **Key Notes**: Surface area data are essential for correlating with catalytic performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
Activation temperature is critical for converting precursor oxide phases into the catalytically active metal. In this study, the catalyst is pre‑treated by hydrogen reduction at 400 °C. At this temperature, Co₃O₄ is effectively reduced to metallic Co, forming the active phase while also preserving the nano‐interface with MnOx. This precise temperature is selected to ensure complete reduction without inducing sintering, thereby maintaining the catalyst stability necessary for long-term Fischer–Tropsch operation.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
The catalyst reduction process is carried out under a controlled pressure of 0.1 MPa. This relatively low pressure during the pre‐reaction activation is intentionally chosen to facilitate effective reduction kinetics while preparing the catalyst for subsequent high‑pressure reaction conditions. The controlled pressure also minimizes potential agglomeration of the active metal particles, supporting uniform particle dispersion.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
A pure hydrogen (H₂) flow is employed as the activation gas. The highly reducing environment provided by pure H₂ ensures the complete conversion of cobalt oxide species to metallic cobalt without yielding any partial carbide species. This approach not only promotes efficient metal reduction but also aids in generating well‑dispersed Co nanoparticles, which are crucial for achieving high catalytic performance and selectivity during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Gas Flow and Space-Time Velocity
During the reduction step, a fixed H₂ flow rate of 50 mL/min is applied based on the catalyst mass. For reaction conditions, the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is defined as 4.8 L·g₍cat₎⁻¹·h⁻¹. This parameter ensures adequate exposure of the catalyst to the activation gas, balancing reduction efficiency with the prevention of over‑reduction or incomplete activation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
The activation process is maintained for 5 hours at 400 °C. This duration is sufficient to achieve a full and uniform transformation of the catalyst precursor into the active metallic state, ensuring robust catalyst performance. Extended testing confirms that this activation time contributes to enhanced metal dispersion and durable catalytic stability during prolonged Fischer–Tropsch operation.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
• Definition: The reaction temperature governs CO activation, chain growth, and product distribution in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
• Experimental Conditions: The reaction was performed within a temperature range of 210 °C to 230 °C.  
• Observations: Testing demonstrated that the Co–MnOx–(C)/SiO₂ catalyst exhibited significantly higher CO conversion and olefin selectivity in this range, with the STY of C₅⁺ olefins reaching 0.103 g·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹ at 230 °C—a performance noted to be 5.2 times that of Co/SiO₂.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
• Definition: The reaction pressure impacts the concentration of syngas components, catalytic activation, and hydrocarbon chain length formation.  
• Experimental Conditions: The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was conducted at an operating pressure of 1.0 MPa using a high-pressure fixed-bed reactor.  
• Observations: A pressure of 1.0 MPa ensures enhanced CO conversion and favorable kinetics, thereby promoting long-chain hydrocarbon formation while supporting catalyst stability over extended time-on-stream.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
• Definition: The H₂/CO ratio is critical for balancing hydrogenation and chain propagation, directly affecting the distribution between olefins and paraffins.  
• Experimental Conditions: A fixed H₂/CO ratio of 2.0 was maintained throughout the experiments.  
• Observations: Operating at a 2.0 ratio helped achieve a high selectivity toward long-chain α-olefins by inhibiting excessive hydrogenation and promoting CO dissociation at the catalyst interface.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
• Definition: Gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) represents the flow rate of syngas relative to the catalyst mass, influencing both conversion efficiency and product selectivity.  
• Experimental Conditions: GHSV was varied between 2.4 and 9.6 L·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹.  
• Observations: Lower GHSV values led to higher CO conversion (up to 75%), while higher GHSV increased the selectivity for C₂⁺ and C₅⁺ olefins. This systematic adjustment underscores the trade-off between conversion and selectivity, ultimately contributing to the catalyst’s long-term stability over 400 hours.

These well-defined conditions, directly extracted from the study, not only provide reproducibility but also enhance our understanding of the delicate balance required to optimize catalyst performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), with crystallite sizes calculated using the Scherrer equation.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the calcined catalysts reveal the presence of cubic Co₃O₄ (JCPDS 01–076-1802), with the most intense diffraction peak at 2θ = 36.8°. Carbon-mediated catalysts (Co-(C)/SiO₂ and Co-MnOx-(C)/SiO₂) display much broader peaks than their non-carbon-mediated counterparts, indicating lower crystallinity and smaller Co₃O₄ nanoparticles. After reaction, metallic Co (44.2°, JCPDS 00–015-0806) becomes predominant, with weak CoO reflections observed. No Mn species are detected by XRD.
- **Observations:** The broadening of XRD peaks in carbon-mediated catalysts signifies the formation of defective, nanosized Co₃O₄, which is beneficial for catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM), and HAADF-STEM with EDS mapping were used to assess particle size, dispersion, and spatial distribution of active phases.
- **Findings:** TEM analysis shows average Co nanoparticle sizes of 8.0 ± 1.4 nm (Co/SiO₂), 8.2 ± 1.5 nm (Co-MnOx/SiO₂), 6.7 ± 1.1 nm (Co-(C)/SiO₂), and 6.2 ± 1.1 nm (Co-MnOx-(C)/SiO₂). HAADF-STEM and EDS mapping confirm intimate contact between Co and MnOx in carbon-mediated catalysts, with no evidence of Mn–Co segregation.
- **Observations:** The carbon-mediated approach yields smaller, more uniformly dispersed Co nanoparticles and maximizes stable Co–MnOx nano-interfaces, which are preserved after reaction and resist sintering.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET) and BJH analyses were performed to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution.
- **Findings:** All calcined catalysts exhibit similar mesoporous textures, as confirmed by BET and BJH methods. The mesoporosity ensures effective reactant diffusion, with external mass diffusion limitations found to be negligible under typical reaction conditions.
- **Observations:** The mesoporous structure supports high catalytic efficiency by facilitating reactant access to active sites.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Valence States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) were used to analyze surface composition and oxidation states.
- **Findings:** XPS reveals Co³⁺ (779.0–779.4 eV), Co²⁺ (780.6–781.5 eV), and Co⁰ (778.2–778.6 eV) species. Carbon-mediated catalysts show significantly higher surface Co²⁺/Co³⁺ ratios (7.4 for Co-(C)/SiO₂, 7.9 for Co-MnOx-(C)/SiO₂) compared to non-carbon-mediated samples (1.8 and 2.4, respectively), indicating abundant oxygen vacancies. XANES confirms a shift toward lower Co chemical states, with absorption edges closer to CoO and lower white line intensity.
- **Observations:** The increased Co²⁺ content and oxygen vacancies enhance CO adsorption and stabilize Co–MnOx interfaces, directly contributing to improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Reducibility and Dispersion
- **Description:** H₂-temperature programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and H₂ chemisorption were employed to assess reducibility and metal dispersion.
- **Findings:** Carbon-mediated catalysts exhibit intensified γ peaks at higher temperatures in H₂-TPR, indicating smaller, highly dispersed Co nanoparticles with strong support interactions. The β peak (CoO to Co⁰ reduction) is broader and shifted to higher temperatures for Mn-promoted samples. H₂ chemisorption shows higher dispersion for carbon-mediated catalysts (e.g., 12.6% for Co-(C)/SiO₂, 11.3% for Co-MnOx-(C)/SiO₂).
- **Observations:** Lower reducibility and higher dispersion are linked to enhanced stability and activity, with Mn promotion further decreasing reducibility.

#### 6.1.6 Lattice Distortion and Oxygen Vacancies
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy was used to probe lattice structure and defect states.
- **Findings:** The A₁g Raman bands for Co-(C)/SiO₂ (30 cm⁻¹) and Co-MnOx-(C)/SiO₂ (50 cm⁻¹) are broader than those for Co/SiO₂ (16 cm⁻¹) and Co-MnOx/SiO₂ (22 cm⁻¹). A red shift of the A₁g mode from 670 cm⁻¹ (Co-(C)/SiO₂) to 657 cm⁻¹ (Co-MnOx-(C)/SiO₂) is observed, indicating increased cation disorder and oxygen vacancies.
- **Observations:** These features confirm the presence of defective Co₃O₄ with abundant oxygen-deficient sites, which are crucial for anchoring MnOx and promoting catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.7 Surface Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was conducted to study CO adsorption and hydrogenation.
- **Findings:** Carbon-mediated catalysts, especially Co–MnOx–(C)/SiO₂, display higher intensities of adsorbed CO* and *CHₓ bands, indicating enhanced CO* (C*) coverage and a high surface CO*/H* ratio due to inhibited H₂ co-adsorption.
- **Observations:** Improved CO adsorption and dissociation at the Co–MnOx interface are directly linked to the observed high selectivity for long-chain α-olefins.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet molar flow rates of CO, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** The optimized Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂ catalyst (Mn/Co = 0.2) achieves a CO conversion of approximately 80% at 220 °C, 1.0 MPa, and GHSV = 2.4 L·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹, maintaining this value for over 400 hours on stream. At 210 °C and GHSV = 4.8 L·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹, conversion is 11.5%. Increasing GHSV from 2.4 to 9.6 L·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹ results in a decrease from 75% to 11% conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both Mn promotion and carbon-mediated synthesis are essential for high and stable CO conversion. Reference catalysts without Mn or carbon mediation exhibit lower activity and faster deactivation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At the optimal Mn/Co ratio (0.2), the Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂ catalyst maintains relatively low CO₂ selectivity, balancing high activity and desired product selectivity. Excessive or insufficient Mn increases CO₂ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The carbon-mediated, Mn-promoted catalyst suppresses CO₂ formation, with the lowest selectivity observed at the optimal Mn/Co ratio.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (210–230 °C, 1.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2.0), CH₄ selectivity is minimized in the Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂ catalyst compared to references, contributing to higher olefin yields.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of MnOx and carbon mediation effectively suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  S_{C_3^+} = \frac{\sum_{i=3}^{n} N_{C,i} \cdot F_{i,\text{out}}}{\sum_{\text{all products}} N_{C,j} \cdot F_{j,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂, C₃⁺ selectivity is 50.1% at 210 °C, 42.0% at 220 °C, and 31.8% at 230 °C (GHSV = 4.8 L·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹).
- **Observations and Trends:** Selectivity for C₃⁺ hydrocarbons decreases with increasing temperature, but remains significantly higher for the optimized catalyst than for references.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory model, represents the probability of chain growth:
  - Calculated from the slope of the log(mole fraction/n) vs. carbon number plot.
- **Experimental Data:** For C₉–C₁₄ olefins, α values are: Co/SiO₂ (0.57), Co-(C)/SiO₂ (0.56), Co-MnOx/SiO₂ (0.63), and Co-MnOx-(C)/SiO₂ (0.70).
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest α is achieved with both Mn promotion and carbon mediation, indicating enhanced selectivity for long-chain olefins.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂ maintains ~80% CO conversion for over 400 h, with negligible deactivation. Reference catalysts show more rapid declines, especially Co-(C)/SiO₂, which deactivates within 20 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The carbon-mediated strategy stabilizes Co–MnOx nano-interfaces, effectively suppressing sintering and deactivation.

#### 6.2.7 Additional Performance Indicators
- **Cobalt-Time Yield (CTY):** At 220 °C, CTY for Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂ is 5.6 × 10⁻⁵ molCO·gCo⁻¹·s⁻¹, more than double that of reference catalysts.
- **Space-Time Yield (STY):** C₅⁺ STY reaches 0.103 g·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹ and 0.79 g·g_Co⁻¹·h⁻¹ at 230 °C.
- **Observations:** These metrics confirm the superior productivity and efficiency of the optimized catalyst.

**Summary:** The Co‐MnOx‐(C)/SiO₂ catalyst, synthesized via a carbon-mediated strategy with optimal Mn/Co ratio, demonstrates outstanding CO conversion, suppressed CO₂ and CH₄ selectivity, high C₃⁺ and C₅⁺ olefin selectivity, elevated α factor, and exceptional long-term stability, outperforming all reference catalysts under identical FTS conditions.
