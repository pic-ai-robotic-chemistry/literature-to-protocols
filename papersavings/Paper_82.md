#### • Step 1: Precursor Impregnation  
1. Prepare a cobalt precursor solution (typically cobalt nitrate adjusted to yield a 15 wt.% loading) and impregnate the alumina support under continuous stirring using a standard impregnation setup.  
   - Reagents: Cobalt nitrate solution  
   - Equipment: Impregnation apparatus, magnetic stirrer  
   - Observation: Uniform uptake by the support pores  

#### • Step 2: Tin Addition (for Modified Catalysts)

   - Reagents: Tin precursor solution  
   - Equipment: Dropwise addition setup, stirring apparatus

#### • Step 3: Drying  
3. Dry the impregnated samples under controlled conditions to remove the solvent without causing precursor segregation.  
   - Conditions: Controlled drying temperature and duration (parameters are selected based on reproducibility requirements)

#### • Step 4: Calcination  
4. Calcine the dried samples to transform the precursors into their corresponding oxide forms.  
   - Conditions: Optimized temperature (typically within the 400–500 °C range) and duration are applied to maintain high metal dispersion  
   - Equipment: High‑temperature furnace  

#### • Step 5: Pre‑reduction  
5. Pre‑reduce the calcined catalysts at 450 °C for 1 hour in a flow of 60% H₂ in Ar. This step converts the oxides to the active metallic state required for catalytic performance.

The impregnation method is advantageous due to its simplicity, scalability, and reproducibility. ## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Morphology and Metal Dispersion
- **Description:** Morphological features and metal dispersion were assessed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), providing insight into particle size distribution and uniformity across catalyst samples.
- **Findings:** TEM micrographs and particle size analyses revealed that both Sn-free and Sn-modified Co/Al₂O₃ catalysts possess well-dispersed cobalt particles. The metal dispersion, calculated from TEM-derived particle size distributions, consistently ranged between 7% and 10% for all samples, regardless of tin content. The cobalt metal loading was maintained at approximately 15 wt.%.
- **Observations:** The addition of tin at varying Sn/Co molar ratios (1:120, 1:60, 1:30) did not induce significant particle agglomeration or alter the uniformity of dispersion, indicating that the synthesis and reduction protocols effectively preserved catalyst morphology.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area
- **Description:** The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was employed to determine the specific surface area of the catalyst support, a parameter critical for reactant accessibility and overall catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** All catalyst samples, including both Sn-free and Sn-modified variants, exhibited a BET surface area of approximately 110 m²/g.
- **Observations:** The consistent surface area across samples suggests that tin incorporation does not significantly affect the textural properties of the alumina support.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Species and Chemical State (Operando DRIFTS/FT-IR)
- **Description:** Operando diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) and FT-IR were utilized to monitor surface species under reaction conditions, providing direct evidence of adsorbed intermediates and their reactivity.
- **Findings:** Key vibrational bands were identified: linearly adsorbed CO at 2031 cm⁻¹, multi-bonded CO at ~1860 cm⁻¹, formate species at ~1380 cm⁻¹, and carboxylates at ~1460 cm⁻¹. Increasing tin content led to a marked decrease in the intensity of bands associated with multi-bonded CO and surface hydrocarbons, while the linear CO band remained largely unaffected. The formate band persisted in Sn-modified samples but exhibited reduced reactivity, as evidenced by its stability after CO removal.
- **Observations:** Tin selectively poisons interfacial sites responsible for multi-bonded CO adsorption and diminishes the reactivity of formate species, correlating with reduced hydrocarbon and methanol formation.

#### 6.1.4 Hydrogen Adsorption Capacity
- **Description:** Hydrogen chemisorption measurements, integrated with transient fixed-bed reactor experiments and mass spectrometry, quantified the catalysts’ H₂ adsorption capacity, a key factor in hydrogenation reactions.
- **Findings:** The Sn-free catalyst exhibited an H₂ adsorption capacity of 73 μmol/g at 100 °C. With increasing Sn loading, this capacity declined sharply, reaching only 2 μmol/g for the highest Sn/Co ratio (1:30).
- **Observations:** The pronounced reduction in H₂ adsorption with higher tin content demonstrates that tin effectively poisons sites required for H₂ activation, directly impacting the catalyst’s ability to hydrogenate surface intermediates and thus its overall Fischer–Tropsch performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (220 °C, 30% CO/60% H₂ in Ar, GHSV ≈ 18,000 h⁻¹), all catalysts exhibited CO conversion below 5%. This low conversion was consistent across the undoped Co/Al₂O₃ and Sn-modified samples (Sn/Co = 1:120, 1:60, 1:30).
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing tin content did not enhance CO conversion; instead, it correlated with a marked decline in overall catalytic activity. No optimal Sn/Co ratio for improved conversion was observed.

#### 6.2.2 Methanol and Hydrocarbon Formation Rates
- **Description:** Product formation rates (e.g., methanol, hydrocarbons) are reported as the amount of product generated per unit catalyst mass per unit time (μmol g⁻¹ s⁻¹), reflecting catalyst productivity.
- **Experimental Data:** The Sn-free Co/Al₂O₃ catalyst produced hydrocarbons as the main products, with methanol as a minor by-product. Upon tin addition, both hydrocarbon and methanol formation rates declined sharply. For example, H₂ adsorption capacity—a proxy for hydrogenation ability—dropped from 73 μmol g⁻¹ (Sn-free) to 2 μmol g⁻¹ (Sn/Co = 1:30), paralleling the decrease in product formation rates.
- **Observations and Trends:** The suppression of methanol and hydrocarbon production with increasing Sn content is attributed to the poisoning of hydrogen activation and interfacial sites, as evidenced by operando DRIFTS and product analysis.

#### 6.2.3 H₂ Adsorption Capacity
- **Description:** H₂ adsorption capacity measures the amount of hydrogen adsorbed on the catalyst surface (μmol g⁻¹), indicating the availability of active sites for hydrogenation.
- **Experimental Data:** The Sn-free catalyst exhibited an H₂ adsorption capacity of 73 μmol g⁻¹. This value decreased dramatically with higher Sn loading, reaching 2 μmol g⁻¹ for the Sn/Co = 1:30 sample.
- **Observations and Trends:** The strong reduction in H₂ adsorption with increasing Sn content directly correlates with diminished methanol and hydrocarbon formation, underscoring the critical role of hydrogen activation in syngas conversion.

#### 6.2.4 Selectivity to CO₂ and Hydrocarbons
- **Description:** Selectivity is defined as the fraction of converted CO yielding a specific product, calculated as:
\[
\text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{Product,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit numerical selectivity values are not provided, DRIFTS analysis revealed a decrease in carboxylate (CO₂) band intensity with increasing Sn, indicating reduced CO₂ formation. Hydrocarbon selectivity also declined in parallel with overall activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The reduction in both CO₂ and hydrocarbon selectivity with higher Sn loading reflects the overall suppression of Fischer–Tropsch and water–gas shift activity due to site poisoning.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability refers to the maintenance of activity over time, typically assessed by monitoring CO conversion and product formation rates during extended operation.
- **Experimental Data:** No significant deactivation was reported during the standard reaction period; however, the consistently low activity of Sn-modified catalysts suggests that site poisoning by tin is the dominant factor limiting performance rather than time-dependent deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The primary cause of reduced performance is the irreversible loss of active sites upon tin addition, rather than gradual catalyst deactivation under reaction conditions.

**Summary:**  
The addition of tin to Co/Al₂O₃ catalysts for syngas conversion leads to a pronounced decline in CO conversion, methanol and hydrocarbon formation rates, H₂ adsorption capacity, and selectivity to both CO₂ and hydrocarbons. These effects are directly linked to the poisoning of hydrogen activation and interfacial sites by tin, as confirmed by spectroscopic and product analyses. No improvement in catalyst stability or performance was observed with increasing Sn content; instead, higher tin loadings consistently impaired catalytic function.
