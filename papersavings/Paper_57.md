## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe/SiO₂ and Fe–M/SiO₂ (M = Bi, Pb) catalysts is carried out using incipient wetness impregnation, ensuring the use of aqueous nitrate solutions to achieve target metal loadings and precise dopant ratios (Fe : M = 100 : 2, corresponding to ca. 10 wt % Fe). The process emphasizes reproducibility by standardizing chemical reagent concentrations and solution formulations.

#### • Incipient Wetness Impregnation Method (Co‑impregnated Variant)
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation:**  
   - Aqueous solutions of hydrous iron nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O), bismuth nitrate (Bi(NO₃)₃·5H₂O), and lead nitrate (Pb(NO₃)₂) are prepared at calculated concentrations.
   - The silica support (CARIACT Q‑10, Fuji Silysia) is impregnated with these solutions to ensure uniform dispersion.
2. **Optional Precipitation–Deposition:**  
   - In some protocols, an additional dropwise addition of the support precursor is performed after initial mixing to enhance metal deposition.
3. **Drying:**  
   - The impregnated catalyst is dried overnight at 100 °C in a controlled oven, which is critical to remove excess moisture and prevent inhomogeneous dispersion.
4. **Calcination:**  
   - The catalyst is then calcined in air at 400 °C for 6 h, using a ramp rate of 1 °C/min. This step facilitates the formation of iron oxide species that ultimately convert to the active phases under reaction conditions.
5. **Post‑treatment:**  
   - Although not explicitly detailed in the synthesis section, the catalyst activation protocol later involves reduction and passivation under controlled conditions.

### Comparative Analysis of the Methods
• The incipient wetness impregnation method offers simplicity and scalability, requiring minimal specialized equipment (e.g., oven for drying and furnace for calcination).  
• Its advantages include precise control over metal deposition and reproducibility, while potential drawbacks may arise from the need for rigorous control of solution concentrations to ensure uniform distribution, especially when incorporating a precipitation–deposition step.

Overall, this synthesis methodology ensures intimate contact between the active iron phase and promoter species (Bi or Pb), which is crucial for enhancing catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis under mild reaction conditions.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the synthesis of the catalysts via incipient wetness impregnation is outlined. Two catalyst types are addressed: the unpromoted Fe/SiO₂ and the promoted Fe–M/SiO₂ (M = Bi, Pb), each prepared to achieve a target metal loading of approximately 10 wt % Fe with a dopant ratio of Fe : M of 100 : 2.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe/SiO₂
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed in an amount calculated according to the target Fe loading. The precise mass is determined based on quantitative formulation, using an analytical balance to ensure accuracy.

##### 3.1.1.2 Fe–M/SiO₂
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed as recommended for achieving 10 wt % iron content.
- **Bi(NO₃)₃·5H₂O / Pb(NO₃)₂**: Weighed in quantities set to maintain a promoter level corresponding to a Fe : M ratio of 100 : 2. The amounts are calculated so that the promoters are incorporated in a reproducible and controlled manner.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe/SiO₂
- **Deionized water**: Transferred by incipient wetness impregnation. The water volume is adjusted to completely dissolve the Fe precursor and fully wet the silica support using appropriate volumetric equipment.

##### 3.1.2.2 Fe–M/SiO₂
- **Deionized water**: Added under the same incipient wetness technique. This facilitates the dissolution and simultaneous incorporation of both the Fe nitrate and the promoter nitrates (Bi(NO₃)₃·5H₂O or Pb(NO₃)₂) into a homogeneous aqueous solution.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe/SiO₂
- **Impregnated mixture**: The support and the Fe nitrate solution are mixed by ensuring complete wetting, then the resulting slurry is dried overnight at 100 °C prior to calcination.

##### 3.1.3.2 Fe–M/SiO₂
- **Impregnated blend**: Mixed following the incipient wetness method to achieve uniform dispersion of both metal precursors and promoters. After thorough mixing, the catalyst is dried overnight at 100 °C and calcined in air at 400 °C for 6 hours, using a ramp rate of 1 °C/min to ensure reproducible formation of the active catalyst phases.

This structured approach, with separate attention to weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing for each catalyst, ensures clarity and reproducibility in the preparation of Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/SiO₂  
##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: Hydrous iron nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O) is weighed to achieve an approximate 10 wt% Fe loading when calculated relative to the mass of commercial amorphous silica (CARIACT Q‑10).  
- **Conditions**: Reagents are dissolved in deionized water under ambient temperature. The calculated aqueous solution ensures that the support is fully saturated via incipient wetness impregnation.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve the required amount of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in water to obtain a clear solution.  
  - Impregnate the silica support by slowly adding the aqueous nitrate solution until complete pore saturation is achieved.  
  - Allow uniform distribution of the solution across the support surface.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure accurate measurement of precursor and maintain complete wetting of the support for uniform metal dispersion.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly and Post-Processing  
- **Reagents/Equipment**: No additional reagents are added at this stage.  
- **Conditions**: Follow standard incipient wetness protocols; no specific stirring speed is required.  
- **Process**:  
  - Following impregnation, proceed directly to drying without any washing steps to avoid loss of precursor.  
- **Key Steps**: Confirm that the impregnated material shows uniform liquid absorption before moving on.

##### Step [3]: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying Conditions**: Dry the impregnated support overnight at 100 °C in a calibrated oven to remove excess water uniformly.  
- **Calcination Conditions**: Calcine the dried material in air at 400 °C for 6 hours with a temperature ramp of 1 °C/min.  
- **Process**:  
  - Load the dried catalyst into a calcination furnace, and monitor the ramp rate and hold time to ensure structural integrity.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain a controlled temperature increase to prevent precursor decomposition before complete drying.

##### Step [4]: Activation  
- **Process**: Activate the catalyst under a controlled flow of CO at atmospheric pressure, typically at 350 °C for 10 hours, ensuring the reduction of iron species and preparing the active sites for Fischer–Tropsch catalysis.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe–M/SiO₂ (M = Bi, Pb)  
The Fe–M catalyst follows a similar workflow with the inclusion of promoter precursors.  

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: In addition to Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, add Bi(NO₃)₃·5H₂O or Pb(NO₃)₂ as calculated to establish a dopant ratio of Fe:M at 100:2.  
- **Process**:  
  - Prepare individual aqueous solutions of the iron and promoter nitrates.  
  - Either coimpregnate the support simultaneously with both solutions or combine via mechanical mixing post-impregnation to achieve intimate contact.
  
Steps [2]–[4] (Catalyst Assembly, Drying & Calcination, and Activation) are performed identically to those described for the Fe/SiO₂ system, ensuring comparable dispersion and subsequent performance enhancement through optimized promoter migration during reaction conditions.

These detailed steps ensure a reproducible protocol that guides the preparation from raw nitrate salts through to an active Fischer–Tropsch catalyst.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

Catalyst activation is essential for exposing the active sites needed for efficient Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. In this work, activation is achieved through two key steps within the synthesis workflow: a calcination step and a subsequent pre‐reaction CO treatment.

#### 5.1.1 Calcination Activation
- **Definition:** Calcination serves to convert the metal nitrate precursors into well-dispersed oxide phases on the silica support, establishing a uniform catalyst structure.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Following an overnight drying at 100 °C, the catalysts are calcined in air at 400 °C for 6 hours using a controlled temperature ramp of 1 °C/min. This deliberate heating schedule minimizes the risk of sintering and ensures the complete decomposition of the nitrate precursors into iron oxides.
- **Observations:** The calcination step yields oxide particles with a narrow size distribution (average particle sizes in the range of 15–17 nm for hematite), which is critical for achieving subsequent high dispersion during reduction.

#### 5.1.2 Pre-reaction CO Activation
- **Definition:** After calcination, the catalyst undergoes a pre‐reaction activation treatment designed to reduce the iron oxide to the catalytically active phase and to facilitate the formation of iron carbide. The CO-rich environment also aids in oxygen scavenging and, in the case of promoted catalysts, assists in the migration of low–melting point elements (e.g., Bi and Pb) into intimate contact with the active sites.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed at 350 °C under atmospheric pressure. A CO‐rich gas stream—essentially pure CO—is used during this step.
- **Observations:** Operating at 350 °C in a CO environment promotes efficient reduction while avoiding excessive sintering. Although no explicit value is provided for the space–time velocity during this step, the conditions ensure that the active iron carbide phase is established efficiently, leading to an enhanced catalytic performance in the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This two‐stage activation strategy integrates the benefits of controlled calcination and a mild CO‐treatment, ensuring reproducible and effective catalyst activation for direct synthesis of light olefins from syngas under mild reaction conditions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis as it governs not only the activation of syngas but also the state of the added promoters. In these experiments, the catalyst evaluations were performed at a temperature of 350 °C. At this temperature, the low melting points of bismuth (271 °C) and lead (327 °C) enable them to attain a pseudoliquid state. This facilitates their migration toward the iron carbide active sites, significantly enhancing the reaction rate and promoting the selective synthesis of light olefins. Observations indicate that under these conditions, the promoted catalysts maintain improved activity compared to the deactivating behavior of the unpromoted Fe/SiO₂ variant.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Pressure is another essential factor that influences syngas concentration and product selectivity. The experiments were carried out over a broad pressure range from 1 to 20 bar. This variation in pressure allows for an evaluation of the catalyst behavior under both mild and more rigorous operating conditions. The observations suggest that while higher pressures can lead to enhanced CO conversion, they also affect the distribution of hydrocarbon products. Notably, at lower pressures, the promoted catalysts remain active, in contrast to the unpromoted version which shows deactivation, underscoring the beneficial impact of promoter addition.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The molar ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed gas is maintained at 1 throughout the catalytic tests. This balanced ratio is pivotal in ensuring that the reaction proceeds efficiently, optimizing the conversion of syngas into the desired hydrocarbon fractions. The controlled H₂/CO ratio contributes to an even hydrocarbon distribution, favoring the formation of light olefins while minimizing excess methane production.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is defined as the volume of syngas passing through a unit mass of catalyst per hour. In these experiments, GHSV values were varied—specifically, values on the order of 3.4 L g⁻¹ h⁻¹ were employed to assess their influence on reaction kinetics. Adjusting the GHSV allows for precise control over reactant contact time, which, in turn, affects CO conversion rates and overall catalyst performance. Observations revealed that while higher GHSV conditions slightly reduced CO conversion, they contributed to enhanced catalyst stability over prolonged durations.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Particle Size (XRD)
- **Description:** X-ray Diffraction (XRD) was employed to identify the crystalline phases present in the catalysts and to estimate particle sizes of both iron oxide and iron carbide phases. This analysis is critical for understanding the active phase evolution during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis and its relationship to catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the calcined catalysts revealed peaks characteristic of hematite (Fe₂O₃), with calculated particle sizes in the range of 15–17 nm. After Fischer–Tropsch reaction, the XRD patterns displayed very broad peaks corresponding to iron carbide phases, with particle sizes estimated at 4–6 nm. The addition of Bi or Pb promoters did not significantly alter the dispersion or size of either phase.
- **Observations:** The consistent particle sizes across promoted and unpromoted catalysts indicate that the observed enhancements in catalytic activity are not due to changes in dispersion, but rather to modifications in the intrinsic activity of the active sites.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Promoter Distribution (XRF, TEM-EDX)
- **Description:** X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) was used to quantify the elemental composition of the catalysts before and after reaction, while Transmission Electron Microscopy with Energy Dispersive X-ray analysis (TEM-EDX) provided insights into the spatial distribution of promoter species relative to iron nanoparticles.
- **Findings:** XRF analysis confirmed that the intended Fe : M (M = Bi, Pb) ratio of 100 : 2 was maintained throughout the reaction, with no detectable loss of promoter elements. TEM-EDX revealed that, in mechanical mixtures, Bi was initially uniformly distributed. Post-reaction, EDX mapping showed migration and preferential localization of Bi at the interface with iron carbide nanoparticles.
- **Observations:** The migration of Bi and Pb promoters under reaction conditions leads to intimate contact with iron carbide phases, which is associated with enhanced oxygen removal and improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Nanostructure (TEM)
- **Description:** High-resolution TEM was utilized to examine the morphology and size distribution of catalyst nanoparticles, providing direct visualization of the active phases and promoter interactions.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed Fe nanoparticles ranging from 10–40 nm, while Bi particles were significantly smaller due to their lower content. After catalysis, Bi was observed to accumulate near iron carbide nanoparticles, corroborating the promoter migration detected by EDX.
- **Observations:** The uniform initial dispersion and subsequent migration of promoter species support the hypothesis that dynamic promoter–iron interactions are central to the observed catalytic enhancements.

#### 6.1.4 Magnetic Properties During Activation (VSM)
- **Description:** Vibrating-sample magnetometry (VSM) was conducted to monitor changes in magnetic properties during catalyst activation, reflecting phase transformations relevant to catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** The saturation magnetization curve evolved as the catalyst was heated under CO, indicating the reduction of iron oxide and formation of magnetic iron carbide phases.
- **Observations:** The formation of iron carbide, as tracked by VSM, aligns with the emergence of the catalytically active phase for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis at 350 °C, H₂/CO = 1, and GHSV = 3.4 L g⁻¹ h⁻¹, the unpromoted Fe/SiO₂ catalyst exhibits low CO conversion, with no activity at 5 bar. In contrast, Bi- and Pb-promoted catalysts show markedly higher conversions:  
  - **FeBi/SiO₂:** 75% (10 bar), 58%, 49%, and 17% at varying conditions.  
  - **FePb/SiO₂:** 60% (10 bar), 55% (1 bar).  
  - **Mechanical mixtures:** 38% (Fe/SiO₂ + Bi/SiO₂), 34% (Fe/SiO₂ + Pb/SiO₂) at 10 bar.
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition (Bi, Pb) results in a 5–10× increase in CO conversion compared to unpromoted Fe/SiO₂, with significant activity retained even at atmospheric pressure.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **FeBi/SiO₂:** 48–49% (at 75% and 58% CO conversion), 48% (at 17% conversion).  
  - **FePb/SiO₂:** 45% (60% and 55% CO conversion).  
  - **Fe/SiO₂:** CO₂ selectivity increases with conversion, approaching 50% only at high conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoted catalysts maintain high CO₂ selectivity (∼45–49%) even at lower conversions, indicating enhanced water–gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Fe/SiO₂:** 14–15% (350 °C, 10 bar).  
  - **FeBi/SiO₂ and FePb/SiO₂:** 24–27% under similar conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane selectivity increases with promoter addition and higher CO conversion, while lower pressures and promoters favor light olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming C₃ and higher hydrocarbons:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **FeBi/SiO₂ (350 °C, 10 bar, GHSV 13.4 L g⁻¹ h⁻¹):** C₂–C₄= (olefins) 36%, C₂–C₄− (paraffins) 16%, C₅⁺ 21%.  
  - **FePb/SiO₂:** C₂–C₄= 31%, C₂–C₄− 11%, C₅⁺ 38%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoted catalysts enhance light olefin (C₂–C₄=) selectivity and modulate the distribution among C₃⁺ products.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model:
\[
\log\left(\frac{W_n}{n}\right) = n \log \alpha + \text{const}
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **FeBi/SiO₂:** α = 0.35 (1 bar), 0.46–0.48 (10 bar), 0.47 (GHSV 6.7 L g⁻¹ h⁻¹).  
  - **FePb/SiO₂:** α = 0.39 (1 bar), 0.48 (10 bar), 0.54 (250 °C, 5 bar).  
  - **Fe/SiO₂:** α = 0.42–0.44.
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition generally increases α, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons; lower pressure reduces α, enhancing light olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion or Iron Time Yield (FTY) over time. Deactivation rate is the decline in activity per hour:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \ (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Promoted catalysts (FeBi/SiO₂, FePb/SiO₂) exhibit stable or increasing activity during time-on-stream, while unpromoted Fe/SiO₂ deactivates rapidly. FTY for FeBi/SiO₂ reaches 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ mol gFe⁻¹ s⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of Bi and Pb significantly enhances catalyst stability, attributed to promoter migration and improved oxygen scavenging at the iron carbide interface.

#### 6.2.7 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY quantifies the moles of CO converted per gram of iron per second.
- **Experimental Data:** FeBi/SiO₂ achieves FTY of 2.4 × 10⁻⁴ mol gFe⁻¹ s⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** This value is among the highest reported for iron-based FT catalysts, underscoring the effectiveness of Bi promotion.

---
**Summary:** The addition of Bi and Pb promoters to Fe/SiO₂ catalysts dramatically improves CO conversion, selectivity to light olefins, chain growth probability, and catalyst stability under mild FT synthesis conditions. These enhancements are linked to the unique pseudoliquid behavior and migration of the promoters, facilitating intimate contact with iron carbide nanoparticles and efficient oxygen removal.
