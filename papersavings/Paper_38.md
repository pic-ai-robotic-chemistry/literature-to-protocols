## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps
The catalyst synthesis employs a MOF‐based route using ZIF‑67 as the precursor to prepare highly loaded Co@SiO₂ catalysts. This method integrates multiple controlled steps that ensure reproducibility and scalable synthesis.

#### Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation
1. Dissolve 2.933 g of Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O in 200 ml of methanol.
2. Dissolve 6.489 g of 2‑methylimidazole in another 200 ml of methanol.
3. Mix the solutions; the initially pink solution turns bright purple, indicating the formation of ZIF‑67.
• Equipment: Magnetic stirrer and filtration apparatus.

#### Precipitation–Deposition
1. Impregnate the as‐formed ZIF‑67 with tetramethyl orthosilicate (TMOS) in a Teflon‑lined autoclave.
2. Heat the autoclave at 333 K for 24 h.
3. Conduct wet N₂ hydrolysis by bubbling N₂ through water in a glass tube setup at 323 K for 30 h to form silica inside the MOF pores.
• Observation: In situ formation of ZIF‑67@SiO₂ as TMOS hydrolyzes within the framework.

#### Drying
1. Air dry the product at 333 K (≈60 °C).
2. Follow with vacuum drying at 373 K (≈100 °C) for 10 h to remove residual solvents.
• Equipment: Drying oven and vacuum system.

#### Pyrolysis
1. Transfer the dried ZIF‑67@SiO₂ to a quartz tubular reactor.
2. Pyrolyze under N₂ atmosphere at a set temperature, choosing one of 773 K, 873 K, or 973 K, for 4 h.
3. Employ a controlled heating ramp of 2 K min⁻¹ to ensure uniform decomposition.
• Outcome: Formation of Co@C–SiO₂ via conversion of the MOF structure and carbonization.

#### Calcination and Post‑Treatment
1. Calcine the pyrolyzed sample in air at 673 K for 2 h using a standard calcination oven to remove the graphitic carbon shells.
2. Finally, perform an in situ reduction in H₂ at 673 K for 10 h to convert Co₃O₄ to metallic cobalt.
• Equipment: Fixed‐bed reactor for reduction.
• Result: Catalysts with high cobalt loading (~50 wt.%) and well‐dispersed, active metallic Co particles suitable for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This structured approach emphasizes chemical precision, detailed reaction conditions, and proper sequencing to achieve reproducible catalyst fabrication.

- **Impregnation Mixture**: Heated in a rotation oven at 333 K overnight for uniform hydrolysis.

These detailed steps, integrating precise weighing, measured liquid transfers, and rigorously controlled mixing, form the foundation of the catalyst synthesis procedure. The consistently applied protocols ensure that the active phase formation—from precursor dissolution through TMOS hydrolysis—is reproducible and directly correlates with the targeted catalytic performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Co@SiO₂ Catalyst (e.g., Co@SiO₂-773)

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 2.933 g Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and 6.489 g 2-methylimidazole dissolved separately in 200 mL methanol.
- **Conditions**: Room temperature; vigorous stirring for 24 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and 2-methylimidazole in separate methanol solutions.
  - Rapidly pour the 2-methylimidazole solution into the cobalt solution under continuous stirring.
  - Maintain vigorous stirring for 24 hours to form a homogeneous ZIF-67 precursor.
  - Collect the resulting bright purple product by filtration, followed by washing with methanol.
  - Dry the collected material at 353 K for 10 hours under vacuum.
- **Key Steps**: Precise timing and vigorous mixing ensure a uniform MOF formation.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: 0.8 g of the synthesized ZIF-67 and 5 mL tetramethyl orthosilicate (TMOS).
- **Conditions**: Impregnation in an autoclave with subsequent wet N₂ hydrolysis at 323 K for 30 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Immerse ZIF-67 in TMOS within an autoclave.
  - Place the autoclave in a rotation oven, ensuring thorough impregnation.
  - Initiate a wet N₂ hydrolysis to facilitate the in situ formation of SiO₂ within the MOF pores.
- **Key Steps**: Controlled impregnation and hydrolysis are critical to integrate the silica matrix uniformly.

##### Step [3]: Pyrolysis, Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Pyrolysis**:
  - **Conditions**: Conduct in a fixed-bed quartz tubular reactor under N₂. Ramp temperature at 2 K min⁻¹ to 773, 873, or 973 K and hold for 4 hours.
  - **Process**: Convert ZIF-67@SiO₂ to Co@C–SiO₂ with controlled cobalt nanoparticle size.
- **Purification & Drying**:
  - Wash the pyrolyzed product with methanol.
  - Dry sequentially: air drying at 333 K and vacuum drying at 373 K for 10 hours.
- **Calcination**:
  - **Conditions**: Calcine in air at 673 K for 2 hours.
  - **Process**: Remove graphitic carbon shells to form Co₃O₄-containing Co@SiO₂.

##### Step [4]: Activation
- **Conditions**: In situ reduction in H₂ at 673 K for 10 hours.
- **Process**: Convert Co₃O₄ to the active metallic cobalt phase, ensuring optimal particle dispersion.

**Caution & Troubleshooting**: Precise control of temperature and reagent addition during each step is essential. Monitoring particularly during the hydrolysis and pyrolysis stages prevents incomplete conversion and cobalt nanoparticle agglomeration, ensuring reproducibility and high catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is an essential pre-reaction treatment designed to convert cobalt oxide species into active metallic cobalt prior to Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This process is performed as an in situ reduction step using pure H₂, ensuring that the final catalyst exhibits excellent reducibility and active site exposure.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for promoting the reduction of cobalt oxides (predominantly Co₃O₄) to metallic cobalt without triggering excessive particle sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at a controlled temperature of 673 K. A precise heating ramp of 2 K/min is employed to reach this temperature steadily.
- **Observations:** Operating at 673 K provides sufficient thermal energy for complete reduction while preserving optimal cobalt dispersion essential for high catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The activation time ensures that the reduction process proceeds uniformly throughout the catalyst, leading to a full transformation of oxidic phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The in situ reduction is maintained for 10 hours in a pure H₂ environment.
- **Observations:** This duration is critical to achieving complete conversion of Co₃O₄ to metallic cobalt, thereby ensuring that all accessible cobalt sites are activated without inducing significant sintering.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The activation gas serves as the reducing agent. Its exclusive use is fundamental in controlling the phase transformation and maintaining catalyst integrity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure H₂ is utilized during the activation process.
- **Observations:** The exclusive use of pure H₂ guarantees a straightforward reduction pathway for the cobalt oxide species, avoiding complications that could arise from mixed gas atmospheres. This controlled environment minimizes the risk of forming undesirable phases and supports uniform nanoparticle size distribution.

The activation conditions—673 K temperature, a heating ramp of 2 K/min, a 10-hour duration, and the use of pure H₂—are exclusively associated with the catalyst synthesis pathway. These parameters have been carefully selected to ensure that the catalyst is properly reduced and optimally prepared for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction testing, distinctly separated from any characterization-specific procedures.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is critical for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis as it governs both the activation of syngas and the kinetics of hydrocarbon chain growth. In these experiments, the reactor was maintained at 483 K. This temperature was chosen to optimize syngas activation, resulting in effective conversion rates while maintaining catalyst stability over extended testing periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure directly influences syngas concentration and can markedly affect product selectivity. The experiments were carried out in fixed-bed microreactors under a pressure range of 20 to 26 bar. This controlled pressure environment not only enhances the contact between the reactants and the catalytic sites but also supports the formation of desired longer-chain hydrocarbons by promoting efficient mass transfer and reaction kinetics.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio plays a pivotal role in determining both the distribution of hydrocarbon products and the overall selectivity of the reaction. Experiments were conducted using H₂/CO ratios of either 1 or 2. According to the reported observations, a lower ratio (1) tends to favor an increase in methane production, whereas a higher ratio (2) promotes olefin formation. This variation is attributed to the influence of the ratio on the reduction pathways and surface chemistry of the cobalt active sites, thereby directing the selectivity toward different hydrocarbon outputs.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Syngas Flow Rate  
The syngas flow rate is an essential parameter affecting the reactor’s space-time velocity and ensuring a consistent supply of reactants. A flow rate of 40 ml/min was maintained during the catalytic tests. This parameter plays a crucial role in controlling the reactant residence time within the reactor. Any fluctuations in this flow can lead to non-uniform reactant distribution, potentially impacting conversion rates and the reproducibility of results. Collectively, these well-defined reaction conditions enable robust catalytic performance and offer a reproducible framework for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis evaluations.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The evolution of crystal phases during catalyst synthesis was monitored by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is critical for identifying active cobalt species and their transformations under different thermal treatments.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the ZIF-67 precursor display characteristic MOF peaks. After pyrolysis under N₂ at 773–973 K, new peaks corresponding to metallic cobalt (2θ = 51.8°, 60.6°) and graphite (2θ = 30.6°) emerge, indicating decomposition of the MOF and formation of cobalt nanoparticles encapsulated in graphitic carbon. Increasing pyrolysis temperature sharpens these peaks, reflecting increased crystallinity and particle size. Subsequent calcination in air at 673 K removes the carbon shells and oxidizes cobalt to Co₃O₄, as evidenced by the disappearance of metallic Co and graphite peaks and the appearance of Co₃O₄ peaks. After in situ H₂ reduction, metallic Co is restored as the active phase.
- **Observations:** The stepwise phase evolution—ZIF-67 → metallic Co/graphite → Co₃O₄ → metallic Co—confirms successful synthesis and activation of the Fischer–Tropsch catalyst.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were assessed by N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K (BET analysis), providing insight into surface area, pore volume, and porosity type, which are crucial for reactant accessibility and catalyst dispersion.
- **Findings:** The Co@SiO₂ catalysts exhibit type-IV isotherms with H3 hysteresis, characteristic of mesoporous materials. Surface area and pore volume increase with pyrolysis temperature:  
  - Co@SiO₂-773: 260 m²/g, 0.46 cm³/g  
  - Co@SiO₂-873: 285 m²/g, 0.50 cm³/g  
  - Co@SiO₂-973: 305 m²/g, 0.51 cm³/g  
  The transition from microporous (type-I, in ZIF-67) to mesoporous structure is attributed to SiO₂ particle agglomeration during pyrolysis and calcination.
- **Observations:** Enhanced mesoporosity and high surface area facilitate cobalt dispersion and accessibility, supporting high catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM), complemented by elemental mapping (EDS/HAADF-STEM), were used to determine nanoparticle size, morphology, and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** After pyrolysis, cobalt nanoparticles are well-dispersed within the carbon matrix, with average sizes increasing with temperature: 5.4 nm (773 K), 11.0 nm (873 K), 13.3 nm (973 K). After calcination and reduction, Co@SiO₂-873 maintains a narrow particle size distribution (~10–12 nm) with minimal aggregation. Elemental mapping confirms homogeneous distribution of Co and Si, and the formation of a silica matrix encapsulating cobalt nanoparticles.
- **Observations:** Controlled synthesis yields uniform, accessible cobalt nanoparticles, with optimal size (~10 nm) in Co@SiO₂-873 correlating with superior catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility and Metal–Support Interaction
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction in H₂ (TPR(H₂)) was employed to assess cobalt reducibility and the strength of metal–support interactions, which influence the formation of active metallic Co sites.
- **Findings:** All Co@SiO₂ samples display two main reduction peaks at ~570 K and ~700 K, corresponding to the stepwise reduction of Co₃O₄ to CoO and then to metallic Co. A broad high-temperature band (850–1150 K) indicates the presence of highly dispersed cobalt species strongly interacting with SiO₂, such as cobalt phyllosilicate. The degree of reduction (DOR) increases with pyrolysis temperature: 66% (773 K), 78% (873 K), 79% (973 K).
- **Observations:** The high DOR and efficient reduction in Co@SiO₂-873 are linked to optimal particle size and minimal formation of irreducible cobalt silicates, supporting high catalytic activity and stability.

#### 6.1.5 Thermal Stability and Decomposition Behavior
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was used to monitor decomposition and weight loss during pyrolysis, informing the selection of synthesis conditions.
- **Findings:** Complete decomposition of ZIF-67@SiO₂ occurs between 800–850 K, with no further weight loss above 700 K, confirming full conversion to the desired catalyst structure.
- **Observations:** TGA results validate the chosen pyrolysis conditions and correlate with the observed changes in surface area and porosity.

#### 6.1.6 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Cobalt loading was quantified by atomic absorption spectroscopy, and elemental distribution was mapped by STEM/EDX.
- **Findings:** The catalysts possess high cobalt loading (~50 wt.%), with uniform spatial distribution of Co and Si throughout the silica matrix.
- **Observations:** High and homogeneous cobalt content is essential for achieving the observed high activity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (483 K, 20 bar, H₂/CO = 1, 40 ml min⁻¹ syngas), CO conversion after 102 h on stream was:
  - Co@SiO₂-773: 13.7%
  - Co@SiO₂-873: 15.8%
  - Co@SiO₂-973: 10.9%
  - Co@SiO₂-cal.: 12.1%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion exhibits a volcano-type dependence on pyrolysis temperature, peaking at 873 K. The Co@SiO₂-873 catalyst demonstrates the highest activity, attributed to optimal cobalt nanoparticle size (~10 nm) and high reducibility.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** Methane selectivity (\( S(\text{C}_1) \)) represents the percentage of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** After 102 h on stream:
  - Co@SiO₂-773: 6.5%
  - Co@SiO₂-873: 5.3%
  - Co@SiO₂-973: 5.8%
  - Co@SiO₂-cal.: 7.5%
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower methane selectivity is observed for catalysts with optimal particle size (Co@SiO₂-873 and -973), indicating suppression of the methanation side reaction.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity reflects the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\sum F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** After 102 h on stream:
  - Co@SiO₂-773: 87.2%
  - Co@SiO₂-873: 90.5%
  - Co@SiO₂-973: 89.5%
  - Co@SiO₂-cal.: 85.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest C₅⁺ selectivity is achieved with Co@SiO₂-873, correlating with its superior CO conversion and optimal cobalt dispersion.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The chain growth probability (\( \alpha \)) indicates the likelihood of hydrocarbon chain propagation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution:
\[
\alpha = \frac{\text{moles of (C}_{n+1}\text{)}}{\text{moles of (C}_n\text{)}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For Co@SiO₂-873, α = 0.94 (C₁₅–C₁₀₀ range, after 201 h TOS).
- **Observations and Trends:** High α values (up to 0.94) are associated with increased long-chain hydrocarbon formation and reduced methane selectivity, reflecting efficient chain growth on well-dispersed cobalt nanoparticles.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is defined as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time on Stream (h)}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Co@SiO₂-873 maintains high CO conversion with only a slight decrease over 201 h, indicating minimal deactivation. In contrast, Co@SiO₂-cal. shows rapid initial deactivation within 50 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior stability of Co@SiO₂-873 is attributed to its optimal particle size and high degree of reduction, which minimize aggregation and oxidation of cobalt nanoparticles.
