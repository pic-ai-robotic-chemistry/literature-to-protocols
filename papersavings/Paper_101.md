## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the SiO₂ supported cobalt catalyst is achieved through an incipient wetness impregnation technique. This method ensures reproducibility by uniformly distributing the cobalt precursor onto the support material, which in this case is Q‑15 SiO₂ gel.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation Procedure

1. • Precursor Mixing / Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
   • Begin by obtaining the commercially available Q‑15 SiO₂ gel (80 wt%) from Fuji Silysia Chemical, Japan.  
   • Prepare an aqueous solution of cobalt nitrate to achieve a final catalyst composition of 20 wt% cobalt.  
   • Impregnate the SiO₂ gel uniformly with the cobalt nitrate solution using the incipient wetness technique, ensuring that the volume of the solution matches the pore volume of the gel.

2. • Drying  
   • After impregnation, transfer the catalyst precursor to an air oven.  
   • Dry the sample at 120°C overnight. This step is critical to remove excess moisture while maintaining catalyst integrity, facilitating a uniform precursor formation without decomposing the cobalt nitrate.

3. • Calcination  
   • Following drying, the precursor is subjected to calcination in an air atmosphere.  
   • Calcine at 200°C for 2 hours. This treatment promotes the thermal decomposition of cobalt nitrate into well-dispersed cobalt oxides on the SiO₂ support, which is essential for the catalyst’s performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### Comparative Analysis of the Method

• Advantages:  
   • The incipient wetness impregnation method offers cost-effective and scalable synthesis, ensuring a homogeneous dispersion of cobalt onto the support.  
   • The simplicity of the process minimizes material waste and avoids complex control measures such as pH adjustments or additional precipitating agents.

• Limitations:  
   • Although effective, the method requires precise control of solution volume and careful monitoring of drying and calcination conditions to ensure reproducibility.  
   • Minimal equipment specifications are mentioned, relying on standard laboratory apparatus such as an air oven and calibrated temperature controllers.

This structured procedure, detailing reagent concentrations, equipment used, and exact operating conditions, provides a clear and reproducible workflow for synthesizing SiO₂ supported cobalt catalysts, meeting the rigorous standards required for Fischer–Tropsch applications.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was conducted in a fixed-bed reactor under rigorously controlled conditions to ensure reproducibility and to allow clear correlation of operating parameters with catalytic performance.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature was maintained at 240 °C. This parameter is critical as it governs the activation of the SiO₂ supported cobalt catalyst and directly influences CO conversion. The study reports that even though higher temperatures promote enhanced CO conversion, they may also contribute to catalyst deactivation over time due to intensified hydrogenation reactions. Observations indicate that under these conditions, a gradual decrease in CO conversion is sometimes noted as the reaction progresses.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
A total system pressure of 4.5 MPa was selected for the experiments. This pressure ensures an optimal concentration of syngas within the reactor, facilitating effective mass transfer and favoring the formation of heavier hydrocarbons (C₅⁺). The control of system pressure is critical in balancing the partial pressures of reactants, thereby stabilizing the reaction environment and improving product distribution.

#### 5.2.3 Syngas and Supercritical Solvent Partial Pressures
In addition to the total pressure, the partial pressure of the feed gases and the supercritical solvent were carefully regulated. The syngas (CO + H₂) was maintained at a partial pressure of 1.0 MPa, which is essential for sustaining high conversion rates and consistent catalytic activity. Moreover, n‑hexane, employed as a supercritical solvent, was maintained at a partial pressure of 3.5 MPa. The high partial pressure of n‑hexane enhances its ability to extract heavier products from the catalyst pores, reducing secondary reactions and improving the 1‑olefin yield. This solvent effect is particularly beneficial in suppressing catalyst fouling under prolonged operation.

#### 5.2.4 Catalyst Weight-to-Feed Ratio Effects
The influence of the catalyst weight-to-feed (W/F) ratio was also examined. The experiments demonstrate that a lower W/F ratio correlates with an increase in CO conversion and improved product selectivity. Conversely, higher W/F ratios tend to show moderate increases in CH₄ and CO₂ selectivity. This parameter, therefore, plays a crucial role in optimizing the performance of the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

The method also allows for the assessment of 1-olefin content, which is found to increase with higher supercritical solvent partial pressure and is notably higher in supercritical fixed bed operation compared to conventional gas-phase reactors.

- **Observations:** The effectiveness of the catalyst, as evidenced by high CO conversion and favorable hydrocarbon selectivity, supports the successful formation of active cobalt phases and uniform dispersion, as intended by the synthesis protocol.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under supercritical phase fixed bed conditions, CO conversion values for the SiO₂ supported cobalt catalyst range from 30.3% to 86.5%, depending on the weight of catalyst to feed ratio (W/F) and temperature. For instance, increasing W/F from 2.5 to 10 g cat. h/mol raises CO conversion from 30.3% to 86.5%. Higher reaction temperatures also enhance CO conversion, with a 20.8% increase observed when temperature is raised from 240°C to 250°C.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increases with both higher temperature and higher W/F. The partial pressure of n-hexane has minimal effect on CO conversion when total and syngas partial pressures are constant.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity is low, increasing from 1.6% to 4.1% as W/F rises from 7.5 to 10 g cat. h/mol. It also increases with temperature and W/F.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SiO₂ supported cobalt catalyst exhibits low CO₂ selectivity due to limited water-gas shift activity. Higher W/F and temperature promote CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity increases with temperature and W/F. For example, a 5.6% increase is observed as temperature rises from 240°C to 250°C. At the start of the reaction, CH₄ selectivity is about 17%, decreasing to 8% after 3 hours on stream.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher temperatures and longer residence times favor methane formation. CH₄ selectivity decreases over time due to catalyst deactivation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{Moles of C in C}_3^+ \text{ products}}{\text{Moles of CO converted}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** C₃⁺ selectivity and hydrocarbon distribution are similar when using n-pentane, n-hexane, or n-heptane as supercritical solvents. However, 1-olefin content is significantly lower with n-decane.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lighter supercritical solvents (C₅–C₇) favor higher 1-olefin content and broader hydrocarbon distribution.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, reflects the probability of chain growth:
  - α ranges from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (infinite chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** α decreases from 0.86 at 220°C to 0.82 at 250°C.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher temperatures reduce α, favoring lighter hydrocarbons and decreasing the probability of long-chain formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate (h}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO conversion drops from 71% at 0.5 h to 45% after 3 h, with a deactivation rate of 0.22 h⁻¹ at 240°C, 4.5 MPa, and 3.5 MPa n-hexane partial pressure.
- **Observations and Trends:** Deactivation is most pronounced in the initial hours, stabilizing thereafter.

#### 6.2.7 1-Olefin Content
- **Description:** 1-Olefin content measures the proportion of terminal olefins in the hydrocarbon products, reflecting selectivity for valuable intermediates.
- **Experimental Data:** 1-olefin content increases with the partial pressure of n-hexane and is higher in the supercritical fixed bed reactor than in the gas-phase fixed bed. It is significantly lower when n-decane is used as the solvent.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced extraction by supercritical fluids (especially n-hexane) improves 1-olefin selectivity, while heavier solvents like n-decane are less effective.
