## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis method reported for the Co‑La‑Zr/AC catalyst using a co‑impregnation approach. The method is designed for reproducibility and scalable catalyst preparation, ensuring uniform deposition of metal nitrates on activated carbon.

#### Co‑Impregnation Method:
1. • Catalyst Preparation:  
   - Activated carbon (derived from coconut shell; 20–40 mesh, BET area 893 m²/g, pore volume 0.57 cm³/g) is first dried for 0.5 hours at room temperature.  
   - Reagents used: Lanthanum nitrate, zirconium nitrate, and cobalt nitrate in an aqueous solution.  
   - Equipment: Rotary evaporator employed at room temperature for even impregnation.
  
2. • Precursor Mixing and Deposition:  
   - The aqueous solution is introduced to the dried activated carbon in the rotary evaporator.  
   - Although a separate precipitation–deposition step can be performed if required, in this procedure, the uniform distribution of the nitrate precursors on the support is achieved during the impregnation process.

3. • Drying:
   - After complete solvent evaporation, the impregnated sample is air dried at 363 K for 8.0 hours, ensuring thorough removal of moisture.

4. • Calcination:
   - The dried catalyst is calcined at 623 K for 3.0 hours under an argon atmosphere.  
   - This step decomposes the precursors, leading to the formation of metal oxides without risking oxidation from ambient air.

5. • Post‑synthesis Treatment (Reduction):
   - The calcined catalyst undergoes in situ reduction in a stainless steel fixed‑bed microreactor using a flow of hydrogen for 12.0 hours.  
   - This reduction converts the metal oxides to active metallic cobalt and facilitates the formation of cobalt carbide species, critical for CO hydrogenation.

### Comparative Analysis:
- Advantages of the co‑impregnation method include homogeneous precursor distribution, minimized equipment complexity, and enhanced scalability.  
- Compared with co‑precipitation methods, co‑impregnation using a rotary evaporator ensures a more uniform deposition, typically leading to improved catalyst dispersion and reproducibility in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

By employing these precise activation conditions—703 K, a hydrogen atmosphere in a fixed-bed microreactor, and a 12-hour duration—the catalyst exhibits enhanced activity and selectivity. ### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases present in the Co-La-Zr/AC catalyst were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a PANalytical X'Pert PRO diffractometer. XRD analysis is essential for determining the nature and distribution of active cobalt species, which directly influence Fischer–Tropsch synthesis performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed the coexistence of metallic cobalt (Co⁰) and cobalt carbide (Co₂C) as the principal cobalt-containing phases after in situ reduction. The presence of distinct diffraction peaks for both Co⁰ and Co₂C confirms their formation and stability under the applied reduction and reaction conditions.
- **Observations:** The optimal reduction temperature (703 K) led to the simultaneous presence of well-dispersed Co⁰ and Co₂C phases. This balance is critical, as Co⁰ sites facilitate CO dissociation and chain growth, while Co₂C sites promote CO insertion, enhancing higher alcohol selectivity. At higher reduction temperatures (e.g., 723 K), the amount of Co₂C decreased, correlating with reduced catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Reducibility and Support Interactions (TPR Analysis)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was conducted using a Micromeritics Autochem 2910 equipped with an Omnistar 300 quadrupole mass spectrometer. TPR profiles provide insight into the reduction behavior of cobalt oxides and the interaction between the active phase and the activated carbon support.
- **Findings:** Two main hydrogen consumption peaks were observed at 480 K and 595 K, corresponding to the sequential reduction of Co₃O₄ to CoO and CoO to Co⁰, respectively. A broad high-temperature peak above 600 K was attributed to the hydrogenation of oxygen-containing groups on the activated carbon, as evidenced by the evolution of CH₄, CO, and CO₂.
- **Observations:** The reduction profile indicates efficient transformation of cobalt oxides to the active metallic state, with the support’s surface chemistry also being modified during reduction. The removal of surface oxygen groups at higher temperatures aids in achieving smaller, more dispersed cobalt particles.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** The textural properties of the activated carbon support were characterized by BET surface area and pore volume measurements, which are crucial for metal dispersion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** The activated carbon exhibited a high BET surface area of 893 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.57 cm³/g.
- **Observations:** These properties facilitate excellent dispersion of cobalt, lanthanum, and zirconium species, contributing to the formation of small, well-dispersed cobalt particles. Enhanced dispersion is directly linked to improved catalytic activity and selectivity for higher alcohols.

#### 6.1.4 Particle Size and Dispersion

- **Findings:** Increasing the reduction temperature from 623 K to 703 K decreased the average cobalt particle size from 32.1 nm to 12.6 nm. At 723 K, the particle size slightly increased to 14.0 nm.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed into products during the reaction. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 3.0 MPa, 495 K, H₂/CO = 2, and GHSV = 500 h⁻¹, the Co-La-Zr/AC catalyst reduced at 703 K achieved a CO conversion of 59.1%. At lower reduction temperatures (623 K), conversion was 25.9%, while at 723 K, conversion decreased to 51.4%.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increases with reduction temperature up to 703 K, attributed to enhanced cobalt dispersion and optimal formation of active phases. Further increase to 723 K results in decreased conversion, likely due to less favorable cobalt phase composition or particle growth.

#### 6.2.2 Product Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity reflects the distribution of converted CO into specific product groups, reported as the percentage of converted CO forming each product.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - **CO₂ Selectivity:** Decreases with increasing reduction temperature, from 6.4% (623 K) to 4.1% (703 K).
  - **Light Hydrocarbon (HC₁–₄) Selectivity:** Decreases from 36.3% (623 K) to 21.6% (703 K).
  - **Heavy Hydrocarbon (HC₅⁺) Selectivity:** Increases from 24.1% (623 K) to 32.3% (703 K).
  - **Total Alcohol (ROH) Selectivity:** Rises from 33.2% (623 K) to 42.0% (703 K), then slightly decreases to 39.1% (723 K).
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal reduction temperature (703 K) yields the highest alcohol selectivity and a favorable shift toward heavier hydrocarbons and higher alcohols, while suppressing CO₂ and light hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.3 Alcohol Distribution and Chain Growth Factor (αROH)
- **Description:** The alcohol chain growth factor (αROH) indicates the probability of forming longer-chain alcohols, with values closer to 1 favoring higher alcohols. It is derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** At 703 K, αROH reaches 0.73, compared to 0.58 at 623 K. The proportion of C₆–C₁₈ alcohols in total alcohols is 50.4% at 703 K, indicating significant higher alcohol formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing reduction temperature enhances αROH and the yield of higher alcohols, peaking at 703 K. This is linked to the formation of cobalt carbide (Co₂C) species, which promote CO insertion and chain growth.

#### 6.2.4 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring performance over time at steady-state conditions.
- **Experimental Data:** Steady-state activity and selectivity were established after 48 hours on stream, with no significant deactivation observed during this period.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst maintains stable performance under the tested conditions, supporting its suitability for higher alcohol synthesis from syngas.
