## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis for the Co/Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalyst is executed through an impregnation method, ensuring systematic reproducibility and scalability. The procedure follows a defined sequence of steps, with each phase meticulously documented to guarantee replication by other researchers.

#### • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
1. Dissolve metal precursors (cobalt and iron salts) in water together with the dopant promoter (lanthanum) aiming for dopant levels of x = 0, 1, 5, and 10.  
2. Adjust the solution pH using acid or base with precise monitoring via a pH meter.  
3. Observe a uniform color change and the formation of a homogeneous solution, indicating complete dissolution.  

#### • Precipitation–Deposition  
1. If the protocol calls for the additional deposition step, add the support precursor (aligned with Al₂O₃) dropwise into the well-stirred metal solution.  
2. Stir the mixture for 1 hour to ensure even dispersion on the support before proceeding to drying.

#### • Drying  
1. Dry the impregnated support at 100–120°C for 2–4 hours using a rotary evaporator to facilitate controlled solvent evaporation.  
2. This step is critical for achieving uniform metal distribution as evidenced by consistent experimental observations.

#### • Calcination  
1. Calcine the dried sample at 500–600°C for 4–6 hours in an air atmosphere.  
2. This step promotes the formation of metal oxides uniformly over the support, a key factor in catalyst activation and stability.

#### • Post‑treatment (Reduction)  
1. Subject the calcined catalyst to reduction in a hydrogen environment at 300–400°C for 2–3 hours.  
2. This reduction transforms the metal oxides into their active metallic form, crucial for enhancing catalytic performance.

This structured protocol—with sequential transitions from precursor mixing, through drying and calcination, to reduction—underlines the meticulous control of reaction conditions, equipment usage (such as pH meters and rotary evaporators), and experimental observations. The precise numerical values and unit specifications further guarantee that the impregnation method is both reproducible and scalable, ultimately ensuring uniform catalyst dispersion and optimal performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Co/Fe-Based Fischer–Tropsch Catalyst (x = 0, 1, 5, 10 dopant levels)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  - 10.0 g Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O  
  - 10.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O  
  - Dopant precursor (e.g., La(NO₃)₃) in amounts corresponding to x = 0, 1, 5, and 10 (measured in mmol)  
  - 100 mL deionized water  
- **Conditions**:  
  - Dissolution at ambient temperature under stirring at 400 rpm  
  - pH adjusted to ~6.5 using dilute NaOH or HCl, verified with a calibrated pH meter  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve metal nitrates and dopant precursor in water until a homogeneous solution forms.  
  - Stir continuously for 45 minutes to ensure complete dissolution.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Accurate pH control and precise dosing of dopant reagent are crucial for reproducibility.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:  
  - Precursor solution from Step 1  
  - 5.0 g Al₂O₃ support, pre-washed with distilled water  
- **Conditions**:  
  - Impregnation carried out at room temperature with continuous stirring  
- **Process**:  
  - Slowly add Al₂O₃ into the precursor solution with vigorous stirring to ensure even distribution.  
  - Maintain mixing for 60 minutes to achieve uniform deposition of metal and dopant species onto the support.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Ensure systematic variation of dopant levels by adjusting reagent concentrations.

##### Step 3: Purification  
- **Process**:  
  - Remove excess liquid by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes.  
  - Rinse the precipitate with deionized water to eliminate unbound ions.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  - Dry the washed sample in an oven at 100°C for 12 hours.  
- **Calcination**:  
  - Heat with a ramp rate of 5°C/min to 600°C and hold for 4 hours in an air atmosphere.

##### Step 5: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  - Optionally, subject the calcined catalyst to reduction under a hydrogen atmosphere at 350°C for 5 hours with a controlled H₂ flow (e.g., 50 mL/min).

**Key Considerations**:  
- Maintain homogeneous mixing during precursor preparation and impregnation.  
- Monitor critical parameters such as temperature, pH, and stirring rates closely to ensure reproducibility across varying dopant levels.  
- Analytical techniques like XRD, BET, and H₂-TPR are recommended post-synthesis to correlate structural attributes with catalytic performance.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO conversion was measured using a fixed-bed flow reactor under controlled temperature, pressure, and gas hourly space velocity (GHSV). Gas chromatography was employed to analyze inlet and outlet CO concentrations. Reported values show that increasing the dopant ratio (x = 0, 1, 5, 10) leads to a progressive enhancement in CO conversion, with the highest conversion observed at intermediate to high dopant levels.
- **Observations and Trends:** The data indicate a positive correlation between dopant content and CO conversion up to an optimal loading. Beyond this point, further increases in dopant may not yield additional improvements. Enhanced metal dispersion and stabilization, as confirmed by characterization, underpin the improved conversion rates.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity was determined by quantifying CH₄ in the product stream via gas chromatography. Under identical reaction conditions, CH₄ selectivity decreases as the dopant ratio increases, with the lowest values observed at the highest dopant loadings.
- **Observations and Trends:** The reduction in methane selectivity with increased dopant content reflects improved control over hydrocarbon chain growth, favoring the formation of heavier products.

#### 6.2.3 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Product analysis reveals that C₃⁺ selectivity increases with higher dopant levels, with the most significant enhancement observed at x = 5 and 10.
- **Observations and Trends:** The shift toward heavier hydrocarbon production is attributed to improved catalyst dispersion and reduced methane formation, as supported by structural characterization.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model, indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination:
  - Values range from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (maximum chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** α values were extracted by fitting hydrocarbon product distributions. An increase in dopant ratio correlates with higher α values, signifying a greater tendency for long-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest α factors are associated with optimal dopant levels, aligning with increased C₃⁺ selectivity and reduced methane production.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate (h}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Time-on-stream measurements show that catalysts with higher dopant levels (x = 5, 10) exhibit significantly lower deactivation rates compared to undoped or low-dopant samples.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced stability at higher dopant loadings is attributed to improved metal dispersion and resistance to sintering, as confirmed by post-reaction characterization.
