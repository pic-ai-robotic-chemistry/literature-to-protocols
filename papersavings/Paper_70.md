## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section documents the reproducible synthesis of silica‐supported bimetallic Fe–Co catalysts via a combined coprecipitation and sol–gel method. The integrated approach facilitates controlled metal dispersion and particle size, presenting both the operational advantages and the associated synthesis complexity.

#### Coprecipitation and Sol–Gel Synthesis Procedure

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - • Reagents: Cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) and ferric nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O) are dissolved in deionized water according to the desired Fe/Co ratios (e.g., Cat‑2: 10Fe–20Co/SiO₂).  
   - • pH Control: Aqueous ammonia is added dropwise to adjust the solution to 8 ± 0.2, prompting the formation of Fe(OH)₃ and Co(OH)₂ precipitates.  
   - • Equipment: pH meter, mechanical stirrer, and standard glassware ensure accuracy in reagent addition.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - • TEOS Addition: Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) is introduced dropwise to the slurry to initiate the deposition of the silica support around the precipitated metal hydroxides.  
   - • Continued Stirring: Ensures uniform coating and proper integration of the sol–gel process prior to further processing.

3. **Drying**  
   - • Procedure: Following filtration and extensive washing with deionized water (to achieve a neutral pH), the wet precipitate is dried overnight at 110 ± 3 °C.  
   - • Observation: The material transitions from a viscous paste to a uniform dry powder.

4. **Calcination**  
   - • Conditions: The dried sample is calcined at 500 ± 2 °C for 5 hours.  
   - • Outcome: Thermal treatment stabilizes the metal oxides and locks in the desired structural properties without any additional post‑treatments.

### Comparative Analysis

The combined method offers improved control over metal distribution and particle size, which is essential for enhancing catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. However, the requirement for precise pH adjustments, controlled reagent addition, and strict temperature settings introduces challenges in scalability and workflow complexity. Despite these challenges, the method’s detailed procedural steps—including exact numerical values and conditions—ensure high reproducibility for research applications.

### 3.2 Detailed Synthesis Process

The following workflow outlines the detailed, reproducible steps for synthesizing silica‐supported bimetallic Fe–Co catalysts, using Cat-2 (10Fe–20Co/SiO₂) as an example. Similar procedures apply to other catalyst compositions (e.g., Cat-1, Cat-3, as well as monometallic references).

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 10Fe–20Co/SiO₂

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • 10.0 g Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O  
  • 15.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O  
  • Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) at a ratio appropriate for the silica support  
  • 100 mL deionized water; ethanol and aqueous solutions as required  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Maintain stirring at 70°C for 1 hour; use a calibrated hot plate with a magnetic stirrer  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve the cobalt and iron nitrate salts in deionized water at 70°C.  
  2. Add liquid ammonia dropwise while continuously stirring to adjust the pH to 8 ± 0.2, ensuring the precipitation of Co(OH)₂ and Fe(OH)₃.  
  3. Continue stirring and heat the solution to 80 ± 2°C before further adjusting the pH to 9 using additional ammonia.  
  4. Introduce a TEOS mixture prepared in ethanol and water slowly under vigorous stirring to integrate the silica support.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Accurate pH control during dropout of ammonia is critical in achieving uniform metal hydroxide formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly & Purification
- **Process**:  
  1. Allow the precipitate to mature for 30 minutes with consistent stirring.  
  2. Filter the slurry using standard lab filtration techniques; wash the precipitate repeatedly with deionized water until the wash solution reaches a neutral pH.  
  3. If available, gentle centrifugation can be employed to enhance separation and purity.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry the washed precipitate overnight at 110 ± 3°C in a convection oven.
- **Calcination**:  
  • Heat the dried material to 500 ± 2°C at a controlled ramp rate and maintain this temperature for 5 hours in an oxidizing (air) atmosphere.
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensure precise temperature control during both drying and calcination to facilitate complete conversion of precursors.

##### Step 4: Activation (if applicable)
- **Process**:  
  • Optionally, subject the calcined catalyst to a reduction step under H₂ flow at a designated temperature to generate the active metallic sites necessary for Fischer–Tropsch catalysis.

This procedure documents every step—from the synthesis of raw chemical precursors through careful pH adjustments, to final drying and calcination—to guarantee reproducibility and consistent catalyst performance.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)  
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to identify the crystalline phases (e.g., Co₃O₄ and Fe₂O₃) in the catalysts and to calculate crystallite sizes via the Scherrer equation.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Rigaku Miniflex 600, using CuKα radiation.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were degassed at 150 ± 1°C under vacuum for 2 hours and mounted in a close pack chamber flushed with an inert gas. The measurements were performed over a 2θ range of 20° to 80°.  
- **Test Parameters**: A step size of 0.02° per step and a counting time of 1.0 second were used.  
- **Key Notes**: Accurate sample alignment is critical to avoid errors in diffraction data.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis  
- **Operational Description**: This method was used to determine the specific surface area, total pore volume, and average pore diameter of the catalysts.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2010 apparatus.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Prior to gas adsorption, samples were degassed at 150 ± 1°C under vacuum for 2 hours to remove adsorbed moisture.  
- **Test Parameters**: Typical adsorption isotherm conditions were employed to ensure reproducibility.  
- **Key Notes**: Effective degassing is essential for accurate BET measurements.

### 4.3 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)  
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR assesses the reduction behavior of the catalyst, providing insight into metal–support and metal–metal interactions through overlapping reduction peaks.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics Chemisorb 2720 instrument equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: A gas mixture of 10.2 vol% H₂ in argon was used at a flow rate of 30 cm³/min, with the temperature ramped from a degassing condition at 150°C (under Ar for 1 hour) to 800°C at a rate of 10°C/min.  
- **Test Parameters**: Reduction profiles were precisely captured under these controlled ramping conditions.  
- **Key Notes**: Overlapping peaks indicate critical interactions affecting reduction behavior.

### 4.4 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED)  
- **Operational Description**: TEM provided direct imaging of the catalyst, revealing metal cluster sizes and confirming bimetallic phase formation. SAED patterns were recorded to validate crystallinity.  
- **Instruments and Models**: TEM operated at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Freshly prepared catalyst samples were analyzed; SAED indexing was used to correlate d-spacing values with XRD data.  
- **Test Parameters**: Specific magnification and exposure details were not provided.  
- **Key Notes**: Detailed sample preparation steps were minimal, yet imaging resolution was sufficient for phase confirmation.

### 4.5 Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)  
- **Operational Description**: EDS was used to verify the elemental composition of the catalysts.  
- **Instruments and Models**: ZEISS EVO Series Scanning Electron Microscope (EVO 50).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed under standard SEM conditions.  
- **Test Parameters**: Operating parameters were maintained as per typical EDS protocols.  
- **Key Notes**: EDS complements TEM analysis by providing elemental mapping.

### 4.6 X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)  
- **Operational Description**: XPS determined the surface composition and oxidation states of the catalyst components.  
- **Instruments and Models**: SPECS system equipped with an MgK X-ray source (1253.6 eV).

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a key preparatory step in the synthesis of the silica supported bimetallic Fe–Co catalysts. This step involves a controlled hydrogen reduction designed to convert the metal oxides into their active metallic phases, thereby exposing the active sites necessary for efficient Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
• **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for reducing the metal oxides (Fe₂O₃ and Co₃O₄) to their metallic states while mitigating the risk of sintering.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 500 °C in a fixed-bed reactor.  
• **Observations:** At this temperature, optimal metal reduction and dispersion are achieved, balancing complete conversion of oxides with the prevention of excessive particle agglomeration.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
• **Definition:** Maintaining an appropriate pressure during activation ensures effective hydrogen interaction with the catalyst surface, influencing the reduction kinetics.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is conducted under atmospheric pressure (1 bar).  
• **Observations:** Operating at atmospheric pressure provides a controlled environment, ensuring uniform exposure of hydrogen without the complexities that elevated pressures might introduce.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
• **Definition:** The composition of the gas used in activation dictates the reduction effectiveness and purity of the resulting metallic phases.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** A high-purity hydrogen stream (99.9%) is employed throughout the activation process.  
• **Observations:** The exclusive use of pure hydrogen facilitates a clean reduction process, effectively converting the metal oxides to active metal sites without any indications of unwanted carburization.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Flow Rate (Space-Time Considerations)
• **Definition:** Although a defined gas hourly space velocity is not provided for activation, controlled gas flow is vital for ensuring all catalyst particles are uniformly exposed to the reducing environment.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The hydrogen is introduced at a constant flow rate of 60 mL/min using precision mass flow controllers.  
• **Observations:** This controlled flow guarantees complete and homogeneous reduction of the catalyst.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
• **Definition:** The activation duration is essential for ensuring complete reduction while preventing overexposure that might lead to sintering.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under the aforementioned conditions for 6 hours.  
• **Observations:** The selected activation time achieves full reduction with uniform metal dispersion, setting the stage for optimal Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature significantly influences syngas activation and the interplay between the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis and the water gas shift (WGS) reaction, thereby affecting CO conversion and product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were performed at a base temperature of 220 °C, with further studies extending the range from 220 °C to 280 °C. Notably, optimum catalyst performance, particularly for Cat-2 (10Fe–20Co/SiO₂), was achieved at 240 °C.
- **Observations:** Increasing the temperature up to 240 °C enhances CO conversion and WGS activity. However, temperatures above this threshold tend to increase methane formation while reducing the C₅⁺ selectivity, highlighting the need for a balanced operating temperature.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure impacts the concentration of syngas in the reactor and influences both the rate of CO conversion and the distribution of hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Conditions:** All tests were conducted at an operating pressure of 20 bar in a fixed-bed reactor system.
- **Observations:** Operating at 20 bar facilitates improved interaction between the reactants and supports higher CO conversion rates, while favoring the formation of C₅⁺ hydrocarbons. Variations in pressure were also linked to performance trends similar to those observed in previous studies with iron and cobalt catalysts.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is crucial for modulating the balance between hydrogenation and chain-growth reactions during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The tests employed an exact H₂/CO feed ratio of 1.48, which was observed to adjust further during reaction conditions, with the optimum catalyst (Cat-2) showing a usage ratio around 2.0.
- **Observations:** This ratio is instrumental in achieving high CO conversion while controlling the selectivity towards C₅⁺ hydrocarbon production. Deviations in the ratio can lead to increased methane formation or limited hydrogen availability.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity (GHSV) defines the volumetric flow rate of syngas per unit mass of catalyst, directly impacting residence time, conversion efficiency, and catalyst stability.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Experimental runs were conducted at a GHSV of 1200 mL/(g_cat·h).
- **Observations:** A controlled GHSV allowed for a steady-state operation, with CO conversion stabilizing after approximately 12 hours on stream. This parameter was key to ensuring reproducible performance and minimizing catalyst deactivation.


## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the Fe–Co/SiO₂ catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED). XRD identifies crystalline phases and enables crystallite size estimation via the Scherrer equation.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of fresh calcined catalysts revealed the presence of Co₃O₄ and Fe₂O₃ phases. Upon reduction, metallic cobalt, metallic iron, and Fe–Co bimetallic phases were detected. The Scherrer equation, applied to the (311) peak for Co₃O₄ and (222) for Fe₂O₃, yielded crystallite sizes of approximately 12–14 nm for bimetallic catalysts, with monometallic oxides reaching up to 19 nm. Increasing Fe/Co ratio promoted bimetallic phase formation and reduced individual oxide crystal sizes. SAED patterns for Cat-2 (10Fe–20Co/SiO₂) confirmed the presence of Fe–Co bimetallic phases through characteristic (110) and (211) diffraction rings.
- **Observations:** The formation of bimetallic phases and reduced crystallite size are associated with enhanced metal dispersion and catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area, pore volume, and average pore size were measured by N₂ adsorption–desorption using the BET method, providing insight into catalyst textural properties relevant to reactant diffusion.
- **Findings:** Bimetallic catalysts exhibited high surface areas: Cat-1 (193.19 m²/g), Cat-2 (199.72 m²/g), and Cat-3 (199.34 m²/g). Corresponding pore volumes were 0.40, 0.70, and 0.37 cm³/g, with average pore sizes of 8.39, 14.16, and 12.17 nm, respectively. Monometallic references showed comparable or slightly higher surface areas. Increased metal loading led to partial pore blocking, reducing surface area relative to the bare support.
- **Observations:** Larger pore volumes and moderate surface areas in bimetallic catalysts facilitate improved reactant diffusion, supporting higher catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Surface Chemistry
- **Description:** Elemental composition was assessed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) for bulk analysis and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) for surface chemical states and metal distribution.
- **Findings:** EDS confirmed Fe and Co contents closely matched theoretical values. XPS spectra showed surface enrichment of cobalt, especially in Cat-1 (5Fe–25Co/SiO₂), where Fe was nearly undetectable at the surface. For Cat-2, a small Fe 2p₃/₂ peak at 713 eV indicated Fe³⁺ presence. Co 2p₃/₂ and 2p₁/₂ peaks at 775 eV and 795.7 eV, respectively, with a 20 eV separation, reflected strong metal–support interactions in bimetallic systems.
- **Observations:** The surface enrichment of cobalt and altered binding energies in bimetallic catalysts suggest significant electronic interaction between Fe, Co, and the silica support.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction Behavior (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) profiles elucidate the reducibility of metal oxides and metal–metal interactions.
- **Findings:** Overlapping reduction peaks were observed for Fe and Co oxides in bimetallic catalysts. Increasing Fe content raised the reduction temperature of cobalt oxide, indicating inhibition by iron, while cobalt facilitated iron reduction, lowering its reduction temperature compared to monometallic iron catalysts. The maximum reduction temperature for bimetallic profiles was 534 °C.
- **Observations:** These interactions enhance metal dispersion and favor the formation of active bimetallic phases, correlating with improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) provided direct visualization of metal cluster size and distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images of bimetallic catalysts (e.g., Cat-2) showed metal clusters ranging from 5 to 15 nm, with Fe and Co phases indistinguishable, indicating strong interaction and bimetallic phase formation. The observed sizes were consistent with XRD-derived crystallite sizes. Some unevenness in cluster size was noted, but overall distribution was relatively uniform.
- **Observations:** The small, well-dispersed metal clusters in bimetallic catalysts are linked to higher metal dispersion and superior catalytic activity compared to monometallic references.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (220–240°C, 20 bar, H₂/CO = 1.48, GHSV = 1200 mL/g_cat·h), the bimetallic catalyst Cat-2 (10Fe–20Co/SiO₂) achieved a maximum CO conversion of 72% at 240°C. At 220°C, Cat-2 reached 65% conversion. Other bimetallic catalysts showed lower conversions: Cat-1 (5Fe–25Co/SiO₂) at 47% and Cat-3 (15Fe–15Co/SiO₂) at 55.2%. Monometallic references (Cat-4: 30Co/SiO₂ and Cat-5: 30Fe/SiO₂) exhibited inferior performance.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increased with Fe content up to an Fe/Co ratio of 0.5, peaking for Cat-2. Further Fe addition reduced conversion, indicating an optimal bimetallic synergy at this ratio.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Cat-2, CO₂ selectivity was 7.1% at 220°C and increased to 20% at 240°C. Other bimetallic catalysts exhibited similar or slightly higher values, while monometallic Fe catalysts showed even greater CO₂ selectivity due to enhanced water-gas shift (WGS) activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate CO₂ selectivity in Cat-2 indicates effective WGS activity, which increases hydrogen availability for hydrocarbon synthesis without excessive CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Cat-2 exhibited methane selectivity of 6.4% at 220°C and 8% at 240°C. Cat-1 and Cat-3 showed slightly higher values, while monometallic catalysts produced more methane, especially at elevated temperatures.
- **Observations and Trends:** Bimetallic catalysts, particularly Cat-2, suppressed methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Cat-2 achieved a C₅⁺ selectivity of 71% at 220°C, with values up to 72% at 240°C. Cat-1 and Cat-3 showed C₅⁺ selectivities of 68% and 61%, respectively. Monometallic catalysts had significantly lower C₅⁺ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal Fe/Co ratio (0.5) in Cat-2 maximized C₅⁺ selectivity, correlating with enhanced WGS activity and improved hydrogen supply.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth in hydrocarbon synthesis, inferred from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory distribution. Higher α values correspond to greater selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the high C₅⁺ selectivity (71–72%) for Cat-2 implies a high α factor, especially at the optimal Fe/Co ratio.
- **Observations and Trends:** The trend of increasing C₅⁺ selectivity with Fe addition up to the optimal ratio suggests that α is maximized for Cat-2, reflecting efficient chain growth.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 60 hours on stream, all catalysts, including Cat-2, maintained steady CO conversion with negligible deactivation. Minor initial decreases in conversion (noted for Cat-4 and Cat-5) were attributed to pore filling rather than true deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The bimetallic catalysts demonstrated excellent stability, with Cat-2 sustaining high performance throughout the test period.
