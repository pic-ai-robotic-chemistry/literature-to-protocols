## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the reproducible catalyst synthesis procedures as reported in the article. The synthesis techniques are compared with respect to their complexity, scalability, cost, and specific equipment requirements.

#### Temperature Programmed Synthesis (Mo₂C)
1. • Precursor Mixing: 10 g of MoO₃ (99.5% purity) is used as the sole precursor.
2. • Reduction and Carburization: The precursor is subjected to a controlled heating process in a 10% CH₄–H₂ gas mixture. The temperature is ramped from room temperature to 973 K at 60 K/h, then held at 973 K for 6 h. This step ensures complete carburization as supported by XRD analyses.
3. • Post‑Processing – Purging and Passivation: After carburization, the sample is purged with N₂ at 3 l/h for 30 min to clear residual reactive gases. This is followed by passivation at room temperature in a 2% O₂–He mixture for 2 h (flow rate of 2 l/h) to protect the catalyst surface from deep oxidation.
4. • Reproducibility: The entire procedure is repeated twice to achieve consistency in surface states and nominal composition.

#### Wet Impregnation Synthesis (Ru/Mo₂C)
1. • Precursor Preparation: An aqueous solution of ruthenium chloride dihydrate (RuCl₃·xH₂O) is prepared to achieve a 1 wt.% Ru loading.
2. • Impregnation and Solvent Evaporation: The Mo₂C support is soaked in the precursor solution, followed by slow solvent evaporation to ensure uniform distribution.
3. • Drying: The impregnated material is dried at 393 K overnight. XPS confirms the Ru species (Ru³⁺) and chlorine presence.

#### Wet Impregnation Synthesis (Co/Mo₂C)
1. • Precursor Preparation: Cobaltous nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) is used in excess to guarantee 1 wt.% Co loading.
2. • Impregnation & Drying: The support is immersed in the Co solution, subjected to slow evaporation, and then dried at 393 K overnight.
3. • Activation: A subsequent H₂ flow treatment at 773 K ensures proper reduction and dispersion of the Co species, as corroborated by XPS and BET results.

Each method is executed with precise control over temperatures, durations, and gas flow rates to guarantee replicability, as further elaborated by the associated QA responses.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Mo₂C  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g MoO₃ (99.5% purity).  
- **Conditions**: The reduction and carburization are conducted in a temperature programmed reactor using a 10% CH₄–H₂ gas mixture. The temperature is ramped from room temperature to 973 K at 60 K/h, followed by a 6-hour hold at 973 K.  
- **Process**:  
  - Load 10.0 g of MoO₃ into the reactor.  
  - Initiate the gas flow (10% CH₄–H₂) and gradually increase the temperature to 973 K at the defined ramp rate.  
  - Maintain the reactor at 973 K for 6 hours to ensure complete carburization and formation of Mo₂C with an atomic C/Mo ratio maintained between 0.3 and 0.5.  
- **Key Steps**: Careful temperature control and prolonged high-temperature hold are crucial for achieving the desired carburization.  

##### Step 2: Purification and Post-Processing  
- **Process**:  
  - After carburization, purge with N₂ at a flow rate of 3 l/h for 30 minutes to remove residual reactive gases.  
  - Passivate the catalyst at room temperature by exposing it to a 2% O₂–He mixture for 2 hours to prevent deep oxidation.  
  - The complete procedure is repeated twice to ensure reproducibility of the Mo₂C powders.  

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Ru/Mo₂C  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation and Impregnation  
- **Reagents**: Pre-prepared Mo₂C and an aqueous solution of ruthenium chloride dihydrate (RuCl₃·xH₂O) to achieve 1 wt.% Ru.  
- **Conditions**: Wet impregnation at room temperature with slow solvent evaporation, followed by drying at 393 K overnight.  
- **Process**:  
  - Immerse the Mo₂C in the RuCl₃ solution ensuring complete wetting.  
  - Allow the solvent to slowly evaporate, then dry the material at 393 K.  
- **Key Steps**: Emphasize homogeneous distribution of Ru on the Mo₂C support.  

#### Catalyst Preparation [3]: Co/Mo₂C  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation and Impregnation  
- **Reagents**: Pre-prepared Mo₂C and cobaltous nitrate hexahydrate for a nominal loading of 1 wt.% Co.  
- **Conditions**: Similar wet impregnation with slow evaporation and drying at 393 K; subsequent activation is performed at 773 K under a H₂ atmosphere.  
- **Process**:  
  - Impregnate Mo₂C with the cobalt solution, ensuring uniform precursor distribution.  
  - Dry at 393 K overnight, then activate at 773 K in pure hydrogen to reduce Co species.  
- **Key Steps**: Activation is critical for ensuring the reduction of cobalt oxide to metallic cobalt, thus enhancing catalytic performance.  

This detailed, stepwise workflow—from raw precursor handling to final activation—ensures that each catalyst synthesis (Mo₂C, Ru/Mo₂C, Co/Mo₂C) is clearly reproducible while addressing the critical parameters and conditions noted in the QA pairs.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation procedure for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is performed as part of the precursor reduction and carburization sequence. Two distinct activation approaches are applied depending on the catalyst composition.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
For Mo₂C (and Ru/Mo₂C), the activation is carried out via a temperature‐programmed method. The MoO₃ precursor is heated from room temperature to 973 K at a rate of 60 K/h to allow controlled reduction and carburization in a 10% CH₄–H₂ atmosphere. After reaching 973 K, the temperature is maintained for 6 hours to ensure complete phase transformation and proper development of the active Mo₂C structure. In contrast, for Co/Mo₂C, a tailored activation is employed where the catalyst is exposed to pure H₂ and heated up to 773 K. This lower maximum temperature is chosen to effectively reduce the cobalt species and secure the metallic state essential for enhanced catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure and Gas Composition
All activation experiments are conducted under atmospheric pressure during the reduction/carburization phase. The gas environment plays a key role:  
• For Mo₂C and Ru/Mo₂C, a 10% CH₄ in H₂ mixture is used to provide both the reducing environment and the carbon necessary for carburization.  
• For Co/Mo₂C, the activation atmosphere consists of pure hydrogen, delivered at a flow of 3.6 L/h. This pure H₂ atmosphere is critical for complete reduction of oxidized cobalt species without interference from other reactive gases.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Post-Treatment and Time
After the carburization step for Mo₂C-based catalysts, a post-treatment is performed: the catalyst is purged with N₂ at 3 L/h for 30 minutes. This is followed by a passivation step at room temperature using a 2% O₂–He mixture at 2 L/h for 2 hours. These steps are designed to stabilize the catalyst surface against deep oxidation while preserving the active carbide phase. The complete synthesis and activation process, including these sequential treatments, is repeated twice to ensure reproducibility and consistent catalyst performance during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter that influences syngas activation and determines catalyst performance during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. In this study, the catalytic tests were conducted over a temperature range of 473–533 K. Lower temperatures (around 473 K) tend to favor catalyst stability, as observed for Ru/Mo₂C, while increasing the temperature to 533 K enhances CO hydrogenation activity. This increase shifts product distribution towards a higher formation of light alkanes and results in reduced alcohol selectivity.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure plays an essential role in optimizing both conversion and product selectivity. The experimental conditions involved operating the stainless steel fixed-bed flow reactor at pressures of 20 bar and 50 bar. Notably, increasing the pressure from 20 to 50 bar significantly enhanced CO conversion, with the apparent activation energy decreasing from 170 to 120 kJ mol⁻¹. Furthermore, at 513 K, this pressure increase resulted in a one-third decrease in CO₂ selectivity (from 31.4% down to 21.2%), indicating a pressure-dependent shift favorable for the target products, although it posed challenges such as rapid catalyst deactivation, especially with Ru/Mo₂C at higher pressures.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio is fundamental in directing the reaction pathway during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. Throughout the experiments, a fixed H₂/CO ratio of 2 was maintained, ensuring that the feed composition is consistent across experiments. This ratio is critical for achieving the desired balance between hydrocarbon formation and the generation of side products. Variations in this ratio could affect selectivity; however, the controlled ratio here supports reproducibility and comparative performance analysis across different catalysts.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The volumetric space velocity (VSV) defines the ratio of the syngas flow rate to the catalyst volume, directly impacting the reactant residence time. In the reported tests, the VSV ranged from 3150 to 7400 h⁻¹. Higher space velocities, corresponding to a shorter exposure time of the reactants to the catalyst, tend to decrease the overall CO conversion but can contribute to improved operational stability over time, further reinforcing the need for precise control of all reaction parameters.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystalline structure and phase purity of the catalysts were assessed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a SIEMENS D5000 diffractometer employing Cu Kα radiation. XRD is essential for confirming the formation and stability of the Mo₂C phase, both before and after catalytic testing.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of Mo₂C, Ru/Mo₂C, and Co/Mo₂C all display the characteristic reflections of hexagonal close-packed molybdenum carbide, indicating successful carburization. The Mo₂C phase is preserved after catalytic testing and after metal impregnation, with no significant formation of new phases. In the first Mo₂C preparation, a minor metallic Mo phase was detected, corresponding to a C/Mo ratio of 0.3, while the second preparation achieved the expected stoichiometry (C/Mo = 0.5).
- **Observations:** The structural integrity of Mo₂C is maintained under reaction and activation conditions, demonstrating the robustness of the synthesis method and the catalyst’s resistance to phase transformation during Fischer-Tropsch operation.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area measurements were performed using the BET method on a QUANTASORB J.R. apparatus. BET analysis provides insights into the accessible surface and porosity, which are critical for catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** Fresh Mo₂C exhibits a BET surface area of 6.7–9.2 m²/g. After catalytic testing, the surface area decreases markedly to 2–3 m²/g, attributed to carbon deposition. Ru/Mo₂C shows a surface area of 7.3 m²/g, while Co/Mo₂C records a lower value of 3 m²/g.
- **Observations:** The reduction in surface area post-reaction is consistent with the accumulation of surface carbon, which can block active sites. The lower surface area of Co/Mo₂C suggests reduced porosity, potentially impacting reactant accessibility and catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Surface Chemistry
- **Description:** Elemental analysis and atomic absorption spectroscopy determined bulk composition, while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) provided surface chemical states and elemental ratios. XPS is particularly informative for identifying oxidation states and surface enrichment phenomena.
- **Findings:** Elemental analysis of fresh Mo₂C yields a C/Mo atomic ratio of 0.3–0.5, increasing to 1.9 after reaction, indicating significant carbon deposition. XPS of Mo₂C shows a Mo 3d₅/₂ peak at 228.2 eV (carbidic Mo) and a C 1s envelope with carbidic, free, and oxidized carbon. After use, the C 1s spectrum is dominated by free carbon, and the (C/Mo) ratio by XPS rises to 33, confirming surface carbon accumulation. For Ru/Mo₂C, XPS reveals a Ru 3d₅/₂ peak at 281.9 eV (Ru³⁺) before reduction, shifting to 280 eV (Ru⁰) after H₂ treatment. Co/Mo₂C displays Co 2p₃/₂ and 2p₁/₂ peaks at 781.0 and 796.6 eV, with shake-up satellites indicating Co²⁺; after reduction, a Co 2p₃/₂ peak at 778.2 eV confirms metallic Co formation.
- **Observations:** The XPS and elemental analysis data collectively demonstrate that surface carbon deposition is a major consequence of FT operation, affecting both surface area and surface chemistry. The oxidation states of Ru and Co are modulated by reduction treatments, with metallic states accessible under H₂ activation.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Passivation and Stability
- **Description:** The effect of passivation and activation on surface properties was monitored by XPS. Passivation is used to prevent deep oxidation of the carbidic phase upon exposure to air.
- **Findings:** Passivation with 2% O₂–He at room temperature forms a thin oxide layer, which is removed by subsequent H₂ activation, restoring the carbidic surface as confirmed by XPS.
- **Observations:** The passivation protocol effectively protects the catalyst during handling without irreversibly altering the active surface, ensuring reproducibility and stability of the Mo₂C phase for catalytic applications.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Mo₂C:** At 513 K and 20 bar, CO conversion is 8.9%. At 473 K and 20 bar, conversion is low (exact value not specified), but significantly lower than promoted catalysts.
  - **Ru/Mo₂C:** At 473 K and 20 bar, conversion is higher than Mo₂C but lower than Co/Mo₂C (exact value not specified).
  - **Co/Mo₂C:** At 473 K and 20 bar, CO conversion is 5.3%; at 473 K and 50 bar, it increases to 6.1%.  
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  CO conversion increases with both temperature and pressure for all catalysts. The activity order is Mo₂C < Ru/Mo₂C < Co/Mo₂C. Co promotion yields about five times higher conversion than Mo₂C at 473 K, 20 bar.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Mo₂C:** 10–30% (e.g., 21.2% at 513 K, 50 bar).
  - **Ru/Mo₂C:** ~30% at 20 bar, decreasing to ~20% at 50 bar.
  - **Co/Mo₂C:** 25.5% at 473 K, 20 bar; 13.2% at 473 K, 50 bar.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  CO₂ selectivity is similar (~30%) for all catalysts at 20 bar but decreases by about one third at 50 bar. Pressure, rather than promoter, is the main factor reducing CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Co/Mo₂C:** At 513 K, 20 bar: 50.4%; at 493 K, 20 bar: 34.4%; at 473 K, 20 bar: 30.6%.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  CH₄ selectivity increases with temperature. Co promotion enhances methane formation compared to Mo₂C and Ru/Mo₂C.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Mo₂C:** At 473 K, 20 bar: C₅⁺ selectivity is 10%.
  - **Co/Mo₂C:** Higher C₃⁺ selectivity than Mo₂C and Ru/Mo₂C, with increased heavy hydrocarbon formation.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Co promotion shifts product distribution toward heavier hydrocarbons, deviating from the Schulz–Flory model at high pressure.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Schulz–Flory model:
  - Calculated from the slope of ln(S/n) vs. n plots.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Mo₂C:** α = 0.39 at 513 K, 50 bar.
  - **Co/Mo₂C:** α ranges from 0.35 (513 K, 20 bar) to 0.43 (493 K, 20 bar).
  - **Ru/Mo₂C:** α decreases from 0.41 to 0.37 as temperature increases from 473 to 513 K.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Co increases heavy hydrocarbon selectivity and causes deviations from the Schulz–Flory model. Ru slightly decreases α, favoring lighter products.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. Deactivation rate is:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \ (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Co/Mo₂C:** Deactivation observed at 50 bar, with conversion decreasing over time due to pore saturation by liquid products.
  - **All Catalysts:** BET surface area drops post-reaction (e.g., Mo₂C: 6.7–9.2 to 2.9 m²/g), correlating with carbon deposition.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Surface carbon accumulation leads to moderate deactivation, especially at high pressure. Despite this, the Mo₂C phase remains structurally stable, as confirmed by XRD.

#### 6.2.7 Alcohol Selectivity
- **Description:** Alcohol selectivity is the fraction of converted CO forming alcohols.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Mo₂C:** 19% at 473 K, 20 bar.
  - **Ru/Mo₂C:** 2% at 473 K, 20 bar.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Ru promotion significantly suppresses alcohol formation compared to bare Mo₂C, with product distribution shifting toward alkanes.
