## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines the methods for synthesizing the Na‐FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5 catalyst system with a focus on reproducibility and clarity.

#### Na‑FeC*x* Synthesis via Co‑precipitation and Syngas Treatment

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Reagents: 30.0 g FeCl₃⋅6H₂O and 11.9 g FeCl₂⋅4H₂O dissolved in 200 mL deionized water with 5.0 mL of 12.0 mol L⁻¹ HCl.  
   • pH Adjustment: NaOH aqueous solution (2.0 mol L⁻¹) is added dropwise under vigorous stirring until the target pH of 10 is reached.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   • This step is executed immediately after pH adjustment to promote uniform gel formation before further processing.

3. Drying  
   • The solid phase is washed with deionized water and then dried at 100 °C overnight.

4. Calcination and Post‑treatment  
   • After drying, the sample undergoes calcination followed by a reduction/passivation sequence as required.

5. Syngas Treatment  
   • The calcined Na‑Fe₃O₄ is converted to the carbide phase by treatment with a syngas mixture (CO/H₂, 1:1) at 300 °C for 24 h, with observable changes from reddish-brown oxide to darker carbide indicative of phase transformation.

#### Zeolite Nanosheets Synthesis via Urea‑Assisted Hydrothermal Method

1. Precursor Mixing  
   • Key components include tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS), tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH) as the structure-directing agent, aluminum isopropoxide, and urea; diethoxydimethylsilane (DEMS) is added for introducing silanol groups when producing s‑S1‑OH variants.

2. Hydrothermal Crystallization  
   • The precursor mixture is transferred to a Teflon‑lined autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 48 h, ensuring precise crystallization of the zeolite framework.

3. Post‑processing  
   • The product is filtered, rinsed, air‑dried, and finally calcined at 550 °C for 4 h to remove the organic templates and stabilize the crystalline structure.

These step-by-step procedures, including exact reagent amounts, pH control, temperature, and duration, allow for reproducibility and ensure that the catalyst exhibits the desired active phase (Fe₅C₂) and optimal promotion by the zeolite nanosheets in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Na‐FeC*x* Component  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 30.0 g FeCl₃⋅6H₂O, 11.9 g FeCl₂⋅4H₂O, 200 mL deionized water, 5.0 mL aqueous HCl; 2.0 M NaOH solution for pH adjustment.  
- **Conditions**: Stir vigorously at room temperature; reaction mixture pH adjusted to 10 using dropwise addition of NaOH.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve FeCl₃⋅6H₂O and FeCl₂⋅4H₂O in deionized water containing HCl.  
  - Add NaOH dropwise under vigorous stirring until the pH reaches 10, ensuring homogeneous precipitation of iron hydroxide intermediates.  
  - Continue stirring for an additional 1 hour to complete the formation of the Na‐modified precursor.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor pH continuously and ensure thorough mixing to achieve uniform precipitation.

##### Step 2: Purification and Drying  
- **Process**:  
  - Separate the precipitate by centrifugation and wash it repeatedly (six times) with deionized water to remove residual ions.  
  - Dry the washed solid in an oven at 100 °C overnight before further processing.

##### Step 3: Calcination and Carburization  
- **Reagents/Conditions**: Calcination in air if required; subsequent treatment with syngas (CO/H₂ = 1:1) at 300 °C for 24 hours with a flow rate of 50 mL/min to convert the oxide to carbide.  
- **Process**:  
  - Preheat the calcined precursor and subject it to syngas treatment, ensuring complete conversion to Na‐FeC*x*, where formation of Fe₅C₂ is confirmed via XRD.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: s‐ZSM‐5 Zeolite Nanosheets  
##### Step 1: Precursor Synthesis  
- **Reagents**: 4.0 g TPAOH, 5.1 g deionized water, 2.8 g TEOS, 0.07 g aluminium isopropoxide, 0.65 g urea; optionally, DEMS for s‐S1‐OH variants.  
- **Conditions**: Stir at room temperature (initial stirring of TPAOH for 0.5 h followed by 12 h after adding TEOS, Al precursor, and urea).  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve TPAOH in water, add TEOS followed by aluminium isopropoxide and urea to form a homogeneous gel.  
  - Transfer the gel to an autoclave and crystallize at 180 °C for 2 days.

##### Step 2: Calcination  
- **Reagents/Conditions**: Calcination at 550 °C for 4 h in air to remove organics.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete removal of the template to achieve the desired framework integrity.

#### Assembly and Final Integration  
- **Process**:  
  - Combine the Na‐FeC*x* and s‐ZSM‐5 components in the requisite ratios (via granule mixing) prior to catalyst activation, ensuring that the zeolite promotes rapid olefin desorption and modulates the catalytic product distribution as confirmed in subsequent characterization studies.

Critical pause points include verifying pH during precipitation, thorough washing of precipitates, and monitoring phase changes via XRD after carburization, ensuring reproducibility and optimal catalytic performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The Na‐FeC*x*/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst activation is performed as a dedicated pre‐reaction treatment designed to convert the sodium‐modified ferric oxide precursor into the active carbide phase. This activation is carried out under carefully controlled conditions to ensure both efficient reduction and appropriate carburization.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical because it simultaneously drives the reduction of the iron oxide precursor and provides sufficient thermal energy for carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The syngas treatment is conducted at 300 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 300 °C facilitates the controlled incorporation of carbon into the iron lattice, favoring the formation of the active Fe₅C₂ phase without excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas directly influences the reduction and carburization processes. Hydrogen serves as the reducing agent while carbon monoxide provides the necessary carbon for carbide formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with a precise 1:1 molar ratio of CO/H₂ is employed.
- **Observations:** The balanced CO/H₂ mixture ensures that the reduction and carburization occur concurrently in the optimal stoichiometric proportions, which is crucial for enhancing the catalyst’s eventual performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step must be sufficient to enable complete transformation of the precursor while avoiding extended exposure that could lead to undesirable sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The treatment is maintained continuously for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** This extended activation period guarantees thorough conversion to the active carbide phase, ensuring high reproducibility in catalytic activity.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Gas Flow
- **Definition:** The flow rate of the activation gas controls the contact time between the gas and the catalyst, impacting the kinetics of the reduction and carburization processes.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A flow rate of 50 mL/min is applied during the syngas treatment.
- **Observations:** The specified flow rate provides sufficient gas exchange, ensuring uniform exposure of the catalyst particles to the activation atmosphere. Although space‐time velocity is not explicitly reported, the fixed flow rate contributes to the overall consistency of the activation process.

This integrated activation protocol uniquely combines reduction and carburization steps to form the active Na‐FeC*x* phase, which is essential for achieving high activity and selectivity in subsequent low‐temperature Fischer–Tropsch to olefins reactions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition of the Na‐FeC*x*/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst was determined by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku D/MAX 2550 diffractometer with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). This analysis is essential for identifying active phases and monitoring phase transformations during synthesis and reaction.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the as-synthesized Na‐FeC*x* catalyst displayed peaks corresponding to both ferric oxides (Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄) and iron carbide (Fe₅C₂). After syngas treatment (CO/H₂ = 1:1, 300 °C, 24 h) and subsequent LT-FTO reaction, the XRD signals of ferric oxides became nearly undetectable, with Fe₅C₂ emerging as the dominant phase (matching PDF #20-0509). The s‐ZSM‐5 zeolite component exhibited characteristic MFI framework reflections, confirming its crystallinity and structural integrity.
- **Observations:** The complete transformation to Fe₅C₂ under reaction conditions indicates effective carburization, which is critical for Fischer–Tropsch activity. The MFI structure of s‐ZSM‐5 is preserved throughout synthesis and reaction.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Dopant Distribution
- **Description:** Elemental composition and dopant distribution were assessed by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). These techniques provide quantitative and spatial information on metal and promoter content.
- **Findings:** ICP analysis confirmed a sodium loading of 5.35 wt% in Na‐FeC*x*, with Si/Al ratios in s‐ZSM‐5 of approximately 26.2 (error ±3%). EDS elemental mapping revealed homogeneous distribution of Fe and Na within the catalyst particles. XPS Na 1s spectra demonstrated sodium migration from Na‐FeC*x* to the zeolite component, particularly in powder-mixed samples, correlating with changes in catalytic performance.
- **Observations:** The washing step effectively tunes sodium content without altering the bulk crystal structure. Uniform dopant distribution is achieved, and sodium migration is sensitive to catalyst mixing methods.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ sorption isotherms were measured using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020M system to determine BET surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution. These parameters are crucial for understanding reactant diffusion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** s‐ZSM‐5 zeolite nanosheets exhibited a BET surface area of 337.3 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.21 cm³/g. These values reflect the thin, nanosheet morphology and high porosity of the zeolite component.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and pore volume of s‐ZSM‐5 facilitate rapid olefin diffusion and desorption, contributing to enhanced catalytic selectivity and activity.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphological features and particle size were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F, 200 kV) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU-8010/SU-1510). These analyses reveal the physical structure and dispersion of catalyst components.
- **Findings:** TEM images of Na‐FeC*x* showed granular particles characteristic of ferric carbide, while SEM images of s‐ZSM‐5 revealed sheet-like crystals with a b-axis thickness of 90–110 nm. EDS mapping confirmed homogeneous elemental distribution.
- **Observations:** The distinct nanosheet morphology of s‐ZSM‐5 and compact Na‐FeC*x* particles are consistently observed, supporting efficient catalyst mixing and performance.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and Olefin Desorption
- **Description:** Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFT) and FTIR were employed to monitor surface species and olefin desorption dynamics. These techniques provide insight into the interaction of products with catalyst surfaces.
- **Findings:** DRIFT spectra after ethene adsorption displayed bands for =CH₂ stretching vibrations (2,966, 2,986, 3,011, 3,124 cm⁻¹; 3,075 cm⁻¹ for asymmetric stretching). Upon Ar purging, these signals decreased more rapidly for Na‐FeC*x*/s‐ZSM‐5 than for other catalyst variants, indicating faster olefin desorption.
- **Observations:** Accelerated olefin desorption from the s‐ZSM‐5 surface is directly linked to improved catalytic performance, as it promotes continuous CO hydrogenation and suppresses deep hydrogenation of olefins.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under LT-FTO conditions (260 °C, 2.0 MPa, GHSV 2,400 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹, CO/H₂/Ar = 45/45/10), the bare Na‐FeCₓ catalyst exhibited a low CO conversion of 1.9%. In contrast, the Na‐FeCₓ/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 82.5%. During long-term stability testing, the Na‐FeCₓ/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst maintained a steady CO conversion of 68.1% after activation, with only a slight decrease to 67.6% after 600 hours on stream.
- **Observations and Trends:** The dramatic increase in CO conversion upon addition of s‐ZSM‐5 is attributed to the zeolite’s ability to accelerate olefin desorption, thereby freeing active sites for continued CO hydrogenation. The catalyst demonstrates exceptional stability, with negligible deactivation over extended operation.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Na‐FeCₓ/s‐ZSM‐5 (granule-mixed), methane selectivity remained low at 3.0–3.8%. In contrast, powder-mixed samples showed much higher methane selectivity (up to 18.8%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Low methane selectivity is maintained with granule mixing, while powder mixing increases methane formation due to sodium migration and enhanced hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.3 C₂–C₁₀ Olefin Selectivity and Olefin/Alkane Ratio
- **Description:** Selectivity for C₂–C₁₀ olefins is the fraction of converted CO yielding olefins with 2–10 carbon atoms. The olefin/alkane (o/a) ratio is the molar ratio of olefins to alkanes in this range.
- **Experimental Data:** Na‐FeCₓ/s‐ZSM‐5 achieved C₂–C₁₀ olefin selectivity of 67.8–72.0% and an o/a ratio of 3.5–5.2. These values remained stable over 600 hours.
- **Observations and Trends:** The zeolite promoter narrows the product distribution to C₁–C₁₀, with high selectivity for valuable light olefins and a high o/a ratio, indicating suppressed deep hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of hydrocarbon chain propagation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution. A moderate α favors olefin production.
- **Experimental Data:** The product distribution for Na‐FeCₓ/s‐ZSM‐5 is dominated by C₁–C₁₀ hydrocarbons, indicating a moderate α value (not numerically specified).
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of s‐ZSM‐5 reduces α compared to bare Na‐FeCₓ, favoring shorter-chain olefins and a narrow product slate.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over time. Deactivation rate is inferred from the change in CO conversion per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** Over 600 hours, CO conversion decreased minimally from 68.1% to 67.6%, and C₂–C₁₀ olefin selectivity remained above 67.8%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibits outstanding durability, with negligible deactivation and consistent product selectivity throughout prolonged operation.

#### 6.2.6 Olefin Productivity
- **Description:** Olefin productivity measures the amount of olefins produced per gram of catalyst per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** Na‐FeCₓ/s‐ZSM‐5 achieved an olefin productivity of 15.3 mmol·g₍FeCₓ₎⁻¹·h⁻¹ at 260 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** This high productivity underscores the catalyst’s efficiency in low-temperature Fischer–Tropsch to olefins.

**Summary:**  
The Na‐FeCₓ/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst demonstrates a remarkable combination of high CO conversion, low methane selectivity, high C₂–C₁₀ olefin selectivity, and excellent long-term stability. The use of MFI zeolite nanosheets as a physical promoter is critical for achieving these performance metrics, primarily by accelerating olefin desorption and suppressing undesired hydrogenation pathways.
