## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis follows a two‑step procedure designed for reproducibility and precise control over the formation of the active c‑Fe5C2 phase and subsequent K doping.

#### Melt‑Infiltration Synthesis (Formation of c‑Fe5C2)
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Reagents: 1.85 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and 1.0 g activated charcoal  
  – Process: Physically grind the reagents in a mortar until a homogeneous black mixture is obtained.  
  – Aging: Place the mixture in a polypropylene bottle and age at 323 K for 24 h to facilitate melt‑infiltration of the iron salt into the charcoal pores.
2. • Thermal Treatment (Drying & Calcination)  
  – Equipment: Alumina boat inside a tube‑type furnace  
  – Heating Conditions: Ramp at 2.7 °C/min until 623 K  
  – Gas: Under steady CO flow at 200 mL/min  
  – Duration: 4 h at 623 K  
  – Observation: Transformation of the precursor to active c‑Fe5C2 nanoparticles is indicated by a persistent homogeneous black appearance.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation (K‑Doping via Precipitation–Deposition)
1. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Reagent: Aqueous K₂CO₃ solution prepared at concentrations targeting K/Fe atomic ratios of 0.025, 0.05, 0.075, and 0.10  
  – Process: Introduce the K₂CO₃ solution dropwise to the dried c‑Fe5C2/charcoal powder under continuous stirring, ensuring uniform deposition.
2. • Post‑Impregnation Thermal Treatment (Calcination)  
  – Conditions: CO calcination at 623 K for 4 h  
  – Purpose: Enhance incorporation and stabilization of potassium within the catalyst structure.
3. • Cooling and Washing  
  – Cooling: Under a steady nitrogen (N₂) flow to maintain an inert atmosphere  
  – Washing: Rinse the catalyst in anhydrous ethanol to remove impurities and prevent rapid oxidation.
4. • Drying  
  – Final Step: Dry in a vacuum oven at 323 K to yield the final K‑doped c‑Fe5C2/charcoal nanocatalyst.

This structured synthesis, including precise numerical values for temperatures, durations, and gas flow rates, ensures high reproducibility and facilitates direct replication by researchers following the experimental protocol as described.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: K-Doped c-Fe5C₂/Charcoal Nanocatalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 1.85 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 1.0 g activated charcoal.
- **Conditions**: Ambient room temperature for grinding; aged at 323 K for 24 hours in an oven.
- **Process**:
  - Grind the Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O with activated charcoal in a mortar until a homogeneous black powder is achieved.
  - Transfer the mixture into a closed polypropylene bottle and age at 323 K for 24 hours to allow complete infiltration of the iron salt into the charcoal pores.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough mixing to achieve a uniform black precursor.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly – Melt Infiltration and Thermal Treatment
- **Reagents**: Pre-aged primary precursor.
- **Conditions**: Temperature ramp rate of 2.7 °C/min up to 623 K under a continuous CO flow at 200 mL/min for 4 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Place the aged mixture into a tube furnace.
  - Heat using the specified ramp rate until reaching 623 K while maintaining a steady CO flow, thereby converting the iron precursor into active c-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles.
- **Key Steps**: Control the heating rate and CO atmosphere closely to promote uniform nanoparticle formation.

##### Step 3: Potassium Incorporation by Incipient Wetness Impregnation
- **Reagents**: Aqueous K₂CO₃ solutions prepared to achieve K/Fe ratios of 0.025, 0.05, 0.075, and 0.1.
- **Conditions**: Dropwise addition to the thermally treated powder.
- **Process**:
  - Impregnate the preformed Fe₅C₂/charcoal powder with the appropriately concentrated K₂CO₃ solution, ensuring full contact via stirring.
- **Key Steps**: Precisely adjust the solution concentration and volume to maintain the targeted dopant levels.

##### Step 4: Post-Impregnation CO Calcination, Purification & Drying
- **Reagents**: K-doped catalyst powder.
- **Conditions**: Calcination at 623 K under CO flow for 4 hours; subsequent immersion in anhydrous ethanol under nitrogen.
- **Process**:
  - Perform CO calcination to finalize the carbide phase.
  - Cool the catalyst while immersed in anhydrous ethanol under N₂ flow to prevent oxidation.
  - Recover and dry the catalyst in a vacuum oven set at 323 K.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain inert conditions during cooling and drying to preserve nanoparticle stability and composition.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical step to ensure the formation of the active monoclinic Hägg carbide (c‑Fe₅C₂) phase in the K-doped iron carbide nanocatalyst. This activation is performed as part of the catalyst synthesis and involves precise thermal treatments under a controlled CO atmosphere to reduce iron nitrates into the active carbide phase without inducing excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is central to driving the reduction and carburization reactions. It ensures that sufficient energy is provided to convert iron precursors into the active phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is heated to 623 K during both the initial thermal treatment and the subsequent CO calcination step following potassium impregnation.
- **Observations:** Operating at 623 K guarantees effective transformation to the c‑Fe₅C₂ phase while maintaining the catalyst structure.

#### 5.1.2 Heating Ramp Rate
- **Definition:** A controlled heating ramp rate minimizes thermal gradients and avoids local overheating that could lead to sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is ramped at 2.7 °C/min until the target temperature of 623 K is achieved.
- **Observations:** This gradual increase in temperature contributes to uniform activation across the catalyst bed, promoting consistent metal dispersion.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** Using a reducing gas is imperative to convert iron oxides into the active carbide phase and prevent oxidation during temperature ramp-up.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Carbon monoxide (CO) is used as the activation gas at a steady flow rate of 200 mL/min during both the initial thermal treatment and the calcination step after potassium incorporation.
- **Observations:** The CO atmosphere effectively facilitates the reduction process and is instrumental in stabilizing the formation of the c‑Fe₅C₂ phase.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration under activation conditions must be sufficient to complete the reduction while avoiding prolonged exposure that could lead to sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained at 623 K for 4 hours under continuous CO flow.
- **Observations:** This controlled activation period has been optimized to ensure complete and reproducible formation of the active phase, enabling high catalytic performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis tests.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis as it governs the activation of synthesis gas and the kinetics of hydrocarbon chain growth, influencing overall catalyst performance.

Collectively, these well-defined parameters ensure that the experimental conditions for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis are both replicable and optimized for high catalytic performance with a strong selectivity towards desired gasoline-range hydrocarbons.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and crystallite size of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are essential for identifying active phases relevant to Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirmed the presence of two Hägg-carbide phases, with all samples exhibiting the monoclinic Fe₅C₂ phase. The crystallite sizes, estimated from the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the (020) diffraction peaks, were consistent across potassium loadings: 20.0 nm (K-free), 20.8 nm (K/Fe = 0.025), 20.8 nm (K/Fe = 0.050), 19.9 nm (K/Fe = 0.075), and 21.0 nm (K/Fe = 0.100). Mössbauer spectroscopy further confirmed that the K-free Fe₅C₂/charcoal sample consisted almost exclusively of the Hägg-Fe₅C₂ phase.
- **Observations:** The consistent crystallite size and phase purity across K loadings indicate structural stability, which is critical for reliable catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were employed to assess nanoparticle morphology, dispersion, and crystallinity.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed well-dispersed Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles embedded in the charcoal support, with average diameters of 17.8 ± 2.7 nm (K/Fe = 0.025), 18.2 ± 2.8 nm (K/Fe = 0.050), 18.0 ± 2.6 nm (K/Fe = 0.075), and 20.4 ± 3.9 nm (K/Fe = 0.100). HRTEM showed clear lattice fringes of 0.205 nm, corresponding to the Fe₅C₂ (510) plane, confirming single-crystal nature.
- **Observations:** The narrow particle size distribution and absence of agglomeration, even after high-temperature treatment, reflect effective synthesis and stability.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen sorption (BET and BJH methods) was used to measure surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which influence catalyst accessibility and dispersion.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area decreased from 1010 m²/g (initial charcoal) to 525 m²/g (K/Fe = 0.075) after Fe and K loading. Total pore volume reduced from 0.85 cm³/g to 0.40 cm³/g, while the average pore size remained constant at 3.8 nm.
- **Observations:** The reduction in surface area and pore volume indicates successful incorporation of Fe and K, while the stable pore size supports uniform dispersion and accessibility of active sites.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Surface Chemistry
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and HAADF-STEM with elemental mapping were used to analyze surface composition, oxidation states, and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** XPS revealed that both K-free and K-doped Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles had partially oxidized surfaces, but K-doped samples exhibited greater resistance to oxidation due to the sacrificial oxidation of potassium. Elemental mapping confirmed uniform distribution of Fe, K, and C, with potassium predominantly located in Fe-rich regions.
- **Observations:** The improved surface stability in K-doped samples is attributed to potassium’s protective effect, and the homogeneous elemental distribution validates the effectiveness of the synthesis protocol.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed into products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is typically defined as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 320 °C, 15 bar, H₂/CO = 1, and GHSV = 8.0 NL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹, the K-doped c-Fe₅C₂/charcoal catalyst at K/Fe = 0.075 achieved CO conversion exceeding 95% over 90 hours on stream. At K/Fe = 0.1, CO conversion decreased, accompanied by catalyst deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increased with K doping up to K/Fe = 0.075, after which further increases in K content led to reduced conversion and stability.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The K-doped catalyst (K/Fe = 0.075) exhibited a low methane selectivity of approximately 5%, while the K-free catalyst produced about 12% methane under identical conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium doping significantly suppressed methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key metric for gasoline-range product formation.
- **Experimental Data:** The K-doped catalyst at K/Fe = 0.075 achieved C₅⁺ selectivity of approximately 50%. Higher K loading (K/Fe = 0.1) resulted in decreased C₅⁺ selectivity, while the K-free catalyst showed lower values.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal K doping enhanced C₅⁺ selectivity, correlating with increased chain growth probability.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher tendency for long-chain products.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the high C₅⁺ selectivity (~50%) at K/Fe = 0.075 implies a relatively high α factor. Excessive K (K/Fe = 0.1) reduced C₅⁺ selectivity, suggesting a lower α.
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor increased with K doping up to the optimal ratio, then declined with further K addition.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and iron-time-yield (FTY) over time. Deactivation is inferred from declining FTY or CO conversion.
- **Experimental Data:** The K-doped catalyst (K/Fe = 0.075) maintained >95% CO conversion and an FTY of 1.54 × 10⁴ mol CO·g_Fe⁻¹·s⁻¹ over 90 hours. At K/Fe = 0.1, FTY decreased to 1.26 × 10⁴ mol CO·g_Fe⁻¹·s⁻¹, with increased CO₂ selectivity and reduced stability.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal K doping conferred excellent stability, while excessive K led to faster deactivation, likely due to increased CO₂ formation and hindered CO dissociation.

#### 6.2.6 Iron-Time-Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY measures the number of CO moles converted to hydrocarbons per gram of iron per second:
\[
\text{FTY} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted}}{\text{g Fe} \cdot \text{s}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The highest FTY (1.54 × 10⁴ mol CO·g_Fe⁻¹·s⁻¹) was observed at K/Fe = 0.075. Lower or higher K/Fe ratios resulted in reduced FTY.
- **Observations and Trends:** FTY trends mirrored those of CO conversion and selectivity, peaking at the optimal K/Fe ratio.

---
**Summary:** The K-doped c-Fe₅C₂/charcoal catalyst at K/Fe = 0.075 demonstrated superior CO conversion, high C₅⁺ selectivity, low methane formation, and excellent stability, establishing it as the optimal composition for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis under the studied conditions.
