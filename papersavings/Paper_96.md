## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe₃O₄@MnO₂ core–shell catalysts is performed via a one‑pot hydrothermal process, with an alternative Fe₃O₄–0.3Mn prepared by a conventional co‑precipitation method. The following details outline the step‑by‑step procedures and compare both methodologies in terms of reproducibility, synthesis complexity, and scalability.

#### One‑Pot Hydrothermal Method
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Reagents: 2.9 g NaOH dissolved in 200 mL deionized (DI) water (maintained at 70 °C), 10 g FeSO₄·7H₂O, and 6.0 g poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP) in water.  
   • Observation: Formation of a dark‑green suspension, indicating the generation of iron hydroxide species.  

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • During stirring, a KMnO₄ solution is added dropwise with amounts of 0.57 g, 1.1 g, 1.7 g, or 2.3 g to achieve MnO₂ loadings of 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4, respectively.  
   • Observation: A dark‑brown precipitate forms upon addition, confirming the deposition of MnO₂ on the Fe₃O₄ core.

3. **Aging and Separation**  
   • The mixture is aged at 70 °C for 2 hours under continuous stirring.  
   • Post‑aging, centrifugation is employed to separate the catalyst from the liquid phase, followed by sequential washing with DI water and absolute ethanol.

4. **Drying and Calcination**  
   • The washed precipitate is dried at 110 °C for 12 hours.  
   • Subsequent heat‑treatment (calcination) is conducted at 400 °C for 4 hours in a tube furnace under high‑purity N₂, ensuring the integrity of the active phases.

#### Co‑precipitation Method (Fe₃O₄–0.3Mn)
• A homogeneous solution containing predetermined amounts of FeSO₄·7H₂O and Mn(NO₃)₂ is prepared to achieve the desired Fe/Mn ratio before precipitation in NaOH solution.  
• This traditional approach involves separate control over metal precursor ratios and tends to be more complex, although it permits fine‑tuning of catalyst composition.

### Comparative Analysis
• The hydrothermal process offers streamlined synthesis with controlled dropwise KMnO₄ addition and effective aging, providing uniform MnO₂ shell deposition and enhanced reproducibility.  
• In contrast, the co‑precipitation method, while established, requires additional procedural steps to ensure homogeneous nucleation and can be less scalable due to increased process complexity.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₃O₄@MnO₂ Core–Shell Catalysts

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  - FeSO₄·7H₂O: 10.0 g dissolved in 100 mL deionized water  
  - NaOH: 2.9 g dissolved in 200 mL deionized water preheated to 70 °C  
  - Poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP): 6.0 g mixed into the FeSO₄ solution  
- **Conditions**:  
  - Temperature: 70 °C during dissolution  
  - Stirring: Magnetic stirring until homogeneous dissolution  
- **Process**:  
  - Prepare two separate solutions: one for NaOH and another for FeSO₄·7H₂O with PVP.  
  - Combine the NaOH solution with the FeSO₄/PVP solution under vigorous stirring to form a dark‐green suspension.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Ensure complete dissolution and homogeneity before proceeding to catalyst assembly.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly (Hydrothermal Process)
- **Reagents**:  
  - KMnO₄: Added dropwise in amounts adjusted by desired MnO₂ loading (0.57 g, 1.1 g, 1.7 g, or 2.3 g for Fe₃O₄@0.1MnO₂, Fe₃O₄@0.2MnO₂, Fe₃O₄@0.3MnO₂, and Fe₃O₄@0.4MnO₂ respectively)
- **Conditions**:  
  - Maintain stirring during dropwise addition at 70 °C  
- **Process**:  
  - Slowly add the KMnO₄ solution to the dark‐green suspension, allowing controlled nucleation and growth of the MnO₂ shell around the Fe₃O₄ core.  
  - After complete addition, age the mixture for 2 hours at 70 °C for thorough reaction and shell formation.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Dropwise addition is critical to control the deposition thickness and avoid aggregation.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:  
  - Centrifuge the reaction mixture (specific speed and duration not stated) to separate the precipitate.  
  - Wash the collected solid several times using deionized water followed by absolute ethanol to remove residual ions and impurities.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying Conditions**:  
  - Dry at 110 °C for 12 hours in a controlled atmosphere.
- **Calcination Conditions**:  
  - Heat-treat in a tube furnace at 400 °C for 4 hours under high-purity N₂ atmosphere (99.9995%), ensuring a controlled ramp rate for uniform calcination.

##### Step 5: Activation
- **Process**:  
  - Reduce the catalyst in a 50% H₂/50% N₂ atmosphere at 350 °C for 10 hours with a flow rate of 5000 mL/(h·gCat) to generate the active phase.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for removing residual organic species and stabilizing the catalyst’s crystalline phases. It ensures that the Fe₃O₄ core is preserved while any unwanted precursors are driven off.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 400 °C in a tube furnace.
- **Observations:** At this temperature, the thermal energy is sufficient to clean the catalyst surface and promote proper crystallization of both the Fe₃O₄ core and the MnO₂ shell, thus exposing active sites for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure can affect gas–solid interactions and reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** No specific pressure is reported for the heat-treatment process.
- **Observations:** The absence of a controlled pressure parameter suggests that the activation was performed under ambient atmospheric conditions.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas environment during activation prevents oxidation and unwanted reactions while promoting the desired reduction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** High-purity nitrogen (99.9995% purity; 5.5 grade) is used to create an inert atmosphere in the tube furnace.
- **Observations:** The use of high-purity N₂ effectively prevents oxidation of the Fe₃O₄ core, maintaining the catalyst in its reduced form and preserving the integrity of the core–shell structure.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity relates to the flow rate of the activation gas relative to the catalyst mass and influences gas–solid contact.
- **Experimental Conditions:** No quantitative details regarding space-time velocity are provided.
- **Observations:** Despite the lack of an explicit value, the controlled flow of high-purity N₂ ensures uniform treatment across catalyst particles.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process is fundamental to achieving full thermal transformation without catalyst sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst undergoes a 4-hour heat-treatment.
- **Observations:** This duration is chosen to guarantee complete phase development and uniform stabilization of the Fe₃O₄@MnO₂ core–shell architecture, thereby priming the catalyst for optimal Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is the operating condition that drives the activation of syngas and the reduction and carburization of the catalyst, which are essential for high catalytic performance and selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer-Tropsch reaction is performed at a temperature of 340 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature facilitates efficient conversion of syngas into light olefins while ensuring that the iron oxide species are properly reduced and carburized. The maintained temperature contributes significantly to catalyst stability over prolonged operation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the partial pressures of reactants, thereby affecting syngas activation, product distribution, and overall CO conversion.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction is conducted at a pressure of 1.5 MPa in the fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure enhances syngas activation and promotes the formation of longer carbon chains. This condition is instrumental in achieving a high CO conversion rate and impacts the selectivity towards light olefins.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is critical in controlling the types of hydrocarbons produced by balancing hydrogen availability with carbon monoxide, thereby influencing product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 2 is employed during the catalytic testing.
- **Observations:** This ratio is optimized to suppress excessive methane formation while promoting the formation of light olefins (C₂–C₄). It ensures a balanced hydrogen supply that favors the desired product distribution.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity and Duration
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) indicates the volume of reactant gas passing per unit catalyst mass per hour, determining the contact time between the gas and the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 11,000 mL/(h·gCat) is maintained, with the catalytic evaluation performed over 48 hours.
- **Observations:** The selected space velocity ensures sufficient reactant exposure while minimizing side reactions. The 48-hour reaction period confirms sustained catalyst performance, stability, and high CO conversion throughout the testing duration.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** Crystal phases and structural evolution were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Rigaku D/MAX2550 VB/PC instrument with Cu Kα radiation. Phase identification before and after reaction elucidates the transformation of the active phase under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.
- **Findings:** Prior to reaction, the main phase present in all catalysts was Fe₃O₄, with a characteristic peak at 2θ = 35.42°. After 48 hours of syngas reaction, XRD patterns revealed the emergence and intensification of χ-Fe₅C₂ (No. 89-8968) peaks, while the Fe₃O₄ peak weakened and eventually disappeared as Mn content increased. For MnO₂ content ≥0.2, a new peak at 2θ = 29.66°, attributed to θ-Fe₃C (No. 85-0871), appeared and intensified with further Mn addition.
- **Observations:** The transformation from Fe₃O₄ to iron carbides (χ-Fe₅C₂ and θ-Fe₃C) is promoted by MnO₂, indicating enhanced carburization and formation of catalytically active phases.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were determined by argon physisorption at −186 °C using a Micrometrics ASAP 2020, with BET and BJH analyses providing surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution.
- **Findings:** Increasing MnO₂ content from 0 to 0.4 led to a rise in BET surface area from 20.4 to 57.6 m²·g⁻¹ and an increase in total pore volume from 0.13 to 0.34 cm³·g⁻¹. The average pore size decreased with higher Mn content.
- **Observations:** Enhanced MnO₂ loading improves Fe₃O₄ dispersity and mesoporosity, yielding smaller grain sizes and more accessible surface area, which are beneficial for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** Reducibility and surface adsorption behaviors were assessed by H₂-TPR, H₂-TPD, and CO-TPD using a Micrometrics AutoChem II 2920.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR profiles showed that MnO₂-coated Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles were more easily reduced than bulk Fe₃O₄, with reduction peaks shifting to lower temperatures as Mn content increased. H₂-TPD revealed increased weakly bound hydrogen (Hα, Hβ) and decreased strongly bound hydrogen (Hγ) with Mn addition, indicating suppressed hydrogenation. CO-TPD demonstrated that MnO₂ introduction enabled dissociative CO adsorption, with the amount increasing alongside Mn content.
- **Observations:** MnO₂ promotes electron donation to Fe₃O₄, enhancing reducibility and CO dissociation while suppressing excessive hydrogenation, which correlates with higher olefin selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Core–Shell Structure
- **Description:** Morphology and elemental distribution were examined by SEM, HRTEM, aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM, and EDS mapping/lining.
- **Findings:** SEM images showed spherical Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, with nanoplates forming upon Mn addition. HRTEM and STEM-EDS lining confirmed Fe-rich cores and Mn-enriched shells, with shell thickness increasing with Mn content (e.g., 3.1 nm for Fe₃O₄@0.3MnO₂). HAADF-STEM and EDS mapping consistently demonstrated a uniform core–shell architecture and surface Mn enrichment.
- **Observations:** The one-pot hydrothermal method reliably produces well-dispersed core–shell structures, with shell thickness tunable by MnO₂ content.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition and Electronic Structure
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze surface elemental composition and electronic interactions.
- **Findings:** Fe 2p peaks shifted to lower binding energies and Mn 2p peaks to higher energies with increasing MnO₂, indicating electron transfer from MnO₂ to Fe₃O₄. In reduced samples, a weak Fe⁰ peak at 706.6 eV increased with Mn content.
- **Observations:** These shifts confirm strong electronic interaction at the core–shell interface, facilitating Fe reduction and carburization.

#### 6.1.6 Iron Carbide Quantification
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified iron carbide phases post-reaction.
- **Findings:** The proportion of χ-Fe₅C₂ increased with MnO₂ content (up to 93.4% at MnO₂ = 0.2), and θ-Fe₃C appeared at MnO₂ ≥ 0.3. Fe₃O₄ was undetectable at higher MnO₂ loadings.
- **Observations:** MnO₂ addition accelerates the formation of active iron carbide phases, crucial for Fischer–Tropsch activity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} (\%) = \frac{N_{\text{in,CO}} - N_{\text{out,CO}}}{N_{\text{in,CO}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \(N_{\text{in,CO}}\) and \(N_{\text{out,CO}}\) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under HTFT conditions (340 °C, 1.5 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 11,000 mL/(h·g_cat)), the CO conversion values for the catalysts are:
  - Fe₃O₄: 32.5%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.1MnO₂: 59.6%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.2MnO₂: 97.6%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.3MnO₂: 91.8%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.4MnO₂: 85.7%
  - Fe₃O₄–0.3Mn (coprecipitation): 89.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increases sharply with initial MnO₂ addition, peaking at 0.2 MnO₂. Further increases in MnO₂ content slightly reduce conversion, likely due to diffusion limitations from excessive shell thickness. The core–shell catalysts outperform the coprecipitated analogue.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} (\%) = \frac{N_{\text{out,CO}_2}}{N_{\text{in,CO}} - N_{\text{out,CO}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Reported values are:
  - Fe₃O₄: 35.8%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.1MnO₂: 36.1%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.2MnO₂: 34.2%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.3MnO₂: 37.9%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.4MnO₂: 35.8%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity remains relatively stable (34–38%) across all catalysts, indicating that both Fe₃O₄ and iron carbide phases contribute to CO₂ formation regardless of MnO₂ shell thickness.

#### 6.2.3 Light Olefin (C₂=–C₄=) Selectivity
- **Description:** Light olefin selectivity is the molar percentage of C₂=–C₄= olefins among total hydrocarbon products, determined by GC analysis.
- **Experimental Data:** Selectivity values after 48 h are:
  - Fe₃O₄: 15.4%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.1MnO₂: 19.2%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.2MnO₂: 25.8%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.3MnO₂: 37.4%
  - Fe₃O₄@0.4MnO₂: 29.6%
  - Fe₃O₄–0.3Mn: 19.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** Light olefin selectivity increases with Mn addition, peaking at 0.3 MnO₂. Excessive MnO₂ (0.4) reduces selectivity, likely due to hindered CO diffusion and increased hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, represents the probability of chain growth:
  - Higher α values (closer to 1) indicate greater selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** α increases from 0.39 (Fe₃O₄) to 0.74 (Fe₃O₄@0.3MnO₂) as Mn content rises.
- **Observations and Trends:** The core–shell structure and optimal MnO₂ content enhance α, attributed to improved Fe dispersion and electron transfer from MnO₂, which favor chain propagation over termination.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion per hour:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\%\,\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe₃O₄@0.3MnO₂, the deactivation rate is 0.02% h⁻¹ over 48 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** This low deactivation rate demonstrates excellent catalyst stability, with carbon balances exceeding 96% throughout the test.

#### 6.2.6 Olefin/Paraffin (O/P) Ratio
- **Description:** The O/P ratio quantifies the suppression of hydrogenation, calculated as the molar ratio of olefins to paraffins in the product stream.
- **Experimental Data:** The O/P ratio increases from 0.45 (Fe₃O₄) to 7.56 (Fe₃O₄@0.3MnO₂).
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate Mn addition strongly suppresses hydrogenation, favoring olefin production.

---
**Summary:** The Fe₃O₄@MnO₂ core–shell catalysts, especially at 0.3 MnO₂ loading, exhibit superior CO conversion, high light olefin selectivity, enhanced chain growth probability, and remarkable stability under HTFT conditions. These improvements are directly linked to the core–shell architecture and optimized MnO₂ promotion.
