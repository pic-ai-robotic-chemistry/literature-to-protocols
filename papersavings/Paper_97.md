## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Co-Re/γ-Al₂O₃ catalyst was synthesized using an incipient co‑impregnation technique, which facilitates uniform distribution of cobalt and rhenium precursors on the mesoporous γ‑Al₂O₃ support. This method is advantageous compared to traditional co‑precipitation or simple impregnation due to improved metal dispersion and controlled catalyst formulation.

#### Incipient Co‑impregnation Approach

1. • Precursor Mixing  
   • Reactants: Cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate and ammonium perrhenate are combined with commercial mesoporous γ‑Al₂O₃, which has a surface area of approximately 148 m²/g and an average pore size near 24.44 nm.  
   • Metal loadings: 20 g/g for cobalt and 0.5 g/g for rhenium, ensuring effective catalytic performance.  
   • Equipment: Controlled mixing using a magnetic stirrer ensures homogeneous impregnation over the support.  

2. • Drying  
   • Conditions: The wet impregnated sample is dried at 120°C overnight.  
   • Purpose: Removes residual solvent and fixes the metal precursors onto the support, ensuring reproducibility.  
   • Equipment: A drying oven with precise temperature control.

3. • Shaping and Calcination  
   • Shaping: The dried material is pressed into tablets to promote uniform handling during thermal treatment.  
   • Calcination: Pressed tablets are heated at 500°C for 6 h in a furnace.  
   • Impact: Calcination converts the metal precursors into active species and enhances metal dispersion by reducing crystallite size, as evidenced by improved cobalt particle distribution.

4. • Post‑treatment  
   • Crushing: The calcined tablets are crushed into particles sized between 0.18–0.25 mm for reactor evaluation, ensuring optimal contact with the reactants during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### Comparative Analysis

The incipient co‑impregnation method not only offers simplicity and scalability but also provides enhanced control over metal dispersion compared to co‑precipitation. While the latter may involve additional pH adjustment complexities, the incipient approach bypasses intermediate precipitation–deposition steps, delivering both economic and practical benefits in catalyst synthesis.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural features of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases and assessing dispersion.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the Co-Re/γ-Al₂O₃ catalyst display characteristic peaks for the γ-Al₂O₃ support and additional reflections attributed to the Co₃O₄ phase. The average crystallite size of cobalt oxide, calculated using the Scherrer equation, decreases from 17.2 nm in Co/Al₂O₃ to 14.1 nm in Co-Re/γ-Al₂O₃, indicating enhanced dispersion of cobalt species upon rhenium promotion.
- **Observations:** The presence of well-defined Co₃O₄ peaks confirms successful cobalt loading and crystallinity. The reduction in crystallite size with Re addition suggests improved dispersion, which is beneficial for catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Reducibility and Hydrogen Activation
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was employed to evaluate the reducibility of cobalt species and the effect of Re on hydrogen activation, both critical for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** Two distinct reduction peaks are observed: the first at approximately 370 °C (Co₃O₄ → CoO) and the second at about 450 °C (CoO → metallic Co). The addition of Re induces a negative shift in these peaks, indicating that Re facilitates the reduction process.
- **Observations:** The negative peak shift is attributed to hydrogen spillover from Re, which enhances the reducibility of cobalt oxides and promotes the formation of active metallic Co sites.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ adsorption-desorption isotherms (BET analysis) were used to assess the textural properties, including surface area and pore structure, which influence catalyst accessibility and mass transfer.
- **Findings:** The commercial mesoporous γ-Al₂O₃ support exhibits a surface area of approximately 148 m²/g and an average pore size of 24.44 nm. Upon cobalt and rhenium loading, a slight decrease in pore volume and pore size is observed, but the mesoporous structure is largely retained.
- **Observations:** The large surface area and pore size facilitate uniform dispersion of metal nanoparticles and efficient reactant diffusion, supporting high catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was utilized to visualize the morphology and particle size distribution of the catalysts, providing direct evidence of dispersion.
- **Findings:** TEM images reveal that Co-Re/γ-Al₂O₃ consists of uniformly dispersed nanoparticles with less pronounced particle growth compared to the unpromoted catalyst. The promoted catalyst exhibits smaller and more uniform particles, consistent with XRD results.
- **Observations:** Enhanced dispersion and minimized aggregation of cobalt nanoparticles are achieved with Re promotion, which is critical for maximizing active surface area.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Composition and Electronic State
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to analyze the surface composition and electronic state of cobalt species, ensuring the preservation of active sites.
- **Findings:** XPS spectra show similar Co 2p binding energies for both Co/Al₂O₃ and Co-Re/γ-Al₂O₃ catalysts, with calibration against the C1s peak at 284.6 eV. This indicates that the electronic state of cobalt is maintained despite Re addition.
- **Observations:** The unchanged binding energies confirm that Re promotion does not alter the oxidation state of cobalt, ensuring the stability of active sites under reaction conditions.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The Co-Re/γ-Al₂O₃ catalyst achieved a maximum CO conversion of 93.7% under optimized conditions (200 °C, 850 kPa, GHSV 400 h⁻¹, H₂/CO = 2.30). At a GHSV of 1000 h⁻¹ and H₂/CO = 2.0, CO conversion increased from 16.05% to 20.56% as the H₂/CO ratio was raised. Lowering GHSV (i.e., increasing residence time) significantly enhanced CO conversion, with the highest values observed at GHSV 400 h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increases with higher temperature, pressure (up to 850 kPa), and lower GHSV. The addition of Re notably improves CO conversion compared to unpromoted catalysts, attributed to enhanced hydrogen activation and cobalt dispersion.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the percentage of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under optimized conditions (GHSV 400 h⁻¹), CH₄ selectivity was reduced to 6.5%. The H₂/CO ratio had minimal effect on CH₄ selectivity, while lower GHSV and higher pressure favored reduced methane formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Co-Re/γ-Al₂O₃ catalyst effectively suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.3 C₁₀–C₂₀ (Diesel) Selectivity
- **Description:** Diesel selectivity is defined as the fraction of total hydrocarbons in the C₁₀–C₂₀ range, calculated as:
\[
\text{C}_{10-20} \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_{10-20},\text{out}}}{\sum F_{\text{hydrocarbons,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The catalyst achieved up to 62.3% selectivity to C₁₀–C₂₀ hydrocarbons, significantly exceeding the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) maximum of 39.7% (for α = 0.87). At GHSV 400 h⁻¹, C₁₀–C₂₀ selectivity reached 55.6%, with concurrent reductions in CH₄ and C₂–C₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high diesel selectivity is attributed to the synergistic effect of Re promotion and optimized reaction conditions, which enhance chain growth and suppress light hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of hydrocarbon chain propagation versus termination, described by the ASF distribution:
\[
W_n = (1 - \alpha) \alpha^{n-1}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the molar fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** The article references a maximum diesel selectivity of 39.7% at α = 0.87, but the Co-Re/γ-Al₂O₃ catalyst surpasses this, indicating a favorable shift in product distribution.
- **Observations and Trends:** The enhanced α factor is linked to improved hydrogen activation and cobalt dispersion, promoting longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over extended operation.
- **Experimental Data:** Over a 15-day continuous test, the catalyst maintained a CO conversion of 93.7% and diesel selectivity of 62.3%, with negligible deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The robust stability is attributed to the uniform dispersion of active sites and the beneficial effect of Re, ensuring sustained performance under low-pressure Fischer–Tropsch conditions.
