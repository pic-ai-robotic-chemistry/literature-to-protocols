## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Ru/TiO₂–x nanocatalysts is performed using a streamlined wet impregnation method, which has been optimized for reproducibility and precise control over nanoparticle dispersion. The experimental procedure is organized through the following sequential steps:

#### 1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
• RuCl₃·3H₂O (1.6 g of an 8.2 wt% solution, equivalent to 0.0317 g Ru per gram of solution) is first diluted to 30 mL with deionized water.  
• Next, 2.0 g of rutile TiO₂ (40 nm, 99.8% metal basis) is added to the Ru solution.  
• The mixture is subjected to vigorous stirring during evaporation in a water bath maintained at 50 °C, ensuring uniform distribution of the Ru precursor onto the TiO₂ support.

#### 2. Precipitation–Deposition (if Reported)  
• This step is not performed in the current wet impregnation procedure; the emphasis is solely on achieving homogeneous mixing and effective precursor deposition via evaporation.

#### 3. Drying and Calcination  
• The evaporated mixture is dried at 120 °C overnight to remove residual moisture.  
• Following drying, calcination is carried out in air at 300 °C for 3 hours. This step is critical for converting the Ru precursor into its oxidized form and establishing strong metal–support interactions.

#### 4. Washing and Post‑treatment  
• To eliminate any residual chlorides, the calcined sample is washed with a 1 mol L⁻¹ ammonia solution. The washing process is repeated until AgNO₃ testing confirms the absence of chloride precipitate.  
• The washed sample is then dried at 60 °C overnight, ensuring complete moisture removal prior to reduction.

#### 5. Pre‑reduction  
• Finally, the catalyst is pre‑reduced in a hydrogen atmosphere (20 mL min⁻¹) at controlled temperatures ranging from 200 °C to 600 °C. This reduction step not only generates metallic Ru nanoparticles (approximately 2 nm in size) but also induces the formation of a tunable TiOₓ overlayer via strong metal–support interactions, essential for optimized CO activation in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This method offers a balance between synthesis simplicity, scalability, and cost‐effectiveness, ensuring uniformity in nanoparticle size and active site distribution critical for subsequent catalytic evaluation.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Ru/TiO₂–x
- **RuCl₃·3H₂O**: Weighed 1.6 g using an analytical balance.
- **Rutile TiO₂ (40 nm)**: Weighed 2.0 g to serve as the support for the catalyst.

In this step, both the metal precursor and the support material are measured with precision to ensure reproducibility. The exact quantities are recorded to maintain consistency in catalyst composition and to facilitate later comparisons of activity.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Ru/TiO₂–x
- **Aqueous RuCl₃·3H₂O solution**: Prepared by dissolving RuCl₃·3H₂O in deionized water; 30 mL of the solution is poured using a syringe.  
- **Deionized water**: When needed for dilution or dissolution, 30 mL is accurately measured and transferred using a measuring cylinder.

These liquid transfer steps emphasize the methodical addition of liquids to guarantee that the precursor is fully dissolved before interacting with the TiO₂ support. The choice of equipment—syringe for the precursor solution and measuring cylinder for plain deionized water—underscores attention to reproducibility and precision.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Ru/TiO₂–x
- **RuCl₃·3H₂O/TiO₂ suspension**: Mixed vigorously during the evaporation process for 30 minutes at 50 °C in a water bath.  
- **Final catalyst mixture**: Stirred continuously to ensure homogeneous dispersion of Ru species on the TiO₂ support, fostering effective metal–support interactions crucial for subsequent TiOₓ overlayer formation.

The mixing process is carried out under strictly controlled temperature conditions to maintain the vigor of agitation required for complete dispersion of the precursor on the support. This step is critical to achieving uniform metal distribution on the catalyst, which in turn affects the catalytic performance and reproducibility of the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Ru/TiO₂–x Nanocatalysts

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  - RuCl₃·3H₂O: Begin with 1.6 g of an 8.2 wt% aqueous RuCl₃·3H₂O solution (equivalent to 0.0317 g Ru per gram of solution), diluted to 30 mL with deionized water.  
  - Rutile TiO₂: 2.0 g (40 nm, 99.8% metal basis).  
- **Conditions**:  
  - Use a 50 °C water bath.  
  - Vigorous stirring throughout the evaporation process ensures homogeneous dispersion and prevents agglomeration.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve and dilute the RuCl₃·3H₂O solution to 30 mL with deionized water.  
  - Add 2.0 g of rutile TiO₂ to the solution to form a uniform suspension.  
  - Evaporate the suspension in the water bath at 50 °C while stirring vigorously until a dry precursor is obtained.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Accurate reagent measurement and controlled stirring are essential for uniform Ru dispersion.

##### Step 2: Drying, Calcination, and Washing  
- **Drying**:  
  - Initial drying at 120 °C overnight is performed to remove moisture gently without degrading the precursors.  
- **Calcination**:  
  - Calcine the dried precursor in air at 300 °C for 3 hours. Air fosters oxidation, promoting the formation of a stable metal–support interface.  
- **Washing**:  
  - Wash the calcined product repeatedly with a 1 mol L⁻¹ dilute ammonia solution until no precipitate forms upon testing with 0.1 mol L⁻¹ AgNO₃. This step removes residual chlorides that could adversely affect catalyst performance.

##### Step 3: Final Drying & Activation  
- **Drying**:  
  - Post-washing, dry the sample at 60 °C overnight to eliminate any remaining moisture.  
- **Activation**:  
  - Pre-reduction is conducted under a H₂ flow rate of 20 mL min⁻¹ at selected temperatures (ranging from 200 to 600 °C). The reduction temperature, noted as “x” in Ru/TiO₂–x, is chosen to optimize the formation of a tunable TiOₓ overlayer on Ru nanoparticles. This overlayer is critical in controlling CO activation and ensuring high catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

Each step is designed to ensure reproducibility and offers clear, controlled conditions, maintaining the integrity of the Ru nanoparticles on the rutile TiO₂ support while allowing systematic tuning of the metal/support interface.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X-ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to determine the crystalline structure of the catalyst, confirming the rutile phase and matching lattice parameters between TiO₂ and RuO₂ while indicating high dispersion of Ru.
- **Instruments and Models**: PANalytical X’Pert-Pro X-ray diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Utilized Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1541 nm) at 40 kV and 40 mA; data acquired in continuous-scan mode from 10° to 80°.
- **Test Parameters**: Scanning rate of 10°/min.
- **Key Notes**: Accurate sample alignment is essential to minimize diffraction errors.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: The BET method was applied to quantify specific surface areas and pore volumes of the catalysts.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2460.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were degassed in vacuum at 110 °C for 1 hour and then at 300 °C for 4 hours; measurements were conducted at –196 °C.
- **Test Parameters**: BET analysis over a relative pressure range of P/P₀ = 0.05–0.30; pore volume determined at P/P₀ = 0.95.
- **Key Notes**: Thorough degassing is critical for reliable adsorption results.

### 4.3 HAADF-STEM and HRTEM
- **Operational Description**: These microscopy techniques were used for imaging Ru nanoparticle dispersion and verifying their uniform size (∼2 nm).
- **Instruments and Models**: JEOL JEM-2100F operated at 200 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were prepared by drying, calcination, and, for HRTEM, in situ reduction under H₂.
- **Test Parameters**: At least 200 particles were counted to ensure statistically relevant size distribution.
- **Key Notes**: Maintaining well-dispersed samples is crucial for accurate imaging.

### 4.4 X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS)
- **Operational Description**: XAS (including EXAFS and XANES) elucidated the Ru–Ru and Ru–O coordination environment dynamically as a function of reduction temperature.
- **Instruments and Models**: Instrument details were optimized for Ru K-edge measurements.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements focused on tracking coordination number changes.
- **Test Parameters**: Coordination numbers altered with temperature, revealing improved metal reduction.
- **Key Notes**: This method complements microscopy by revealing atomic-level interactions.

### 4.5 X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: XPS was performed to analyze the surface chemical state and quantify the exposure of metallic Ru.
- **Instruments and Models**: Thermofisher ESCALAB 250Xi using monochromated Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were pretreated in H₂ and maintained under an inert atmosphere to minimize air exposure.
- **Test Parameters**: Standard energy settings were applied for surface analysis.
- **Key Notes**: Rapid transfer post-pre-treatment is crucial to prevent oxidation.

### 4.6 Underpotential Deposition (Cu upd) Experiments
- **Operational Description**: Cu upd was utilized to quantify the surface area of metallic Ru via controlled electrochemical deposition and stripping.
- **Instruments and Models**: Electrochemical cell with pre-cleaned electrodes.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Conducted in a solution of 0.1 M H₂SO₄ and 0.002 M CuSO₄ with electrode polarization at 0.3 V for 100 seconds.
- **Test Parameters**: Followed by linear voltammetry to oxidize the deposited Cu.
- **Key Notes**: Strict electrode preparation and polarization control ensure reproducibility.

### 4.7 In Situ DRIFTS with CO Chemisorption
- **Operational Description**: In situ DRIFTS was applied to monitor CO adsorption behavior and distinguish between Ru⁺ species and metallic Ru sites.
- **Instruments and Models**: Bruker Equinox 55 DRIFTS setup.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were pretreated under H₂ (20 mL/min) for 1 hour, purged with He for 30 minutes, then cooled to 160 °C before introducing 5 vol% CO in He (20 mL/min).
- **Test Parameters**: IR spectra were recorded every 30 seconds over a 10-minute period during a subsequent switch to 10 vol% H₂ in He.
- **Key Notes**: This method provides time-resolved insights into adsorbed species dynamics.

### 4.8 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR evaluated the reduction behavior of the catalyst across a temperature range, revealing active Ru site accessibility.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics AutoChem II 2920.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Performed using a H₂ flow of 20 mL/min, with temperature ramping from 200 °C up to 600 °C.
- **Test Parameters**: A ramp rate of 10 °C/min was maintained, following pretreatment in an inert atmosphere.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and precise temperature control are critical for accurate reduction profiles.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
The catalyst pre‐reduction step is a critical component in preparing the Ru/TiO₂–x catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. In this process, the catalyst precursor is subjected to H₂ pre‐reduction at defined temperatures to convert oxidized species into active metallic Ru while simultaneously inducing the formation of a tunable TiOₓ overlayer through strong metal–support interactions (SMSI). The experimental protocol specifies several distinct activation temperatures: 200 °C, 300 °C, 400 °C, 450 °C, 500 °C, and 600 °C. According to the reported findings, operating at 450 °C produces the catalyst with the highest intrinsic activity, achieving a balance between sufficient Ru exposure and optimal overlayer formation. This temperature facilitates the migration of TiOₓ species onto the Ru nanoparticles without over-encapsulation, a factor essential for effective CO activation in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas  
The pre‐reduction procedure utilizes H₂ as the sole activation gas, leveraging its reducing capabilities to transform Ru-based oxides into metallic Ru. The use of H₂—flowing at a controlled rate of 20 mL/min—ensures a consistent and reproducible reduction environment. This hydrogen atmosphere plays a dual role in both reducing the catalyst precursor (e.g., RuCl₃·3H₂O-derived RuO₂) and promoting the formation of the active metal–support interface via the development of the TiOₓ overlayer. The direct correspondence between the H₂ flow rate and activation efficiency is critical, as it influences the extent and uniformity of the Ru reduction process.

Taken together, the defined activation temperature range and controlled H₂ flow rate are explicitly reported as integral parameters within the catalyst synthesis protocol. These conditions, sourced directly from the experimental details, ensure that the catalyst’s active state is achieved reproducibly, thereby enabling a clear correlation between pre‐reduction conditions and observed catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) reaction conditions are precisely defined to ensure reproducibility and optimal catalyst performance for the Ru/TiO₂–x nanocatalysts.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature is set at 160 °C. This parameter is critical, as it governs the activation of CO and subsequent bond cleavage at the Ru/TiO₂ interface. At 160 °C, the reactor setup maintains consistent heat using a stainless-steel fixed-bed reactor design. Observations indicate that this temperature supports effective CO activation and maintains catalyst stability, with the Ru/TiO₂–450 catalyst showing consistent activity over prolonged reaction times.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Conducted at a pressure of 2 MPa, the experiments control the syngas concentration in the reactor. The elevated pressure is vital for enhancing reaction kinetics and ensuring an efficient distribution of reactants, which in turn favors the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons. The pressure setting benefits the overall selectivity of the Fischer–Tropsch process and stabilizes the catalyst by minimizing fluctuations in active site exposure.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The syngas mixture employed has a composition of H₂/CO/Ar = 64/32/4 (v/v/v), which corresponds to an effective H₂/CO ratio of 2. This ratio is carefully chosen to promote the formation of higher carbon number products while limiting excessive methane production. The balance in hydrogen availability is critical for controlling hydrogenation reactions and ensuring a desirable hydrocarbon distribution.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
Space-time velocity is defined as the gas flow rate per catalyst mass, measured in mL/(g_cat·h). Operating within a range of 1200 to 6000 mL/(g_cat·h), this parameter regulates the exposure time of syngas to the active sites on the catalyst. A controlled space-time velocity ensures a consistent reaction environment that supports reproducibility and prevents catalyst deactivation. Through careful adjustment of the flow rates, the experiments confirm stable catalytic performance and allow for precise tuning of the reaction kinetics.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of the catalysts were assessed using X-ray Diffraction (XRD). This technique is essential for confirming the integrity of the TiO₂ support and the dispersion state of Ru species, both of which influence catalyst stability and performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS).
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirmed that the TiO₂ support retained the rutile phase across all samples. No characteristic peaks for metallic Ru⁰ were observed, indicating that Ru nanoparticles were highly dispersed and below the XRD detection limit. The rutile TiO₂ and RuO₂ phases exhibited matching lattice parameters, facilitating the formation of a RuO₂/TiO₂ interphase during calcination.
- **Observations:** The lattice matching between TiO₂ and RuO₂ supports the resistance to Ru nanoparticle aggregation, even after high-temperature reduction, which is critical for maintaining catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was employed to determine the specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution. These parameters are important for ensuring efficient reactant diffusion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** All Ru/TiO₂–x samples exhibited nearly identical BET surface areas and pore volumes, with pore volume determined for pores less than 40.3 nm in diameter at P/P₀ = 0.95. The uniformity in textural properties across samples indicates that observed catalytic differences arise from surface and interface modifications rather than from changes in porosity.
- **Observations:** Consistent surface area and porosity across the catalyst series ensure that variations in catalytic performance are attributable to changes in the metal/support interface.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) were used to visualize Ru nanoparticle dispersion, size, and the formation of the TiOₓ overlayer. These morphological features are directly linked to catalyst reactivity and stability.
- **Findings:** Ru nanoparticles were uniformly dispersed on the TiO₂ support with an average size of approximately 2 nm. At reduction temperatures above 300 °C, a distinct TiOₓ overlayer was observed encapsulating the Ru nanoparticles, with the degree of encapsulation increasing at higher temperatures (up to 600 °C).
- **Observations:** The formation of the TiOₓ overlayer is tunable via reduction temperature and is central to modulating the metal/support interface, which governs CO activation and overall FTS performance. Post-reaction TEM confirmed that the Ru particle size and dispersion remained stable, particularly for the optimally reduced Ru/TiO₂–450 catalyst.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Surface States
- **Description:** X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) were utilized to determine the oxidation states and surface composition of Ru and Ti, as well as the extent of TiOₓ overlayer formation. Underpotential deposition of Cu (Cu upd) quantified the surface exposure of metallic Ru.
- **Findings:** XPS revealed an increase in Ti³⁺ content from 6.7% (at 300 °C) to 11.6% (at 600 °C), indicating progressive reduction of TiO₂ and growth of the TiOₓ overlayer. The surface Ru/Ti atomic ratio decreased with increasing reduction temperature, consistent with greater Ru encapsulation. XAS (EXAFS) showed that the Ru–Ru coordination number increased (2.2 to 5.3) and Ru–O decreased (4.0 to 2.4) as reduction temperature rose, confirming enhanced reduction and overlayer formation. Cu upd experiments demonstrated that surface metallic Ru exposure peaked at intermediate reduction temperatures and declined at higher temperatures due to overlayer coverage.
- **Observations:** The tunable TiOₓ overlayer, as evidenced by these techniques, is critical for optimizing the balance between active Ru sites and strong metal–support interaction, directly impacting catalytic activity and selectivity in FTS.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** In situ Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS) with CO chemisorption was conducted to probe the nature of surface Ru sites and their interaction with CO, a key step in FTS.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS spectra identified bands corresponding to CO adsorbed on both partially oxidized Ru⁺ sites (multi- and mono-carbonyls at ~2136 and 2075 cm⁻¹) and metallic Ru sites (linear CO at 2056 cm⁻¹). H₂ purging selectively removed CO from Ru⁺ sites, indicating their higher reactivity.
- **Observations:** The presence and accessibility of Ru⁺ sites at the Ru/TiO₂ interface, modulated by the TiOₓ overlayer, are essential for efficient CO activation and contribute to the superior FTS performance of the optimally reduced catalyst.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  X_{\text{CO}} = 1 - \frac{A_{\text{out}}(\text{CO}) / A_{\text{out}}(\text{Ar})}{A_{\text{in}}(\text{CO}) / A_{\text{in}}(\text{Ar})}
  \]
  where \(A\) denotes the chromatographic peak area and Ar serves as the internal standard.
- **Experimental Data:** Ru/TiO₂ catalysts reduced at various temperatures (200–600 °C) display a volcano-type trend in CO conversion. The catalyst reduced at 450 °C (Ru/TiO₂–450) achieves the highest CO conversion under standard FTS conditions (160 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO/Ar = 64/32/4). Lower conversions are observed for catalysts reduced at both lower (200–300 °C) and higher (600 °C) temperatures.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal CO conversion at 450 °C is attributed to the formation of a balanced TiOₓ overlayer, which enhances the Ru/TiOₓ interface for CO activation. Excessive overlayer coverage at 600 °C reduces active Ru exposure, diminishing conversion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of undesirable oxidation. It is determined from GC peak areas using Ar as an internal standard:
  \[
  S_{\text{CO}_2} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity is measured for all catalysts, with values varying according to reduction temperature and CO conversion. Specific numerical values are not detailed, but the trend follows the activity profile.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower CO₂ selectivity is generally favored and is minimized at the optimal reduction temperature (450 °C), correlating with enhanced CO activation and hydrocarbon selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity denotes the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  S_{\text{CH}_4} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity increases under conditions favoring hydrogenation, particularly for Ru/TiO₂–450. The selectivity is determined via GC analysis, normalized with Ar.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Ru/TiO₂–450 catalyst exhibits a favorable balance, with CH₄ selectivity reflecting efficient CO activation and a preference for chain growth over methane formation under standard FTS conditions.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  S_{\text{C}_3^+} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** All Ru/TiO₂–x catalysts demonstrate high C₅⁺ selectivity, reaching up to 90% for Ru/TiO₂–450.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high C₃⁺ (and C₅⁺) selectivity for Ru/TiO₂–450 underscores the effectiveness of the optimized Ru/TiOₓ interface in promoting long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model:
  \[
  \log\left(\frac{W_n}{n}\right) = n \cdot \log(\alpha) + \text{constant}
  \]
  where \(W_n\) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \(n\).
- **Experimental Data:** Ru/TiO₂–450 exhibits an α factor of 0.90, indicating a strong propensity for chain growth.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high α value for Ru/TiO₂–450 correlates with the optimized metal/support interface, facilitating the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation rate} = \frac{X_{\text{CO,initial}} - X_{\text{CO,final}}}{\text{time (h)}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Ru/TiO₂–450 demonstrates exceptional stability, with a deactivation rate of less than 0.1% per hour over 100 hours of continuous operation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low deactivation rate and stable Ru particle size (confirmed by post-reaction TEM) highlight the durability of the Ru/TiO₂–450 catalyst, attributed to strong metal–support interactions and the protective TiOₓ overlayer.
