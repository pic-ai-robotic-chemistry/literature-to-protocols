## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fischer–Tropsch catalysts in this study is achieved by a straightforward physical mixing method, which is favored for its simplicity and scalability compared to more complex techniques such as co‑precipitation or precipitation–deposition. The method involves the following structured steps:

#### • Catalyst Precursor Mixing
1. Drying/Pre-treatment:  
   • Both the catalyst precursor (α‑Fe₂O₃) and the inert oxide supports (SiO₂ or Al₂O₃) are dried at 120 °C for 12 hours to remove moisture, ensuring optimal interaction during mixing.  
2. Grinding:  
   • The dried powders are then ground in a mortar for 30 minutes. This critical step ensures a homogeneous mixture and uniform dispersion of the active α‑Fe₂O₃ relative to the support, as confirmed by the even distribution observed in the detailed characterization (e.g., in‑situ XPS and Mössbauer spectroscopy).

#### • Pressing and Sieving
3. Pressing:  
   • The well-mixed powders are pressed under 8 MPa. This pressing force compacts the mixture, enhancing the contact between active sites and ensuring a consistent catalyst bed height, which is vital for reproducible performance in fixed‑bed reactors.  
4. Sieving:  
   • Post‑pressing, the catalysts are sieved to obtain particles in the 20–40 mesh range, ensuring uniform particle size distribution and optimal flow characteristics during reaction.

#### • Post‑processing – Reduction and Passivation
5. Reduction:  
   • Prior to catalytic testing, the pressed catalysts undergo reduction in a fixed‑bed reactor using syngas (H₂/CO = 1) at 350 °C for 10 hours. This step promotes the transformation of α‑Fe₂O₃ into various iron carbide phases.
6. Passivation:  
   • A subsequent passivation step is carried out with a 1% O₂/99% Ar mixture at room temperature, stabilizing the catalysts for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This physical mixing procedure, devoid of additional chemical deposition steps, benefits from lower equipment demands and enhanced reproducibility. The outlined method, with careful control of temperature, pressure, and mixing durations, not only simplifies the synthesis workflow but also ensures consistent catalyst composition and performance across batches.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD patterns are obtained to confirm the crystalline phases in the catalysts, identifying the transformation of α‑Fe₂O₃ to iron carbide phases.
- **Instruments and Models**: Shimadzu XRD‑6000.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) at 40 kV and 30 mA, with a 2θ scan range of 10–90° at a rate of 5°/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Samples are degassed at 180 °C for 6 hours under vacuum prior to measurement.
- **Key Notes**: Accurate sample mounting is critical for reliable diffraction patterns.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Evaluates reduction behavior by monitoring the conversion of Fe oxides under a reducing atmosphere.
- **Instruments and Models**: Not specified.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Catalyst particles (20–40 mesh) are pretreated at 200 °C for 1 hour under N₂, then exposed to 10% H₂/90% Ar at 30 mL/min; temperature is ramped to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Maintaining uniform loading is essential.
- **Key Notes**: Reveals formation of Fe–Si and Fe–Al interactions that influence reduction profiles.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Monitors the carburization process, transforming reduced iron species into various iron carbides.
- **Instruments and Models**: Not specified.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Uses 10% CO/90% Ar at 30 mL/min; key temperature landmarks include 350 °C for initial reduction and ~630 °C for carburization.
- **Test Parameters**: Similar ramping conditions to H₂‑TPR.
- **Key Notes**: Highlights the onset of FexC formation.

### 4.4 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: Determines the surface area and porosity via N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms.
- **Instruments and Models**: Quantachrome Autosorb‑iQ‑C.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements at −196 °C using degassed samples.
- **Test Parameters**: BET surface area ~6.72 m²/g; pore volume of 0.019 cm³/g.
- **Key Notes**: Confirms a nonporous structure minimizing diffusion limitations.

### 4.5 In‑situ X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: Analyzes the surface electronic state and chemical shifts of Fe species after syngas treatment.
- **Instruments and Models**: SHIMADZU AXIS Supra with an Al Kα source.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Treatment at 280 °C (GHSV = 9000 mL/g‑Fe₂O₃·h⁻¹), with binding energies calibrated via the C1s peak (284.8 eV).
- **Test Parameters**: Gaussian–Lorentzian fitting for the Fe 2p peaks.
- **Key Notes**: Detects subtle shifts indicative of ε‑Fe₂C formation.

### 4.6 Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS)
- **Operational Description**: Observes CO adsorption behavior on catalysts to infer active sites.
- **Instruments and Models**: DRIFTS cell (specific model not provided).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Sample diluted with KBr (1:30), re‑reduced at 350 °C (30 mL/min, 1 hour); CO exposure at room temperature followed by a N₂ purge.
- **Test Parameters**: Spectra acquired at 4 cm⁻¹ resolution over 32 scans.
- **Key Notes**: Careful sample preparation minimizes contamination.

### 4.7 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)
- **Operational Description**: Complements DRIFTS by detecting adsorbed species on the catalyst surface.
- **Instruments and Models**: Not specified.
- **Characterization Conditions and Test Parameters**: Recorded at 4 cm⁻¹ resolution.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

- **Definition:** The activation temperature is fundamental for initiating the reduction of the iron oxide precursor into its active carbide forms.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 350 °C in a controlled fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** At this temperature, α‑Fe₂O₃ is efficiently reduced, facilitating the subsequent carburization to form active iron carbide species such as ε‑Fe₂C.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation is critical for creating a dual environment that promotes both reduction and carburization of the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with a H₂/CO ratio of 1, supplemented with 5% argon as an internal standard, is introduced during the activation step.
- **Observations:** This balanced gas composition ensures that hydrogen acts as a reducing agent while carbon monoxide provides the necessary carbon source. The use of argon further aids in maintaining a consistent gas flow, thereby enhancing the uniformity of the reduction and carburization processes.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation is designed to allow complete transformation of the precursor into its active form, ensuring full development of the desired iron carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction process is carried out in the fixed-bed reactor for a period of 10 hours.
- **Observations:** The extended activation time is critical for achieving complete carburization. This prolonged exposure under well‐controlled conditions contributes to the formation of highly active, carbon‐rich phases without inducing significant sintering.

#### 5.1.4 Reactor Setup
- **Definition:** A well-designed reactor environment is essential to guarantee reproducibility and uniform catalyst treatment.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed in a stainless steel fixed-bed reactor featuring an 8 mm inner diameter and equipped with mass flow meters to precisely regulate the gas flow.
- **Observations:** The controlled reactor configuration ensures consistent temperature distribution and gas-solid contact throughout the catalyst bed, thus promoting homogeneous reduction and activation across all catalyst particles.

These carefully defined activation parameters collectively enable the transformation of the precursor into a catalyst with enhanced performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** Reaction temperature is critical in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis as it governs the activation of syngas and promotes the formation of the active iron carbide phases, such as ε‑Fe₂C.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was maintained at 280 °C in a fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** At 280 °C, the catalysts—particularly the SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃ mixtures—demonstrated enhanced syngas activation and formation of active phases. However, the pure α‑Fe₂O₃ catalyst experienced deactivation after reaching its maximum activity within a few hours, likely due to the formation of a graphitic carbon layer.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure controls the concentration of reactants in the system and influences the kinetic behavior, enabling better gas solubility and reaction rates.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The tests were conducted under a pressure of 1 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at 1 MPa ensured that syngas remained at a sufficient concentration for effective conversion, leading to improved product distribution and minimizing the potential buildup of byproducts.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio affects the nature of the hydrocarbon products formed, balancing between chain growth and undesired byproduct formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An equimolar H₂/CO ratio (1:1) was employed.
- **Observations:** This balanced ratio was fundamental in promoting the formation of long-chain hydrocarbons and the active iron carbide phases, while also stabilizing product selectivity.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The Gas Hourly Space Velocity (GHSV) is defined as the volumetric flow rate of syngas per unit mass of catalyst per hour, directly impacting reactant exposure and conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was maintained at 10,000 mL/(g‑Fe₂O₃·h).
- **Observations:** The chosen GHSV ensured efficient contact between syngas and the catalyst without inducing significant mass transfer limitations. Although a higher flow rate may reduce contact time, it simultaneously prevents catalyst deactivation by minimizing the accumulation of heavy carbonaceous deposits.

These precisely defined conditions—280 °C, 1 MPa, H₂/CO of 1, and a GHSV of 10,000 mL/(g‑Fe₂O₃·h)—are critical for reproducibility and have been demonstrated to optimize both the formation of the active ε‑Fe₂C phase and the overall catalytic performance in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases present in the catalysts were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for tracking the transformation of α‑Fe₂O₃ into active iron carbide species under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of fresh catalysts display peaks corresponding to crystalline α‑Fe₂O₃ (PDF#33-0664). After reduction and Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS), these peaks disappear, and new diffraction peaks emerge in the 2θ range of 40°–50°, indicative of iron carbide phases such as χ‑Fe₅C₂, θ‑Fe₃C, and ε‑Fe₂C. The similarity of XRD patterns for reduced and used catalysts in this region confirms the stability of these carbide phases during FTS.
- **Observations:** The formation and persistence of iron carbides, particularly ε‑Fe₂C, are directly linked to enhanced catalytic activity and stability.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption–desorption (BET) analysis was used to determine surface area and porosity, which influence diffusion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** The catalysts exhibit a BET surface area of 6.72 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.019 cm³/g, with type-II isotherms indicating a nonporous structure.
- **Observations:** The low surface area and pore volume minimize diffusion limitations, ensuring that observed catalytic performance reflects intrinsic activity.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Carburization Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) with H₂ and CO was employed to assess the reducibility and carburization of iron oxides, as well as the influence of inert oxide promoters.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR profiles show that the first reduction step (α‑Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄) shifts to higher temperatures for SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃, indicating Fe–Si and Fe–Al interactions. The second reduction step (Fe₃O₄ to FeO/Fe) shifts to lower temperatures, facilitating carburization. CO-TPR reveals that Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃ has the lowest carburization temperature and highest CO consumption.
- **Observations:** These shifts confirm that inert oxide mixing promotes the formation of C-rich iron carbides, especially ε‑Fe₂C.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Composition and Electronic State
- **Description:** In-situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze surface elemental composition and electronic states, particularly the effects of oxide promoters.
- **Findings:** After FTS, the Fe 2p binding energies in SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃ shift to higher values compared to pure α‑Fe₂O₃, indicating enhanced electron transfer due to Fe–Si or Fe–Al interactions. The proportion of ε‑Fe₂C is significantly higher in the mixed catalysts.
- **Observations:** The electronic modification of Fe by inert oxides is directly correlated with increased ε‑Fe₂C formation and improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Quantification of Iron Carbide Phases
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy provided quantitative analysis of iron carbide species present after reaction.
- **Findings:** The ε‑Fe₂C content increases from 0.6% in pure α‑Fe₂O₃ to 9.8% in SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃ and 14.2% in Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃.
- **Observations:** The substantial increase in ε‑Fe₂C with oxide mixing is a key factor in the observed enhancement of FTS activity and stability.

#### 6.1.6 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** Scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were used to examine particle morphology and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** All powders have particle sizes above 300 nm, ensuring nonporous character. EDS mapping confirms uniform distribution of SiO₂ and Al₂O₃ around α‑Fe₂O₃. TEM images show that mixed catalysts lack the graphitic carbon overcoat observed in deactivated pure α‑Fe₂O₃, and ε‑Fe₂C forms preferentially at oxide interfaces.
- **Observations:** Uniform mixing and absence of carbon overcoating in mixed catalysts contribute to their superior stability.

#### 6.1.7 Surface Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was used to probe CO adsorption characteristics.
- **Findings:** Linear CO adsorption bands at 2004 cm⁻¹ (SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃) and 2006 cm⁻¹ (Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃) are assigned to ε‑Fe₂C, with increased intensity correlating with higher ε‑Fe₂C content and activity.
- **Observations:** The presence and intensity of these bands substantiate the role of ε‑Fe₂C as the most active phase for CO activation in FTS.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (280 °C, 1.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1, GHSV = 10,000 mL/g-Fe₂O₃·h⁻¹), the Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃ = 1 catalyst achieved a CO conversion of up to 61.6%, which is approximately 3.3 times higher than that of the pure α‑Fe₂O₃ catalyst. The SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃ = 1 catalyst also exhibited a significant increase in CO conversion compared to the pure catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** The incorporation of Al₂O₃ or SiO₂ notably enhances CO conversion, correlating with increased formation of the active ε‑Fe₂C phase. The trend in CO conversion follows the order: α‑Fe₂O₃ < SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃ < Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water-gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The study reports that the mixed oxide catalysts (SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃) exhibit lower CO₂ selectivity compared to pure α‑Fe₂O₃, indicating suppressed water-gas shift activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower CO₂ selectivity in the mixed catalysts is attributed to the higher content of ε‑Fe₂C, which favors hydrocarbon formation over CO₂ production.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO that yields methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** While specific numerical values are not provided, the article notes that the mixed oxide catalysts shift selectivity away from methane toward longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of ε‑Fe₂C in the mixed catalysts is associated with reduced methane selectivity and enhanced production of higher hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of catalyst effectiveness for fuel-range products.
- **Experimental Data:** The mixed oxide catalysts demonstrate increased C₃⁺ selectivity relative to pure α‑Fe₂O₃, consistent with the higher α factor and improved chain growth.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced C₃⁺ selectivity is directly linked to the formation of the ε‑Fe₂C phase, which promotes longer hydrocarbon chains.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon formation. Higher α values indicate a greater tendency to form longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** The mixed oxide catalysts exhibit higher α values than pure α‑Fe₂O₃, reflecting their improved ability to produce heavier hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The increase in α correlates with the increased content of ε‑Fe₂C, as confirmed by Mössbauer spectroscopy and in-situ XPS.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The mixed oxide catalysts (SiO₂/α‑Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃/α‑Fe₂O₃) maintain stable CO conversion over 30 hours, with negligible deactivation. In contrast, pure α‑Fe₂O₃ shows significant deactivation after reaching peak activity, attributed to graphitic carbon layer formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior stability of the mixed catalysts is due to the suppression of carbon encapsulation and the stabilization of active ε‑Fe₂C phases.

#### 6.2.7 Olefin/Paraffin Ratio (O/P)
- **Description:** The O/P ratio in the C₂–C₄ fraction indicates the selectivity toward olefins versus paraffins, relevant for chemical feedstock applications.
- **Experimental Data:** The pure α‑Fe₂O₃ catalyst exhibits a high O/P ratio of 4.2, attributed to its nonporous structure and the prevalence of θ‑Fe₃C.
- **Observations and Trends:** The mixed oxide catalysts show a more balanced O/P ratio, reflecting enhanced secondary hydrogenation and improved hydrocarbon distribution.

---

**Summary:**  
The incorporation of inert oxides (SiO₂, Al₂O₃) into α‑Fe₂O₃ catalysts significantly enhances CO conversion, C₃⁺ selectivity, and catalyst stability, primarily through the promotion of the ε‑Fe₂C phase. These modifications suppress CO₂ and CH₄ selectivity and mitigate catalyst deactivation, resulting in superior Fischer–Tropsch synthesis performance.
