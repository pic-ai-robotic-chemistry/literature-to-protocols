## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fischer–Tropsch catalysts in this work is divided into two main components: the preparation of the SiC-based support (with and without TiO₂ decoration) and the subsequent cobalt catalyst preparation via impregnation. In what follows, each method is detailed step-by-step with precise experimental conditions and observations for reproducibility.

#### Support Synthesis via Gas–Solid Reaction and Post‑processing
1. • Precursor Mixing: A gas–solid reaction is initiated where SiO vapor reacts with dispersed solid carbon to form β‑SiC.  
   • Reagents: Silicon source generating SiO vapor and finely dispersed carbon.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition: For TiO₂ decoration, microsized silicon powder is mixed with TiO₂ nanoparticles and a carbon-containing resin, forming a paste.
3. • Shaping & Carbidization: The paste is shaped (e.g., extrudates or beads) and carbidized under flowing argon at approximately 1350 °C for 1 h.  
   • Observation: CO removal via argon flow promotes complete carbidization.
4. • Post‑calcination: The TiC phase is oxidized to TiO₂ by calcination at 550 °C in air for 2 h.

#### Catalyst Preparation via Impregnation and Thermal Treatments
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation: For both undecorated 10Co/SiC and decorated 10Co/TiO₂–SiC catalysts, cobalt nitrate is dissolved in an ethanol/water (50:50) mixture.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition: The cobalt nitrate solution is introduced by pore volume impregnation onto the prepared support.  
   • Note: This step ensures even distribution of cobalt, minimizing nanoparticle agglomeration.
3. • Drying: The impregnated support is first dried at room temperature for 4 h, followed by oven‑drying at 110 °C for 8 h.
4. • Calcination: The dried material is calcined at 350 °C with a controlled heating rate of 1 °C/min for 2 h to form a Co₃O₄ precursor.
5. • Reduction: Finally, the Co₃O₄ precursor is reduced under hydrogen (pure H₂) at 300 °C for 6 h to yield the active cobalt phase.

A comparative analysis of these synthesis methods reveals that while the gas–solid reaction and subsequent TiO₂ decoration enhance metal–support interactions and lead to smaller Co particle sizes, the additional processing steps may increase synthesis complexity and cost relative to the straightforward impregnation on undecorated SiC. Each step is reported with precise conditions (temperatures, durations, and atmospheres) to ensure reproducibility and clarity for researchers aiming to replicate the Fischer–Tropsch catalyst preparation.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, we detail the preparation of the reagents for both the 10Co/SiC and 10Co/TiO₂–SiC catalysts, ensuring reproducibility through careful solid weighing, precise liquid transfer, and controlled mixing protocols.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 10Co/SiC  
- **Co(NO₃)₂·xH₂O**: Weighed according to the required cobalt loading for catalyst impregnation using an analytical balance with appropriate precision.  
- **SiC precursor**: Weighed in quantities that match the targeted support mass for optimal pore volume impregnation.

##### 3.1.1.2 10Co/TiO₂–SiC  
- **Co(NO₃)₂·xH₂O**: Weighed to achieve 10 wt % cobalt loading on the TiO₂-decorated SiC support, ensuring consistent catalyst composition.  
- **TiO₂ nanoparticles**: Weighed to obtain a decoration level of approximately 17 wt %, facilitating improved cobalt dispersion.  
- **SiC precursor**: Weighed precisely to provide the necessary support mass for the decorated catalyst synthesis.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 10Co/SiC  
- **Cobalt nitrate solution (50:50 ethanol/water)**: Transferred  using a graduated cylinder to guarantee that the solution fully covers the SiC support by pore volume impregnation.

##### 3.1.2.2 10Co/TiO₂–SiC  
- **Cobalt nitrate solution (50:50 ethanol/water)**: Transferred by pipetting into the TiO₂-decorated SiC support to ensure uniform distribution of the precursor across the support material.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 10Co/SiC  
- **Impregnated mixture**: Mixed gently at ambient temperature to promote even wetting of the support particles, followed by drying at room temperature for 4 hours and subsequent oven-drying at 110 °C for 8 hours.

##### 3.1.3.2 10Co/TiO₂–SiC  
- **Impregnated mixture**: Mixed thoroughly under ambient conditions to achieve a homogeneous dispersion of the cobalt precursor on the TiO₂-decorated support. This mixture then undergoes a similar drying protocol (room temperature drying for 4 hours, then 8 hours at 110 °C) to ensure complete solvent removal and reproducibility of catalyst performance.

These carefully controlled reagent preparation steps, delineated for each catalyst, integrate the weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing procedures to support a reproducible synthesis route for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 10Co/SiC

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • β-SiC support (prepared via a gas–solid reaction of SiO vapor with dispersed solid carbon)  
  • Cobalt nitrate (quantities calculated to reach a 10 wt % Co loading) dissolved in a solvent mixture of ethanol and water (50:50 by volume)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Standard laboratory stirring apparatus at ambient temperature  
- **Process**:  
  • Begin with the synthesis of the β-SiC support. Using a controlled gas–solid reaction, form β-SiC via reactive interaction between SiO vapor and carbon.  
  • Prepare the cobalt nitrate solution by dissolving the appropriate amount in the ethanol/water mixture.  
  • Mix the solution with the SiC support via pore-volume impregnation to ensure homogenous contact between precursor and support.  
- **Key Steps**: Careful control of reagent concentrations and solvent ratio is critical to maintain consistency.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**: The impregnated SiC support containing cobalt precursor  
- **Conditions**: Impregnation under room temperature stirring  
- **Process**:  
  • Allow the solution to uniformly wet the support, ensuring even loading.  
  • No additional pH adjustments are applied during this step.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  • First, allow air-drying at room temperature for 4 hours.  
  • Follow with oven-drying at 110 °C for 8 hours to remove residual solvents.  
  • Calcine the dried material at a controlled heating rate of 1 °C/min up to 350 °C, holding for 2 hours to convert cobalt nitrate into Co₃O₄.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor temperature ramping closely to avoid overheating and ensure reproducibility.

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Conditions**: Reduction under a flowing hydrogen atmosphere at 300 °C for 6 hours  
- **Process**:  
  • Perform reduction to transform Co₃O₄ into the active metallic cobalt phase, as verified by XRD and TPR analysis.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: 10Co/TiO₂–SiC

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Microsized silicon powder, TiO₂ nanoparticles, and a carbon-containing resin  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Formation of a paste at ambient mixing conditions  
- **Process**:  
  • Mix silicon powder, TiO₂ nanoparticles, and resin thoroughly to form a homogeneous paste.  
  • Shape the paste into extrudates or beads.  
  • Subject the shaped mix to carbidization at ~1350 °C under flowing argon, followed by calcination at 550 °C in air for 2 hours to convert any TiC phase into TiO₂ decoration.

##### Steps 2–4: Catalyst Assembly, Purification, Drying, Calcination & Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • Impregnate the TiO₂–decorated SiC support with the cobalt nitrate solution (ethanol/water 50:50), following the same drying (4 h at room temperature, then 8 h at 110 °C) and calcination (1 °C/min up to 350 °C) protocols as described above.  
  • Finally, reduce under hydrogen at 300 °C for 6 hours.  
- **Key Steps & Caution**: Maintain rigorous temperature control and ensure the uniform dispersion of cobalt nanoparticles.  

This systematic workflow—from precursor synthesis to final activation—ensures reproducibility and scalability of the Fischer–Tropsch catalyst preparation.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD is used to determine the crystalline structure and confirm the complete reduction of cobalt oxide in the catalysts. The powdered sample is packed on a glass slide and pretreated by outgassing at 250 °C under vacuum for 14 hours to remove moisture and adsorbed species.
- **Instruments and Models**: Bruker D‑8 Advance diffractometer equipped with a Vantec detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Cu Kα radiation (1.5406 Å) is employed at 40 kV and 40 mA. The scan range is set from 10° to 80° (2θ).
- **Test Parameters**: A step size of 0.02° and a scan speed of 0.5°/min are used.
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment and comprehensive pre-treatment are critical to avoid interference with phase identification.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: BET measurements evaluate the surface area of the SiC and TiO₂–SiC supports before and after cobalt deposition.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics sorptometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are outgassed at 250 °C under vacuum for 14 hours. Measurements are carried out at liquid nitrogen temperature using nitrogen as the adsorbate over a relative pressure range of 0.06 to 0.99.
- **Test Parameters**: The undecorated SiC shows a decrease from 40 m²/g (fresh) to 33 m²/g after cobalt deposition, while the TiO₂–SiC support decreases from 101 m²/g to 25 m²/g.
- **Key Notes**: The drop in surface area is attributed to pore plugging due to cobalt impregnation.

### 4.3 Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (TPR)
- **Operational Description**: TPR tracks the reduction behavior of cobalt oxide species under a diluted hydrogen environment.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP‑2100 setup with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Experiments use 10% H₂ in Argon with a heating rate of 15 °C/min covering a temperature range from room temperature up to 700 °C. Prior outgassing at 250 °C is performed.
- **Test Parameters**: The first peak appears at ~230 °C, with additional peaks (e.g., around 700 °C for TiO₂–SiC) indicating stronger cobalt–support interactions.
- **Key Notes**: Steam generated during reduction is trapped at −88 °C using an acetone/liquid nitrogen mixture.

### 4.4 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)
- **Operational Description**: SEM provides morphological insights, particularly regarding particle dispersion and surface texture.
- **Instruments and Models**: JEOL 6700F operating at 10 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are mounted with graphite paste and coated with a thin gold layer to diminish charging effects.
- **Test Parameters**: Qualitative assessments indicate finer cobalt dispersion in TiO₂–decorated catalysts.
- **Key Notes**: Enhanced metal dispersion is directly correlated with improved catalyst performance.

### 4.5 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & EDS/EFTEM and 59Co Zero‑Field NMR
- **Operational Description**: TEM (with EDS/EFTEM) evaluates particle size distributions and elemental mapping, while 59Co Zero‑Field NMR provides a bulk view of cobalt phases.
- **Instruments and Models**: JEOL 2100F (FEG) TEM/STEM operating at 200 kV; NMR measurements conducted at 2 K, 4.2 K, and 77 K.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Catalyst powders are crushed, sonicated in ethanol, and drop‑cast onto porous carbon copper grids. EFTEM captures Si‑L₂₃, Ti‑L₂₃, and Co‑L₂₃ edges.
- **Test Parameters**: TEM reveals an average Co particle size of ~40 nm on SiC and a bimodal distribution—with particles as small as 15 nm on TiO₂–SiC.
- **Key Notes**: These methods validate that TiO₂ decoration promotes smaller, better dispersed cobalt particles, enhancing catalyst activity.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation of the Fischer–Tropsch catalyst is a decisive step in ensuring the formation of the active metallic cobalt phase required for efficient synthesis. The process is executed in two distinct stages that target catalyst reduction and pre-reaction conditioning.

#### 5.1.1 Hydrogen Reduction Temperature and Duration
The first activation stage is the hydrogen reduction of the cobalt precursor. In this step, the calcined catalyst (comprising a Co₃O₄ precursor obtained after calcination at 350 °C with a ramp rate of 1 °C/min) is subjected to a hydrogen environment at an activation temperature of 300 °C for 6 hours. This moderate temperature is chosen to ensure complete reduction of cobalt oxide to metallic cobalt while preserving a high degree of metal dispersion and avoiding sintering. Evidence from subsequent X-ray diffraction analyses confirms the absence of residual cobalt oxide, indicating a successful reduction.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas and Flow Conditions
Pure hydrogen is employed during the reduction step to provide a highly reducing atmosphere. The gas is introduced at a flow rate of 30 mL/min per gram of catalyst. This parameter ensures an efficient exchange between the hydrogen molecules and the catalyst surface, ultimately promoting the full conversion of the Co₃O₄ precursor. The precise gas composition and flow conditions are critical in achieving a uniform reduction of the cobalt phase, thereby establishing the active sites necessary for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

#### 5.1.3 Pre-Reaction Pretreatment Under Synthesis Gas/Argon
Following hydrogen reduction, the catalyst undergoes an additional activation step aimed at preparing the catalyst for in situ synthesis conditions. This pretreatment involves exposing the catalyst to a synthesis gas/argon mixture for three days at the intended reaction conditions. This extended conditioning period is designed to further stabilize the metallic cobalt phase and optimize its performance during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The combination of hydrogen reduction and subsequent pretreatment ensures that the catalysts, including both 10Co/SiC and 10Co/TiO₂–SiC formulations, are fully activated, exhibiting enhanced metal dispersion and a controlled particle size distribution.


### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for syngas activation and kinetic control in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It influences product distribution and catalyst behavior.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic evaluation was performed at a reaction temperature of 230 °C, with process ranges from 215 to 230 °C reported.  
- **Observations:** At 230 °C, both 10Co/SiC and 10Co/TiO₂–SiC catalysts demonstrated enhanced CO conversion and maintained high selectivity towards C₅⁺ hydrocarbons. The controlled temperature helps balance activity and methane suppression, ensuring catalyst stability over extended on-stream testing (up to 100 hours).

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure governs syngas concentration in the reactor and plays a fundamental role in product selectivity and conversion efficiency.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was conducted in a tubular fixed-bed stainless-steel reactor at an elevated pressure of 40 bar.  
- **Observations:** Operating at high pressure favors enhanced CO conversion and liquid hydrocarbon formation while mitigating the risk of catalyst deactivation due to carbon deposition or sintering.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO molar ratio is central to controlling the chain growth mechanism and product distribution.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A stoichiometric ratio of 2 was maintained throughout the catalytic tests.  
- **Observations:** This precise ratio helps promote longer-chain hydrocarbon production while keeping methane formation at a minimal level, consistent with optimal performance for both catalyst variants.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, expressed as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), quantifies the ratio of gas flow rate to catalyst mass, directly influencing contact time and conversion efficiency.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 2850 h⁻¹ was employed.  
- **Observations:** This parameter ensures sufficient reactant-catalyst contact, contributing to stable CO conversion and supporting reproducible performance across multiple testing periods.

Collectively, the described conditions, managed within a tubular fixed-bed reactor setup, promote a controlled environment that ensures both high reproducibility and reliable catalytic performance during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis process.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and reduction state of cobalt were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D-8 Advance diffractometer. XRD is essential for confirming the formation of the active metallic cobalt phase and the absence of residual oxides after reduction.
- **Findings:** For both 10Co/SiC and 10Co/TiO₂–SiC catalysts, XRD patterns after reduction at 300 °C in hydrogen for 6 hours displayed only reflections corresponding to the SiC support and metallic cobalt. No peaks attributable to cobalt oxides (e.g., Co₃O₄) were observed, confirming complete reduction of the cobalt precursor.
- **Observations:** The absence of cobalt oxide peaks indicates effective reduction, while the presence of metallic cobalt is critical for Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were measured by nitrogen adsorption using a Micromeritics sorptometer. Samples were outgassed at 250 °C under vacuum for 14 hours prior to analysis to ensure accurate measurement of accessible surface area.
- **Findings:**  
  - **10Co/SiC:** BET surface area = 33 m²/g, pore volume = 0.139 cm³/g, average pore diameter = 17.1 nm  
  - **10Co/TiO₂–SiC:** BET surface area = 25 m²/g, pore volume = 0.082 cm³/g, average pore diameter = 13.0 nm  
  The TiO₂–SiC support initially exhibited a higher surface area (101 m²/g) before cobalt deposition, which decreased significantly after impregnation due to pore plugging.
- **Observations:** The pronounced drop in surface area and pore volume for TiO₂–SiC after cobalt loading is attributed to cobalt particles blocking micropores formed during TiC-to-TiO₂ transformation. This structural change impacts catalyst accessibility and dispersion.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was performed using 10% H₂ in argon to assess the reducibility of cobalt species and their interaction with the support.
- **Findings:** Both catalysts exhibited a first reduction peak around 230 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of residual cobalt nitrate and the initial reduction of Co₃O₄ to CoO. The second, broader reduction step (extending beyond 500 °C for 10Co/TiO₂–SiC) is associated with the reduction of CoO to metallic Co and the presence of strongly interacting cobalt species, particularly in the TiO₂-decorated catalyst.
- **Observations:** The extended reduction profile for 10Co/TiO₂–SiC indicates stronger metal–support interactions, which are linked to the formation of smaller, more stable cobalt particles.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Morphological features and cobalt particle size distributions were analyzed by SEM (JEOL 6700F) and TEM/EFTEM (JEOL 2100F). TEM sample preparation involved dispersion in ethanol and deposition on carbon grids.
- **Findings:**  
  - **10Co/SiC:** TEM revealed a relatively uniform cobalt particle size averaging ~40 nm.  
  - **10Co/TiO₂–SiC:** A bimodal distribution was observed, with Co particles of 5–15 nm on TiO₂ regions and 30–50 nm on SiC. EFTEM mapping confirmed the preferential association of small Co particles with TiO₂.  
  Particle size measurements from XRD (Scherrer equation) were consistent with TEM results.
- **Observations:** TiO₂ decoration significantly enhances cobalt dispersion, increasing the fraction of small (<15 nm) particles, which correlates with improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Bulk Cobalt Distribution
- **Description:** ^59Co zero-field NMR provided a bulk assessment of cobalt particle size distribution, complementing local TEM/EFTEM observations.
- **Findings:** NMR spectra indicated a higher proportion of small cobalt particles in the TiO₂–SiC catalyst compared to the undecorated SiC catalyst, supporting the enhanced dispersion observed by microscopy.
- **Observations:** The increased fraction of small, well-dispersed cobalt particles in the TiO₂–SiC catalyst is directly linked to higher Fischer–Tropsch activity and selectivity.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (230 °C, 40 bar, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 2850 h⁻¹), the 10Co/SiC catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 35.4%, while the TiO₂-decorated 10Co/TiO₂–SiC catalyst reached 50.5%. Across the temperature range (215–230 °C), the TiO₂–SiC catalyst consistently outperformed the undecorated SiC support.
- **Observations and Trends:** The incorporation of TiO₂ nanoparticles into the SiC support significantly enhances CO conversion, attributed to improved cobalt dispersion and increased active surface area.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 230 °C, CH₄ selectivity was 5.4% for 10Co/SiC and 5.9% for 10Co/TiO₂–SiC. At lower temperatures (215–225 °C), values ranged from 2.9–4.5% (SiC) and 3.2–4.5% (TiO₂–SiC).
- **Observations and Trends:** Both catalysts exhibited low methane selectivity, with a slight increase at higher temperatures and marginally higher values for the TiO₂–SiC catalyst.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 230 °C, C₅⁺ selectivity was 91.6% for 10Co/SiC and 91.7% for 10Co/TiO₂–SiC. At lower temperatures, both catalysts maintained high selectivity (93–95%).
- **Observations and Trends:** High C₅⁺ selectivity is maintained across all conditions, reflecting a strong preference for long-chain hydrocarbon formation. The TiO₂–SiC catalyst shows slightly higher selectivity at lower temperatures.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination, influencing product distribution. It is typically derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, with values closer to 1 favoring longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** Both catalysts exhibited an α factor of 0.92, consistent with the observed high C₅⁺ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high α value underscores the catalysts’ efficiency in promoting long-chain hydrocarbon synthesis.

#### 6.2.5 Cobalt Time Yield (CoTY)
- **Description:** CoTY measures the molar rate of CO conversion per gram of cobalt per second:
  \[
  \text{CoTY} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted}}{\text{g Co} \cdot \text{s}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 230 °C, CoTY was 5.3 × 10⁻⁵ molCO gCo⁻¹ s⁻¹ for 10Co/SiC and 7.5 × 10⁻⁵ molCO gCo⁻¹ s⁻¹ for 10Co/TiO₂–SiC.
- **Observations and Trends:** TiO₂ decoration leads to a ~40% increase in CoTY, directly linked to improved cobalt dispersion and smaller particle size.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over extended time-on-stream. Deactivation rate is inferred from the change in conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time (h)}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Both catalysts demonstrated negligible deactivation over 100 h on-stream, with stable CO conversion and selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SiC-based supports, especially with TiO₂ decoration, provide excellent thermal management and structural stability, minimizing deactivation due to sintering or oxidation.

---
**Summary:**  
The TiO₂–decorated SiC-supported cobalt catalyst exhibits superior CO conversion, high C₅⁺ selectivity, elevated chain growth probability, and outstanding stability under industrially relevant Fischer–Tropsch conditions. These enhancements are directly attributed to improved cobalt dispersion and optimized particle size distribution enabled by TiO₂ modification.
