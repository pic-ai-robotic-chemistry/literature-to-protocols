## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fe–Mn Spinel Ferrite Microspheres are synthesized via a one‑step solvothermal method that emphasizes reproducibility and uniformity. This method is reported as simpler than traditional co‑precipitation techniques and offers direct control over Mn doping levels (Fe‑0Mn, Fe‑2Mn, Fe‑13Mn, and Fe‑31Mn).

#### • One‑Step Solvothermal Synthesis Procedure

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Reagents: Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O and MnCl₂·4H₂O (for the desired Mn level) in ethylene glycol, which functions as both solvent and reducing agent. The mixture is prepared with a total ion concentration of 0.1 M.  
   - pH Stabilization: Add sodium acetate to provide a slightly alkaline environment. This not only aids in electrostatic stabilization—preventing agglomeration—but also promotes uniform nucleation as evidenced by the quick formation of a light precipitate.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - Although direct “Precipitation–Deposition” is not separately isolated, the rapid formation of intermediate nuclei is enhanced by the controlled addition of sodium acetate. This stage, occurring during vigorous stirring for 1 hour, is critical to ensure the formation of monodisperse microspheres.

3. **Solvothermal Treatment**  
   - The well‑stirred precursor solution is sealed in a Teflon‑lined stainless‑steel autoclave.  
   - Reaction Conditions: The autoclave is heated to 473 K and maintained at this temperature for 8 hours, during which the formation of the spinel-like MnxFe₃₋ₓO₄ structure is achieved.  
   - Natural Cooling: Post‑reaction, the autoclave is allowed to cool naturally, a step that minimizes thermal shock and promotes structural uniformity.

4. **Post‑processing—Washing and Drying**  
   - The resulting product is washed sequentially with ethanol (to remove organic residues) and water (to eliminate inorganic impurities).  
   - Magnetic Collection: The microspheres are separated magnetically, indicative of successful conversion.  
   - Finally, the product is dried and stored under vacuum to maintain catalyst stability.

### Comparative Analysis  
– Advantages: Simplified one‑step process ensuring uniform particle size, high reproducibility, and potential scalability.  
– Disadvantages: Limited control over intermediate reaction parameters compared to multi‑step techniques, which might restrict fine-tuning of particle morphology in larger batches.

This detailed approach, validated by a series of QA insights, ensures that every parameter—chemical concentrations, pH control, reaction conditions, and equipment specificity—is explicitly defined for reproducibility.

## 4. Characterization Methods

- **Operational Description**:  
  XRD is performed to determine the crystalline structure, calculate grain size via Scherrer’s Equation, and examine lattice parameter expansion due to Mn doping. In situ measurements capture structural changes during reduction.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with an Anton Paar XRK‑900 reaction chamber.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Measurements are made using Cu‐Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The fresh catalyst is scanned in a 10–80° 2θ range with a step of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 0.24°/min; for spent catalysts, a rate of 3°/min is used. In situ analysis involves heating the sample in 5% H₂/Ar from room temperature to 623 K at 5 K/min and holding it for 5 hours.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Prior to measurement, the sample is outgassed at 463 K and 1 mmHg for 6 hours.
- **Key Notes**:  
  Proper mounting in the reaction chamber ensures accurate diffraction patterns.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a crucial pre-reaction treatment designed to convert the as-synthesized Fe–Mn spinel ferrite microspheres into an active state suitable for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The activation step involves a controlled reduction environment that ensures the formation of active metallic and carbide phases while maintaining optimal metal dispersion.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential to achieve the complete reduction of iron oxide precursors to metallic iron, setting the stage for subsequent carbide formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced at a precise temperature of 623 K in a stainless-steel continuous fixed-bed micro-reactor.
- **Observations:** This temperature ensures that the precursors are effectively converted without inducing excessive sintering, thereby preserving the catalyst’s high surface area.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The activation pressure is managed to stabilize the catalyst structure and prepare it for the Fischer–Tropsch environment.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Immediately following the reduction, the system is pressurized to 2.0 MPa using syngas with a H₂/CO ratio of 1.
- **Observations:** Although the pressure is mainly applied during the pre-reaction phase, its consistent use across catalyst formulations contributes to standardized reaction conditions.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation dictates the reduction kinetics and influences the phase evolution of the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A high-purity hydrogen atmosphere is used, with a controlled flow rate of 30 mL/min during the reduction process.
- **Observations:** The use of pure H₂ is critical to achieve effective reduction; its controlled flow minimizes the risk of over-reduction and helps suppress undesired carburization.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation ensures adequate exposure of catalyst particles to the reducing environment, promoting uniform transformation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction treatment is maintained for 5 hours.
- **Observations:** This specific duration has been optimized to enhance metal dispersion and facilitate the formation of active phases, while avoiding significant agglomeration or sintering.

Overall, these precisely controlled activation parameters provide a reproducible and robust method for catalyst pre-treatment, ensuring that each catalyst—regardless of its Mn doping level—attains the optimal state required for high-performance Fischer–Tropsch-to-olefins synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was conducted at an exact temperature of 533 K. Temperature plays a critical role in activating syngas and determining the formation of active iron carbide phases. In this study, maintaining the reaction at 533 K ensured a balance between enhancing CO conversion and achieving an optimal iron time yield (FTY). Higher temperatures were observed to improve the overall reaction rate; however, they also necessitated careful control to avoid unwanted shifts in product selectivity.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The experiments were performed under a reactor pressure of 2.0 MPa. This elevated pressure increases the concentration of syngas within the reactor, which is essential for driving the formation of desired hydrocarbon products. The chosen pressure assisted in maintaining a favorable environment for CO activation while mitigating issues such as excessive carburization. An optimal pressure of 2.0 MPa was found to balance the reaction kinetics and product distribution, particularly benefitting the formation of lower olefins.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
A H₂/CO ratio of 1.0 was employed during the catalytic tests. This stoichiometric balance is vital for directing the reaction pathway towards the generation of lower olefins (C₂–C₄) while suppressing excessive methane formation. The study highlights that slight variations in the H₂/CO ratio can significantly impact CO conversion and byproduct selectivity. Therefore, maintaining a constant ratio of 1.0 was imperative for reproducible and targeted performance outcomes.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) was set at 4000 h⁻¹. This parameter, defined as the volumetric flow rate of syngas per unit catalyst volume per hour, is crucial in controlling the contact time between the gaseous reactants and the catalyst. A GHSV of 4000 h⁻¹ was carefully selected to ensure sufficient exposure for the active sites while reducing the likelihood of catalyst deactivation through carbon deposition. Together, these parameters define a robust framework for reproducible and reliable Fischer–Tropsch synthesis evaluation using Fe–Mn spinel ferrite microspheres.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the Fe–Mn spinel ferrite microspheres were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman spectroscopy. XRD patterns were recorded with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 10–80°, with a step size of 0.02°. The Scherrer equation was applied to the (311) peak to determine crystallite size, and lattice parameters were refined using Rietveld analysis.
- **Findings:** XRD revealed that increasing Mn content led to a shift of diffraction peaks toward lower angles, indicating lattice expansion from 8.39 Å (Fe-0Mn) to 8.44 Å (Fe-31Mn). The formation of spinel MnxFe₃₋ₓO₄ was confirmed, with additional MnFe₂O₄ and MnO phases emerging at higher Mn loadings. Raman spectra supported these results, showing a main band at ~660 cm⁻¹ (A₁g mode of Fe₃O₄) for Fe-0Mn and Fe-2Mn, and new peaks at 339 cm⁻¹ (E_g) and 625 cm⁻¹ (A₁g) for MnFe₂O₄, as well as 598 cm⁻¹ (A₁g) for MnO in Fe-13Mn and Fe-31Mn.
- **Observations:** The expansion of the lattice and emergence of Mn-rich phases confirm successful Mn incorporation and structural modification, which are linked to enhanced catalyst stability and activity.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Dopant Distribution
- **Description:** The actual Mn content and elemental distribution were determined by ICP-MS and SEM-EDS mapping.
- **Findings:** ICP-MS and EDS confirmed Mn incorporation levels of approximately 2%, 13.3%, and 31.4% for Fe-2Mn, Fe-13Mn, and Fe-31Mn, respectively. EDS mapping showed uniform Fe and Mn distribution within the microspheres.
- **Observations:** The dopant ratios achieved were consistently lower than the theoretical maximum, reflecting the limits of the solvothermal process, but sufficient for phase modification.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area, Porosity, and Morphology
- **Description:** BET analysis (N₂ adsorption at 77 K) was used to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size. SEM provided morphological assessment and particle size distribution.
- **Findings:** BET surface area increased markedly with Mn doping, from 43 m²/g (Fe-0Mn) to 213 m²/g (Fe-31Mn), while mean grain size decreased from 390 nm to 180 nm. SEM images revealed uniform, monodisperse microspheres with reduced aggregation at higher Mn content.
- **Observations:** The increased surface area and improved morphology are attributed to Mn-induced reduction in surface energy and the stabilizing effect of sodium acetate during synthesis, which collectively enhance catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility and Phase Evolution
- **Description:** H₂-TPR profiles assessed the reducibility of Fe species, while ⁵⁷Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy and XPS characterized phase evolution and surface chemical states after reaction.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR showed a decrease in reduction degree with increasing Mn, from nearly 100% (Fe-0Mn, Fe-2Mn) to 46.9% (Fe-31Mn), indicating stabilization of MnxFe₁₋ₓO. Mössbauer spectra of spent Fe-13Mn revealed a high ε-Fe₂C content (33%, ε-Fe₂C/χ-Fe₅C₂ ratio 0.80). XPS detected surface α-Fe₂O₃ and subsurface ν-Fe₅C₂, with Mn-doped samples exhibiting thicker carbon layers post-reaction.
- **Observations:** Mn doping suppresses excessive Fe reduction and promotes the formation of active iron carbide phases, particularly ε-Fe₂C, which correlates with improved selectivity for lower olefins and catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Deposition and Surface Stability
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy and XPS were used to evaluate carbon deposition and surface stability after reaction.
- **Findings:** Spent catalysts, especially Fe-13Mn, exhibited strong D and G bands in Raman spectra, indicating significant graphitic and disordered carbon deposition. XPS showed that thick carbon layers covered the catalyst surface, with iron carbide signals only detectable after sputtering.
- **Observations:** The enhanced carbon deposition with Mn addition is associated with increased CO dissociation and contributes to surface passivation, which can moderate deactivation and maintain catalyst performance over time.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  X_{\text{CO}}(\%) = \frac{Q_{\text{out}} \sum \nu_{i} C_{i}}{Q_{\text{in}} \nu_{\text{CO}}} \times 100\%
  \]
  where \( Q_{\text{in}} \) and \( Q_{\text{out}} \) are the inlet and outlet flow rates, \( \nu_{i} \) is the volume fraction of product \( i \), \( C_{i} \) is its carbon number, and \( \nu_{\text{CO}} \) is the CO concentration in the feed.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch-to-olefins (FTO) conditions (533 K, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1, GHSV = 4000 h⁻¹), the CO conversion values for the catalysts are:
  - Fe-0Mn: 6.5%
  - Fe-13Mn: 7.1%
  - Fe-31Mn: 3.3%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion exhibits a volcano-type trend with Mn content, peaking at moderate doping (Fe-13Mn). Excessive Mn (Fe-31Mn) reduces conversion, attributed to suppressed Fe reduction and diminished active carbide formation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  S_{\text{CO}_2}(\%) = \frac{Q_{\text{out}} \nu_{\text{CO}_2} C_{\text{CO}_2}}{Q_{\text{in}} \nu_{\text{CO}} X_{\text{CO}}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Reported CO₂ selectivities are:
  - Fe-0Mn: 16.7%
  - Fe-13Mn: 25.2%
  - Fe-31Mn: 32.7% (at H₂/CO = 0.5)
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity increases with Mn content, indicating enhanced water–gas shift activity at higher Mn levels. However, excessive Mn can lead to over-oxidation and reduced hydrocarbon yield.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, determined by:
  \[
  S_{\text{CH}_4}(\%) = \frac{Q_{\text{out}} \nu_{\text{CH}_4} C_{\text{CH}_4}}{Q_{\text{in}} \nu_{\text{CO}} X_{\text{CO}}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe-13Mn, CH₄ selectivity is 17.0%. Increasing Mn up to 13% suppresses methane formation, while further increase (Fe-31Mn) causes a slight rise.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal Mn doping minimizes methane selectivity, favoring olefin production.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbons:
  \[
  S_{C_3^+}(\%) = \frac{Q_{\text{out}} \sum_{i=3}^{n} \nu_{i} C_{i}}{Q_{\text{in}} \nu_{\text{CO}} X_{\text{CO}}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** As Mn content increases, selectivity to lower olefins (C₂–C₄) rises (Fe-13Mn: 54.4%), while C₃⁺ and methane selectivities are suppressed. At high Mn (Fe-31Mn), C₃⁺ selectivity slightly increases again.
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate Mn doping optimizes short-chain olefin selectivity, while excessive Mn reverses this trend.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability (Deactivation Rate)
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by the rate of CO conversion decline over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{X_{\text{CO,initial}} - X_{\text{CO,final}}}{\text{Time}} \quad (\%\ \text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe-13Mn, the deactivation rate is 0.04% per hour over 20 hours on stream.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low deactivation rate indicates excellent stability, attributed to lattice expansion and stabilization of MnxFe₁₋ₓO phases by Mn doping.

#### 6.2.6 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY quantifies the moles of CO converted per second per gram of iron:
  \[
  \text{FTY} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted}}{\text{g Fe} \cdot \text{s}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe-13Mn achieves the highest FTY at 16.9 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹, compared to Fe-0Mn and Fe-31Mn (values not specified but lower).
- **Observations and Trends:** FTY follows the same volcano trend as CO conversion, peaking at moderate Mn content.

---

**Summary:**  
Moderate Mn doping (Fe-13Mn) in spinel ferrite microspheres maximizes CO conversion, FTY, and lower olefin selectivity while minimizing methane formation and maintaining excellent catalyst stability. Excessive Mn content suppresses active phase formation and reduces overall performance.
