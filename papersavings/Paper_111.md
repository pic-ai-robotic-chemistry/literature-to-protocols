## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalysts (CuCoLa₂O₃, CuCoAl₂O₃, and CuCoZrO₂) are synthesized using a pH‑coprecipitation method that ensures reproducibility and high dispersion of the metallic phases. This section details the experimental sequence and associated conditions extracted directly from the article.

#### Procedure for pH‑Coprecipitation Method

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Dissolve cobalt nitrate, copper nitrate, and the appropriate promoter salt (e.g., La(NO₃)₃·6H₂O for CuCoLa₂O₃) together in 100 mL of deionized water.  
   - Conduct the dissolution under magnetic stirring at 80 °C.  
   - Maintain the Cu:Co:promoter molar ratio at 2:2:1.  
   
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Gradually titrate the solution with ammonia carbonate to adjust and maintain a target pH of 7.00 ± 0.2.  
   - This step, directly following the precursor mixing, ensures uniform precipitation and deposition of the metal hydroxides.  
   - No specific instrument is mentioned for pH measurement; magnetic stirring assists uniform distribution.

3. • Aging  
   - Allow the precipitated solution to age at room temperature for 6 hours.  
   - This period facilitates complete precipitation and initial formation of the catalyst precursor.

4. • Drying  
   - Recover the solid via washing and filtration to remove residual ions and impurities.  
   - Dry the recovered precipitate at 90 °C for 24 hours, an essential step for moisture removal and precursor stabilization.

5. • Calcination  
   - Calcine the dried precipitate at 500 °C for 3 hours under air flow.  
   - This post‑treatment transforms the precursor into the active catalyst with the formation of distinct oxide phases.

### Comparative Analysis

• Advantages:  
   - The method’s systematic control of pH and temperature yields highly dispersed metal clusters critical for catalytic performance.  
   - It is scalable, with defined temperatures, durations, and volumes ensuring reproducibility.

• Disadvantages:  
   - While not explicitly mentioned, precise control of titration and aging may require careful monitoring to avoid any deviation in catalyst structure.

This detailed procedure, including all critical parameters and equipment used, allows another researcher to replicate the synthesis accurately and compare the performance of the different promoter-stabilized catalysts.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: CuCoLa₂O₃

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O, 10.0 g Cu(NO₃)₂·3H₂O, and 5.0 g La(NO₃)₃·6H₂O dissolved in 100 mL deionized water.
- **Conditions**: The solution is maintained at 80 °C under continuous magnetic stirring.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve all metal nitrates together to obtain a homogeneous solution, ensuring the molar ratio of Cu:Co:La is 2:2:1.
  - Initiate the coprecipitation process by titrating ammonia carbonate dropwise to adjust the pH to 7.00 ± 0.2.
  - Verify that all salts are completely dissolved and the pH remains stable during the reaction.
- **Key Steps**: Precise dissolution, strict pH control (7.00 ± 0.2), and consistent temperature at 80 °C are crucial for reproducibility.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: The primary solution from Step 1.
- **Conditions**: The reaction suspension is maintained at 80 °C during titration and then aged at room temperature.
- **Process**:
  - After completing the pH adjustment, allow the solution to age for 6 hours at room temperature. This aging facilitates the maturation of the precipitate into well-defined metal hydroxides.
- **Key Steps**: Continuous stirring and aging ensure efficient aggregation of the hydroxides.

##### Step [3]: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Recover the formed precipitate by filtration.
  - Wash the solid thoroughly with deionized water to remove residual salts and impurities, ensuring purity for the subsequent stages.
- **Key Steps**: Adequate washing minimizes contamination and ensures a clean precursor for drying.

##### Step [4]: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the washed precipitate at 90 °C for 24 hours in an oven to remove residual moisture.
- **Calcination**:
  - Calcine the dried sample at 500 °C for 3 hours under an air flow atmosphere. Ensure a controlled ramp rate to prevent thermal shock and to achieve complete conversion into the oxide form.
- **Key Steps**: Full moisture removal before calcination and strict thermal control during calcination are essential for obtaining the desired catalyst structure.

##### Step [5]: Activation (if applicable)
- For CuCoLa₂O₃, no additional activation is required prior to catalytic testing, though this step may be applied for other catalyst systems if needed.

This structured workflow ensures that every synthesis stage—from precursor preparation, coprecipitation, aging, purification, drying, to calcination—is clearly defined and reproducible. Similar protocols are followed for CuCoAl₂O₃ and CuCoZrO₂ to enable a systematic comparison of catalyst performance based on promoter effects.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to monitor catalyst reduction by tracking phase transformations from oxides (e.g., CuO, Co₃O₄) to metallic phases. Catalyst samples were mounted in an Anton Paar XRK900 reactor chamber, where a controlled 4% H₂/He atmosphere was introduced during analysis.
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements were performed using a PANalytical X'Pert Pro MPD diffractometer with Cu K radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm).
- **Characterization Conditions**: The analysis used a scanning range of 15° to 70° with a fine step size of 0.0167°.
- **Test Parameters**: Although voltage and current details were not specified, the high-resolution step size allowed detection of subtle phase transitions.
- **Key Notes**: Pre-reduction in the reactor chamber ensured that phase changes were captured in situ, which is critical for distinguishing between crystalline and reduced phases.

### 4.2 H₂‑Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR was conducted to evaluate the reduction behavior of the catalysts. A 50 mg calcined sample was first pretreated in helium at 150 °C for 30 minutes to remove moisture, then exposed to a reducing atmosphere.
- **Instruments and Models**: The analysis was performed on an Altamira AMI‑200 system with a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: A 10% H₂/Ar gas mixture flowed at 50 sccm, while the temperature was ramped from 150 °C (pretreatment) to 550 °C at 5 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: The reaction profile allowed determination of reduction onset (around 190 °C for CuCoLa₂O₃) and complete reduction by 300 °C.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and controlled ramping are essential to ensure reproducible reduction kinetics.

### 4.3 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: BET analysis quantified the surface area via N₂ adsorption at −196 °C, following sample pretreatment.
- **Instruments and Models**: An Altamira AMI‑200 system configured for BET measurements was used.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were pretreated in helium at 150 °C for 30 minutes.
- **Test Parameters**: N₂ adsorption isotherms were recorded, yielding surface areas of 94.1 m²/g (CuCoAl₂O₃), 88.8 m²/g (CuCoZrO₂), and 49.7 m²/g (CuCoLa₂O₃).
- **Key Notes**: Pre-adsorption desorption eliminates interference from residual moisture.

### 4.4 X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: XPS analyzed the catalyst surface composition, confirming the Cu/Co ratio and chemical states.
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements were performed using a Kratos AXIS 165 spectrometer with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV) under ultra-high vacuum (10⁻⁸–10⁻⁹ Torr).
- **Test Parameters**: Survey scans at 160 eV pass energy and high-resolution scans at 40 eV were employed.
- **Key Notes**: Sample alignment with survey spectra is crucial for accurate elemental quantification.

### 4.5 Temperature‑Programmed Oxidation (TPO)
- **Operational Description**: TPO was used to assess carbon deposition on spent catalysts post-reaction.
- **Characterization Conditions**: The catalyst was gradually heated from 200 °C upward under an oxidizing gas atmosphere.
- **Key Notes**: Comparative analysis revealed that CuCoZrO₂ accumulated more polymeric carbon, potentially impacting its catalytic performance.

### 4.6 In‑situ XRD
- **Operational Description**: In‑situ XRD monitored the phase evolution under a hydrogen atmosphere in real time.
- **Key Notes**: Although details on ramp rates or gas compositions were limited, this technique provided critical insights into phase transformations during reduction.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis involves a carefully controlled pre-reaction reduction step, distinct from milder activation treatments used for characterization. This procedure is pivotal in converting the oxidic precursor into an active metallic phase while preserving catalyst integrity.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is the key parameter driving the reduction of metal oxides to their active metallic state.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are reduced at 400 °C for 3 hours in a tube furnace integrated within the Altamira AMI-200R-HP system.
- **Observations:** This temperature ensures complete reduction of cobalt and copper oxides, effectively activating the catalyst without causing detrimental sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure is crucial for effective gas-solid interactions during the reduction and subsequent reaction stages.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Following the reduction step, the reactor is cooled and pressurized to 30 bar before introducing the reaction gas mixture.
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure promotes optimal catalyst activation under simulated reaction conditions, supporting consistent performance during Fischer–Tropsch testing.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation governs the extent and efficiency of the reduction process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Initially, a reducing mixture of H₂ and He is used for catalyst reduction at 400 °C. After reduction, the gas is switched to a simulated syngas composed of H₂ and CO at a 2:1 ratio.
- **Observations:** The use of a hydrogen-rich environment ensures that oxide phases are effectively converted to metallic active sites while the subsequent syngas exposure aids in establishing the appropriate reaction conditions.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity controls the flow rate of gases relative to the catalyst mass, affecting gas–catalyst interaction during both activation and reaction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The simulated syngas is introduced at a space velocity of 36,000 scc/g_cat/h.
- **Observations:** This precise control of the gas flow rate assists in maintaining a stable and uniform reduction environment, thereby enhancing reproducibility and catalyst performance.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Activation time ensures sufficient exposure to the reduction conditions for complete conversion of the oxide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts undergo a 3-hour reduction at 400 °C.
- **Observations:** This duration is found to be optimal, providing complete reduction without evidence of adverse effects such as over-reduction or sintering.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was conducted at a controlled temperature of 250 °C. This temperature is critical for activating syngas and achieving the desired balance between hydrocarbon chain growth and oxygenate formation. Operating at 250 °C ensures that catalyst deactivation is minimized while maintaining stable reaction kinetics. Previous observations have shown that temperatures above the reduction onset (around 185 °C) promote the full reduction of CuO and Co₃O₄ phases, which is essential for catalyst activity.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The experiment was performed under a total pressure of 30 bar. Elevated pressure significantly enhances syngas concentration, thereby improving the overall catalytic activity and selectively favoring the formation of higher alcohols. This condition is especially important as it increases the contact between reactants and the active metal sites in the catalyst, contributing to a more efficient conversion process.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
A fixed H₂/CO ratio of 2 was employed throughout the catalytic evaluation. This stoichiometric balance plays a dual role: it promotes the formation of higher alcohols, such as ethanol and methanol, while mitigating the undesired production of methane. Maintaining the ratio at 2 helps in achieving an optimal selectivity towards the target oxygenates, ensuring that the catalyst performs consistently under differential conversion conditions (<3% CO conversion).

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Gas Hourly Space Velocity (GHSV)  
The reactor was operated at a GHSV of 36,000 scc/g_cat/h, which defines the volume of syngas passing per gram of catalyst per hour. This high space velocity minimizes residence time, thereby reducing side reactions and enabling precise control over product distribution. The plug-flow reactor system, equipped with state-of-the-art instrumentation, allows for constant monitoring of reaction products, ensuring reproducibility and direct extraction of experimental data for further analysis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and their transformations were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD), employing a PANalytical X'Pert Pro MPD diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm) in an Anton Paar XRK900 reactor chamber. In situ XRD enabled monitoring of phase changes during reduction under 4% H₂/He flow.
- **Findings:** Fresh, calcined catalysts exhibited distinct peaks for CuO and Co₃O₄. Upon reduction, these peaks diminished, and new peaks at 43.2° and 50.3° 2θ appeared, corresponding to metallic phases. For CuCoAl₂O₃, a CoO phase was transiently observed at 300 °C, disappearing by 400 °C, indicating complete reduction to metallic cobalt.
- **Observations:** The phase evolution confirms that the synthesis and reduction protocols yield well-defined oxide and metallic phases, with the promoter oxide influencing the reduction pathway and phase stability.

#### 6.1.2 Reducibility (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) assessed the reducibility of copper and cobalt oxides, using an Altamira AMI-200 system with a 10% H₂/Ar mixture and a ramp rate of 5 °C/min.
- **Findings:** All catalysts began reducing at ~185 °C, with reduction essentially complete by 300 °C. CuCoLa₂O₃ showed copper reduction onset at ~190 °C and a sharp asymmetric peak at 215 °C, attributed to the concurrent reduction of Co₃O₄ to CoO and CoO to Co⁰. Unlike CuCoAl₂O₃ and CuCoZrO₂, CuCoLa₂O₃ lacked a broad shoulder above 250 °C, indicating more facile and complete reduction.
- **Observations:** The La₂O₃ promoter enhances hydrogen spillover and metal dispersion, facilitating more efficient reduction, as reflected in the higher reduction degree (97.5%) for CuCoLa₂O₃ compared to CuCoAl₂O₃ (92.7%).

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity (BET)
- **Description:** Surface area was measured by N₂ adsorption at −196 °C after pretreatment in He at 150 °C, using an Altamira AMI-200 system.
- **Findings:** BET surface areas were 94.1 m²/g for CuCoAl₂O₃, 88.8 m²/g for CuCoZrO₂, and 49.7 m²/g for CuCoLa₂O₃.
- **Observations:** The choice of promoter oxide significantly impacts surface area, with La₂O₃ leading to lower values, likely due to particle growth or sintering during calcination. Despite its lower surface area, CuCoLa₂O₃ demonstrated superior selectivity to higher alcohols, indicating that surface chemistry, rather than area alone, governs catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Surface States (XPS, ICP-OES)
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) were used to determine surface and bulk elemental compositions and oxidation states.
- **Findings:** XPS revealed Co 2p₃/₂ peaks at ~779.3 eV (Co₃O₄) and Cu 2p₃/₂ at ~933.6 eV (CuO), with a shake-up satellite at 942 eV. Surface Cu/Co atomic ratios closely matched bulk values from ICP-OES, confirming uniform composition.
- **Observations:** The synthesis method ensured consistent metal distribution, and the presence of Co₃O₄ and CuO on the surface after calcination was confirmed across all samples.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Deposition and Carburization (TPO, XPS)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed oxidation (TPO) and post-reaction XPS characterized carbon species and carburization on spent catalysts.
- **Findings:** TPO showed that CuCoZrO₂ accumulated the most polymeric carbon, with significant peaks at 200–300 °C, while CuCoLa₂O₃ and CuCoAl₂O₃ had lower carbon deposition. For CuCoLa₂O₃, TPO and XPS detected cobalt carbide (Co₂C) and graphitic carbon after reaction.
- **Observations:** High carbon deposition on CuCoZrO₂ correlates with lower catalytic activity, while the formation of cobalt carbide and graphitic carbon on CuCoLa₂O₃ is indicative of surface reconstruction during syngas exposure, supporting enhanced oxygenate selectivity.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 250 °C, 30 bar, GHSV 36,000 scc/g_cat/h, and H₂/CO = 2, the steady-state CO conversion values after 9 hours were:
  - CuCoAl₂O₃: 1.93%
  - CuCoLa₂O₃: 0.76%
  - CuCoZrO₂: 0.58%
- **Observations and Trends:** The Al₂O₃-promoted catalyst exhibited the highest CO conversion, followed by La₂O₃ and ZrO₂. The lower conversion for CuCoLa₂O₃ is notable despite its superior oxygenate selectivity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of total oxidation:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 9 hours:
  - CuCoAl₂O₃: 23.25%
  - CuCoLa₂O₃: 18.03%
  - CuCoZrO₂: 22.67%
- **Observations and Trends:** CuCoLa₂O₃ showed the lowest CO₂ selectivity, suggesting a more efficient use of CO for value-added products.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** Methane selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding CH₄:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 9 hours:
  - CuCoAl₂O₃: 35.63%
  - CuCoLa₂O₃: 31.37%
  - CuCoZrO₂: 28.14%
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane selectivity was highest for CuCoAl₂O₃ and lowest for CuCoZrO₂. For CuCoLa₂O₃, methane selectivity increased with time-on-stream, paralleling the rise in alcohol selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–C₆ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity to C₂–C₆ hydrocarbons reflects the fraction of converted CO forming longer-chain hydrocarbons:
  \[
  \text{C}_2\text{–C}_6 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\sum F_{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 9 hours:
  - CuCoAl₂O₃: 26.06%
  - CuCoLa₂O₃: 14.45%
  - CuCoZrO₂: 29.63%
- **Observations and Trends:** CuCoZrO₂ and CuCoAl₂O₃ favored higher hydrocarbon formation, while CuCoLa₂O₃ suppressed hydrocarbon selectivity in favor of oxygenates.

#### 6.2.5 Alcohol Selectivity
- **Description:** Alcohol selectivity is the percentage of converted CO forming C₁–C₆ alcohols.
- **Experimental Data:** After 9 hours:
  - CuCoAl₂O₃: 13.1% (methanol: 5.0%, ethanol: 5.7%)
  - CuCoLa₂O₃: 34.9% (methanol: 16.9%, ethanol: 10.5%)
  - CuCoZrO₂: 17.8% (methanol: 4.4%, ethanol: 7.3%)
- **Observations and Trends:** CuCoLa₂O₃ demonstrated the highest alcohol selectivity, with methanol and ethanol selectivity increasing over time, attributed to surface reconstruction and enhanced basicity.

#### 6.2.6 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, indicates the probability of chain propagation:
  \[
  \alpha = \frac{\text{moles of C}_{n+1}}{\text{moles of C}_n}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the product distributions suggest:
  - CuCoAl₂O₃ and CuCoZrO₂: higher α (favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons)
  - CuCoLa₂O₃: lower α (favoring alcohols and short-chain products)
- **Observations and Trends:** The lower α for CuCoLa₂O₃ aligns with its suppressed hydrocarbon selectivity and enhanced oxygenate formation.

#### 6.2.7 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** All catalysts exhibited some deactivation over 9 hours. CuCoLa₂O₃ showed a gradual decrease in CO conversion but a concurrent increase in alcohol selectivity, indicating beneficial surface reconstruction. CuCoAl₂O₃ and CuCoZrO₂ reached steady-state more rapidly with less change in selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The unique time-dependent behavior of CuCoLa₂O₃ highlights the impact of La₂O₃ on catalyst stability and product distribution.

---
**Summary:**  
Catalyst performance is strongly influenced by promoter type. CuCoAl₂O₃ achieves the highest CO conversion and methane selectivity, while CuCoLa₂O₃ excels in alcohol selectivity, particularly for methanol and ethanol, due to surface reconstruction and increased basicity. CuCoZrO₂ favors higher hydrocarbon formation but suffers from greater carbon deposition. These findings underscore the critical role of promoter selection in tuning activity, selectivity, and stability for higher alcohol synthesis from syngas.
