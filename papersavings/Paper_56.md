## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the combined co‑precipitation and impregnation approach used for preparing Fe–Mn–Cu–SiO₂ catalysts. The method was designed to ensure uniform dispersion of Fe and Mn species on a SiO₂ support, followed by controlled incorporation of Cu promoter via impregnation.

#### Co‑precipitation and Precipitation–Deposition
1. • The process begins with the mixing of aqueous solutions containing Fe(NO₃)₃ and Mn(NO₃)₂ with silica gel.  
   • Chemical reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ and Mn(NO₃)₂; support: silica gel.  
   • Reaction medium: Aqueous system with HNO₃/NH₄OH, maintaining pH at ~9.0 (± 0.2) by continuous addition of NH₄OH solution.  
   • Reaction conditions: Precipitation performed at 70 °C.
2. • Immediately following precursor mixing, a precipitation–deposition step is integrated.  
   • This ensures that the metal precursors are deposited uniformly onto the silica support.  
   • The resulting slurry is aged under vigorous stirring for 6 h to facilitate complete precipitate formation and stabilization.

#### Drying, Grinding, and Impregnation
3. • After aging, the slurry is filtered and dried at 90 °C for 4 h.  
   • Equipment: Standard filtration setup and drying oven.
4. • The dried material is then ground to a specification of 120 mesh to ensure uniform particle size, which facilitates subsequent processing.
5. • The catalyst is subsequently impregnated with an aqueous Cu(NO₃)₂ solution to incorporate the Cu promoter at loadings of 0, 0.5, 1.5, and 3.0 wt%.

#### Post‑impregnation Treatments
6. • Post impregnation, a second drying stage is performed at 90 °C for 4 h to remove residual moisture from the copper precursor.
7. • Finally, the catalyst undergoes calcination in static air at 550 °C for 6 h.  
   • This calcination step is critical to form the desired active phases and improve the interaction between metal oxides and the SiO₂ support.

### Comparative Analysis of Synthesis Techniques
• The co‑precipitation method advantages include uniform metal distribution and simplicity in maintaining the pH.  
• In contrast, the impregnation step allows precise control over the Cu loading, though it adds additional drying and calcination steps.  
• The integration of these methods optimizes both the dispersion of active sites and the overall catalyst performance for effective Fischer–Tropsch synthesis with low CO₂ emissions.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe–Mn–Cu–SiO₂ Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃, Mn(NO₃)₂, and silica gel (in a weight ratio of 100:10:12). HNO₃ and NH₄OH are used to adjust and stabilize the pH.
- **Conditions**: The co-precipitation is performed at 70 °C with continuous stirring. The pH is maintained at 9.0 ± 0.2 by the controlled, dropwise addition of NH₄OH.
- **Process**:
  - Pre-heat a precipitation vessel to 70 °C and introduce silica gel.
  - Add aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ and Mn(NO₃)₂ simultaneously.
  - Carefully add NH₄OH while monitoring the pH to achieve a stable value of 9.0 ± 0.2.
  - Allow the slurry to age under vigorous stirring for 6 hours, ensuring complete precipitation of metal hydroxides.
- **Key Steps**: Continuous pH monitoring and constant temperature control are essential for reproducible precursor formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly & Purification
- **Process**:
  - Filter the aged slurry to separate the precipitate.
  - Wash the collected solid (optionally using 5 wt% nitric acid to remove impurities).
  - Dry the filtered precipitate at 90 °C for 4 hours.
  - Grind the dried material to a 120 mesh powder, ensuring increased surface area for subsequent steps.
- **Key Steps**: Proper filtration and thorough drying are critical for high-quality catalyst assembly.

##### Step 3: Copper Impregnation
- **Reagents**: Aqueous Cu(NO₃)₂ solution, added in amounts corresponding to 0, 0.5, 1.5, and 3.0 wt% Cu.
- **Conditions & Process**:
  - Impregnate the ground precursor uniformly with the Cu solution.
  - Dry the impregnated mixture again at 90 °C for 4 hours to remove excess moisture.
- **Key Steps**: Homogeneous copper dispersion is crucial for enhancing reduction properties of the final catalyst.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Conditions**: Calcination is carried out under static air at 550 °C for 6 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Gradually heat the dried, Cu-impregnated precursor to 550 °C at a controlled ramp rate.
  - Maintain the final temperature for 6 hours to complete the formation of active Fe–Mn–Cu sites.
- **Key Steps**: Strict control of the heating profile and atmosphere ensures catalyst stability and reproducibility.

This comprehensive, step-by-step synthesis protocol—from raw precursor preparation through catalyst assembly, copper impregnation, and calcination—provides a clear and reproducible method for generating effective Fe–Mn–Cu–SiO₂ catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch applications.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to confirm the presence of the hematite (α‑Fe₂O₃) phase, verify iron oxide dispersion, and check the integration of Mn and Cu into the hematite lattice.
- **Instruments and Models**: Rigaku Smart‑Lab X‑ray diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Utilizes Cu‑Kα₁ radiation (λ = 0.15406 Å) with an operational voltage of 40 kV and current of 40 mA. The 2θ scanning range spans from 10° to 90° with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 4°/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Samples are freshly calcinated to ensure proper phase manifestation.
- **Key Notes**: XRD is highly effective for crystalline phase determination though its sensitivity to amorphous material is limited.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: This method assesses the reduction behavior of the catalyst by identifying two key temperature regions corresponding to different oxide reducibilities.
- **Instruments and Models**: Catalyst (100 mg) is loaded in a quartz sample tube.
- **Characterization Conditions**: A gas mixture of 5% H₂ in He flows at 50 mL/min. The temperature is ramped from 100°C to 900°C at a steady rate of 10°C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Region I (300–500°C) reflects the reduction of CuO, α‑Mn₂O₃, and α‑Fe₂O₃, while Region II (600–850°C) indicates further reduction toward metallic Fe. Increased Cu content shifts reduction peaks to lower temperatures.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and controlled temperature ramping are essential for reliable reduction profiling.

### 4.3 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: BET analysis measures the surface area, average pore size, and total pore volume via N₂ adsorption/desorption.
- **Instruments and Models**: Quantachrome Quadrasorb EVO gas sorption analyzer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements are performed at −196°C. Each sample is degassed at 250°C under vacuum for 10 hours.
- **Key Notes**: The incorporation of SiO₂ significantly enhances the surface area due to well‐dispersed Fe–Mn–Cu crystallites.

### 4.4 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)
- **Operational Description**: SEM is used to examine the granule morphology and surface dispersion of the freshly calcinated catalyst.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Quanta FEG MK2.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Conducted under standard imaging conditions.
- **Key Notes**: Provides critical insights into particle shape and aggregation.

### 4.5 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)
- **Operational Description**: TEM coupled with EDS mapping offers detailed visualization of the catalyst’s microstructure and elemental distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI 80‑300 Titan‑S.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Specific operating voltages and magnifications were not detailed.
- **Key Notes**: Confirms fine dispersion of Fe–Mn–Cu crystallites and effective elemental integration.

### 4.6 In‑situ Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)
- **Operational Description**: In‑situ FTIR monitors CO and H₂ adsorption behavior on the catalyst surface through real‑time spectral collection.
- **Instruments and Models**: Nicolet iS50 FT‑IR spectrometer with a diffuse reflection environmental chamber.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are pretreated by mixing with KBr (10:1) and ground, then reduced at 20 bar while heating to 500°C at 10°C/min under H₂ for 2 hours, followed by treatment at 500°C under He for 30 minutes. Subsequent spectra are collected continuously with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ during a temperature ramp from 30°C to 300°C at 10°C/min, with a total gas flow rate of 10 mL/min and a H₂/CO ratio of 2.0.
- **Key Notes**: This method effectively elucidates adsorption dynamics, ensuring reproducible and precise monitoring of catalyst activation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the Fe–Mn–Cu–SiO₂ catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu-Kα₁ radiation (λ = 0.15406 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA, with a 2θ scanning range of 10°–90° and a scanning rate of 4°/min.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of freshly calcinated catalysts with varying Cu loadings revealed only the hematite phase (α-Fe₂O₃), with broad diffraction peaks at 2θ = 24.4°, 33.2°, 35.7°, 49.6°, and 54.6°. No distinct crystalline phases of Mn or Cu were detected, indicating their low concentration or incorporation into the hematite lattice.
- **Observations:** The broadness of the α-Fe₂O₃ peaks suggests well-dispersed iron oxide. The absence of crystalline Cu or Mn phases supports the high dispersion and possible solid solution formation, which is beneficial for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Reducibility and Redox Properties
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was used to assess the reducibility of the catalysts, with H₂-DTG analysis providing further confirmation.
- **Findings:** Two main reduction regions were observed: Region I (300–500 °C), corresponding to the reduction of CuO, α-Mn₂O₃, and α-Fe₂O₃ to their respective lower oxides or metals; and Region II (600–850 °C), representing further reduction to metallic Fe. The addition of Cu shifted both reduction peaks to lower temperatures, with higher Cu loadings resulting in more pronounced and merged low-temperature peaks.
- **Observations:** Cu facilitates the reduction of Fe and Mn oxides, lowering the activation temperature and increasing the reduction rate. This enhanced reducibility is critical for activating the catalyst under Fischer-Tropsch conditions.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ adsorption–desorption measurements (BET method) were performed to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution. Samples were degassed at 250 °C under vacuum for 10 hours prior to analysis.
- **Findings:** Catalysts supported on SiO₂ exhibited higher BET surface areas compared to unsupported analogues, attributed to the formation of small, well-dispersed crystallites. The average pore size remained in the mesoporous range, and although Cu impregnation slightly decreased surface area and pore volume, the overall textural properties were largely preserved.
- **Observations:** The mesoporous structure and high surface area facilitate reactant diffusion and provide abundant active sites, supporting efficient catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were used to examine particle morphology and elemental dispersion.
- **Findings:** SEM images showed irregularly shaped particles with faceted surfaces, composed of numerous small Fe–Mn–Cu crystallites. TEM and EDS mapping confirmed fine dispersion of Fe, Mn, and Cu at the nanoscale, with no Cu detected in unpromoted samples. After reaction, Cu-promoted catalysts retained their powder form, while the Cu-free sample exhibited severe aggregation.
- **Observations:** The addition of Cu inhibits particle aggregation during reaction, maintaining catalyst dispersion and structural integrity, which is essential for sustained activity.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** In-situ FTIR spectroscopy was employed to study CO and H₂ adsorption behavior under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** FTIR spectra revealed that CO adsorption bands on metallic Fe (2032 cm⁻¹ and 2014 cm⁻¹) diminished as CO₂ bands appeared, indicating transformation to iron carbide. The duration of CO adsorption was shorter for Cu-promoted samples, suggesting more efficient carbonization. Delayed CO adsorption in these samples also indicated higher initial H₂ adsorption, reflecting competitive adsorption between CO and H₂.
- **Observations:** Cu promotion enhances both the reduction and carbonization processes, facilitating H-assisted CO dissociative adsorption and rapid formation of the active iron carbide phase, which are key for high Fischer-Tropsch activity and selectivity toward light olefins.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{M_{\text{CO,in}} - M_{\text{CO,out}}}{M_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( M_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( M_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the moles of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (300 °C, 20 bar, H₂/CO = 2:1, 1500 h⁻¹, 80 h), the CO conversion for the Cu-free Fe–Mn–SiO₂ catalyst (Cu-0) is 74%. With Cu promotion, conversion increases markedly: Cu-0.5 and Cu-1.5 samples reach ~95%, and Cu-3.0 achieves 96.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Cu loading significantly enhances CO conversion, indicating improved catalyst reducibility and activity. The effect plateaus at higher Cu content, with the most pronounced improvement observed between Cu-0 and Cu-0.5.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{M_{\text{out}} \times P_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{M_{\text{CO,in}} - M_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( M_{\text{out}} \) is the total moles of effluent gas and \( P_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} \) is the mole fraction of CO₂ at the outlet.
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity decreases with Cu addition, reaching as low as 23.0% for Cu-3.0, compared to higher values typically observed in unpromoted systems.
- **Observations and Trends:** The suppression of CO₂ formation with Cu promotion is attributed to hindered water–gas shift activity and strong CO₂ adsorption, resulting in superior carbon utilization.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{W_{\text{CH}_4}}{\sum W_{\text{C}_n}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( W_{\text{CH}_4} \) is the weight of methane produced and \( \sum W_{\text{C}_n} \) is the total hydrocarbon product weight.
- **Experimental Data:** While exact CH₄ selectivity values are not explicitly listed, the study notes that decreased surface basicity from Cu addition can increase CH₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The trend suggests a moderate increase in CH₄ selectivity with higher Cu loading, consistent with the shift toward shorter hydrocarbon chains.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\sum W_{\text{C}_n \geq 3}}{\sum W_{\text{C}_n}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** As Cu loading increases, C₃⁺ selectivity decreases, with C₅⁺ selectivity dropping from 32.7% (Cu-0) to 9.5% (Cu-3.0). Light olefin (C₂–C₄) selectivity rises from 31.3% to 40.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Cu promotion shifts the product distribution toward light olefins and short-chain hydrocarbons, reducing the formation of heavier products.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon synthesis, with values closer to 1 favoring longer chains. It is derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** The α factor decreases from 0.77 (Cu-0) to 0.37 (Cu-3.0) as Cu content increases.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower α values with higher Cu loading correspond to increased selectivity for light olefins and methane, confirming the role of Cu in limiting chain growth.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate reflects the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Cu-promoted catalysts maintain stable CO conversion (~95%) over 80 h, while the Cu-free sample shows a decline after reaching its maximum.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Cu significantly enhances catalyst stability, as evidenced by sustained activity and preserved powder morphology after reaction.
