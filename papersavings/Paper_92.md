## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

• Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques  
The catalyst, a sodium modified Fe–Mn microsphere system for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, is primarily prepared using a one‑pot solvothermal route. This method, compared with post‑synthesis sodium impregnation, offers the advantage of homogeneous sodium incorporation during formation. However, the one‑pot approach may face scalability challenges, whereas impregnation, though more complex, allows enhanced control over promoter introduction.

#### One‑Pot Solvothermal Synthesis (Example Template)

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Reagents: 5.4 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 0.52 g manganese acetate, and 10.8 g ammonium acetate are dissolved in 100 mL ethylene glycol, which acts as both solvent and partial reducing agent.  
   - Additive: 2.4 g PVP is incorporated to control particle size and ensure uniform microsphere morphology.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - After thorough magnetic stirring, the solution is transferred to a 200 mL Teflon‑lined autoclave.  
   - Reaction conditions: Hydrothermal treatment is carried out at 200 °C for 12 h, where crystallization results in the formation of a characteristic black precipitate.

3. **Drying**  
   - Post washing (six times with deionized water and absolute ethanol), the precipitate is dried under vacuum at 110 °C for 12 h to remove residual solvents and moisture.

4. **Calcination**  
   - The dried sample is calcined at 400 °C for 5 h in a nitrogen atmosphere, ensuring the formation of well‑crystallized Fe₃O₄ microspheres.

5. **Additional Post‑treatments (Sodium Introduction)**  
   - Sodium is introduced either by a subsequent impregnation step—where the pre‑formed catalyst is treated with sodium carbonate solution, then dried and calcined—or by one‑pot addition, with sodium precursors added at the initial mixing stage.  
   - Comparative analysis indicates that while one‑pot incorporation yields a more uniform distribution, the impregnation method allows for finer promoter control.

This methodical and detailed procedure, with explicit reagent amounts, reaction conditions, and equipment (e.g., Teflon‑lined autoclave, pH meter, rotary evaporator), ensures clarity and reproducibility for subsequent catalyst synthesis.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the reagent preparation steps are organized into three distinct parts—solid weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing—each subdivided for catalyst formulations differing by their sodium introduction method. The catalysts are identified by their labels (e.g., Mn15 for the one‑pot method and Mn15Na16im/Mn15Na32im for the impregnation method), ensuring clarity in reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Mn15 (One‑pot Method)
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O**: Weighed in the required stoichiometric amount appropriate for the Fe₃O₄ formation using an analytical balance.
- **Manganese Acetate (Mn(Ac)₂)**: Weighed to provide the Mn15 promoter level.
- **Ammonium Acetate (NH₄Ac)**: Weighed to serve as a supporting reagent.
- **PVP (Polyvinylpyrrolidone)**: Weighed to act as a surfactant and particle size controller.

##### 3.1.1.2 Mn15Na16im (Impregnation Method)
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O, Manganese Acetate, Ammonium Acetate, and PVP**: Weighed as for Mn15.
- **Sodium Carbonate**: Weighed in a calculated amount to achieve a 1.6 level of sodium incorporation via the impregnation step.

##### 3.1.1.3 Mn15Na32im (Impregnation Method)
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O, Manganese Acetate, Ammonium Acetate, and PVP**: Weighed as indicated for the base catalyst.
- **Sodium Carbonate**: Weighed to provide a 3.2 sodium contribution, ensuring proper promoter balance during the post‑synthesis impregnation.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Mn15
- **Ethylene Glycol**: Transferred 100 mL using a graduated cylinder into the reaction vessel, serving as both solvent and partial reducing agent.

##### 3.1.2.2 Mn15Na16im
- **Ethylene Glycol**: Transferred 100 mL into the synthesis flask.
- **Sodium Carbonate Solution**: Added subsequently during the impregnation stage using a pipette to ensure controlled liquid addition.

##### 3.1.2.3 Mn15Na32im
- **Ethylene Glycol**: Delivered 100 mL using standard lab glassware.
- **Sodium Carbonate Solution**: Poured in a controlled manner during the impregnation protocol.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Mn15
- **Reaction Mixture**: Mixed by magnetic stirring to dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O, manganese acetate, ammonium acetate, and PVP until a homogeneous solution was obtained at room temperature.

##### 3.1.3.2 Mn15Na16im
- **Solution**: Stirred to ensure complete dissolution and then subjected to ultrasonic treatment to promote uniform dispersion prior to hydrothermal processing.

##### 3.1.3.3 Mn15Na32im
- **Mixture**: Homogenized via continuous stirring followed by a brief ultrasonic treatment, ensuring thorough mixing and consistent catalyst precursor quality.

This systematic grouping and detailed documentation provide a clear and reproducible approach for preparing the sodium modified Fe–Mn microsphere catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Sodium Modified Fe–Mn Microsphere Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • FeCl₃·6H₂O (5.4 g)  
  • Manganese acetate (0.52 g)  
  • Ammonium acetate (10.8 g)  
  • Ethylene glycol (100 mL)  
  • PVP (2.4 g)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Magnetic stirring at room temperature until complete dissolution  
  • No specific pH adjustment is required  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O in ethylene glycol.  
  2. Add manganese acetate and ammonium acetate sequentially, ensuring complete dissolution.  
  3. Introduce PVP to control particle size and prevent aggregation.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Ensure homogeneous mixing before proceeding to the hydrothermal reaction.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly via Hydrothermal Reaction  
- **Equipment & Conditions**:  
  • Transfer the precursor solution to a 200 mL Teflon-lined autoclave.  
  • Reaction is carried out at 200 °C for 12 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Seal the autoclave and heat under specified conditions to promote formation of Fe₃O₄ microspheres.  
  2. Allow the autoclave to cool naturally to room temperature before opening.

##### Step 3: Purification  
- **Process**:  
  1. Collect the resulting black precipitate.  
  2. Wash six times with deionized water followed by absolute ethanol to remove unreacted precursors and impurities.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry the washed catalyst under vacuum at 110 °C for 12 hours.  
- **Calcination**:  
  • Calcine the dried material at 400 °C for 5 hours in a nitrogen atmosphere.  
- **Important Note**: Maintain rigorous control of temperature and duration to ensure reproducibility.

##### Step 5: Sodium Incorporation  
- **Reagents & Methods**:  
  • Sodium carbonate is introduced either by the impregnation method (e.g., with 0.01–0.053 g depending on target dopant level) or added in one pot during precursor preparation.  
- **Process**:  
  1. For impregnation, impregnate the pre-calcined catalyst with a sodium carbonate solution, then repeat drying and calcination.  
- **Key Consideration**:  
  Impregnation typically yields better sodium distribution and enhanced catalytic electronic properties.

##### Step 6: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  Reduce the calcined catalyst under a controlled H₂/N₂ atmosphere (e.g., at 350 °C for 10 hours) to complete the formation of the active phase, ensuring effective precursor conversion before catalytic testing.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X-ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD patterns were collected to assess the crystalline phases and structural integrity of the catalysts. The technique confirms the formation of well-crystallized Fe₃O₄ and allows observation of crystallinity changes with promoter addition.
- **Instruments and Models**: D/ MAX 2550 VB/PC (RIGAKU, Japan) utilizing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Operated at 40 kV and 100 mA; scanning range from 10° to 80° with a step size of 0.02°. Prior to analysis, catalysts were dispersed in ethanol and treated with ultrasound to ensure uniformity.
- **Test Parameters**: Precise sample alignment ensuring accurate diffraction profiles.
- **Key Notes**: Strict adherence to alignment protocols minimizes errors in phase identification.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: This method evaluates the reduction behavior of the catalyst. Approximately 50 mg of catalyst (60–120 mesh) was subjected to a controlled reduction.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micro fixed-bed reactor equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Treatment in 10% H₂/90% Ar at a flow rate of 50 mL/min, with the temperature ramped from room temperature to 900 °C at 10 K/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Detection of two reduction peaks – one around 300 °C (surface Fe₂O₃ reduction) and another in the 550–650 °C range (conversion of Fe₃O₄).
- **Key Notes**: Promoters such as Mn and Na distinctly influence the reduction profiles, shifting peak positions to lower temperatures.

### 4.3 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: Measurement of surface area and pore characteristics via argon physical adsorption.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2020.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Conducted at −186 °C with surface areas calculated by the BET method and pore properties determined by the BJH method.
- **Test Parameters**: The catalysts, pre-calcined at 400 °C for 5 h in nitrogen, exhibited surface areas in the range of 9.8–27.1 m²/g.
- **Key Notes**: The nearly solid structure of the microspheres necessitates careful control to avoid pore-blocking effects.

### 4.4 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) & High‑Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM)
- **Operational Description**: These methods visualize the catalyst’s morphology and nanostructure.
- **Instruments and Models**: SEM conducted on an Inspect F50 (10 kV); HRTEM performed on a Tecnai F30 (300 kV).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were dispersed in ethanol and sonicated to obtain uniform images.
- **Test Parameters**: Microspheres are observed at 300–400 nm, while constituent nanocrystals measure approximately 10–20 nm.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform dispersion is critical for accurate morphological analysis.

### 4.5 Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)
- **Operational Description**: EDS mapping confirms the even distribution of Mn and Na across the catalyst.
- **Instruments and Models**: Genesis XM2 Energy Dispersive Spectrometer.
- **Key Notes**: This method validates successful impregnation and uniform promoter distribution.

### 4.6 X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: XPS assesses the surface electronic state and confirms electron donation effects from Mn and Na.
- **Instruments and Models**: American Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi using Al Kα radiation (hv = 1486.6 eV) at 150 W with a 500 μm beam spot.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were pre-dispersed via ethanol-ultrasound treatment.
- **Test Parameters**: Shifts in Fe 2p binding energies indicate increased electron density.
- **Key Notes**: Binding energy shifts corroborate enhanced CO adsorption due to promoter effects.

### 4.7 Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: This technique identifies iron phase transitions, particularly the conversion of Fe₃O₄ to the active χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements carried out at room temperature using a 57Co/Pd radioactive source.
- **Key Notes**: The high proportion of χ‑Fe₅C₂ detected confirms effective activation of the catalyst post-reaction.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation procedure is designed to convert the precursor material into a catalytically active phase while preserving the structural integrity of the Fe–Mn microspheres. This activation comprises two sequential steps: an inert calcination stage followed by a reduction under a controlled syngas atmosphere.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for both stabilizing the catalyst morphology and promoting the necessary phase transformations.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Initially, the catalyst precursor is calcined at 400 °C for 5 hours in a nitrogen atmosphere. Following calcination, a reduction is performed at 350 °C using a flowing syngas mixture.
- **Observations:** Calcination at 400 °C preserves the spherical morphology, while reduction at 350 °C efficiently converts the precursor (primarily Fe₃O₄) into the active χ-Fe₅C₂ phase.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The activation pressure affects the kinetics of gas–solid interactions essential for effective precursor reduction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction step is carried out at an exact pressure of 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** This pressure supports a controlled reduction environment, ensuring thorough transformation without causing catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation is fundamental for providing the necessary reducing atmosphere.
- **Experimental Conditions:** During the reduction phase, a syngas mixture with a hydrogen to nitrogen ratio of 1:1 is employed. In contrast, nitrogen is used exclusively during the calcination step.
- **Observations:** The syngas atmosphere effectively reduces the catalyst precursor, while the inert nitrogen atmosphere prevents premature oxidation during calcination.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity defines the volumetric flow rate of the activation gas per unit weight of the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 5000 mL/(h·g_cat) is maintained during reduction.
- **Observations:** This flow rate provides optimal contact between the catalyst and the reducing gases, ensuring uniform and complete activation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The activation time must be carefully controlled to achieve complete precursor conversion while avoiding excessive sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction treatment is conducted for 10 hours after calcination.
- **Observations:** The selected duration ensures full transformation to the active phase with minimal risk of catalyst deactivation.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature refers to the operating condition at which the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis occurs. In the present study, the catalytic evaluation was conducted at a temperature of 320 °C. This parameter is critical as it governs the kinetics of syngas conversion and the formation of active iron carbide (χ-Fe₅C₂). Experimental observations indicate that operating at 320 °C provides optimal catalyst performance, with improved activity and selectivity toward light olefins. The temperature setting also aids in maintaining catalyst stability over prolonged reaction periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure is defined as the overall system pressure within the fixed-bed reactor during the process. The system was maintained at 1.5 MPa, a condition that enhances syngas concentration and thus promotes higher conversion rates. Under these pressure conditions, the catalyst demonstrated favorable performance, with improved product selectivity observed—particularly an increase in light olefin yields and a suppression of methane byproduct formation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio in the feed gas determines the balance of hydrogenation reactions and influences the distribution of hydrocarbon products. For this system, an H₂/CO ratio of 2 was employed. This ratio was selected to optimize the formation of higher carbon number products while reducing unwanted methane formation. The chosen value ensures that reactive hydrogen levels are sufficient for promoting olefin formation without over-hydrogenating the intermediates.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The space-time velocity, expressed as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), is the ratio of the syngas flow rate to the catalyst mass and directly affects the reactant exposure time. The study utilized a GHSV of 11,000 mL/(h∙g_cat), which ensured a high flow rate through the reactor, leading to effective contact between the reactants and the catalyst. This parameter not only aids in maximizing CO conversion but also contributes to maintaining a stable reaction environment throughout the catalytic evaluation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and crystallinity of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases and understanding the influence of promoters on structural properties.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirmed the presence of well-crystallized Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) in all samples, with characteristic peaks at 2θ values of 30.1°, 35.4°, 43°, 53.6°, 57.1°, and 62.6°. Mn-containing catalysts exhibited lower crystallinity, as indicated by weaker diffraction peaks, while sodium addition (especially via the one-pot method) increased crystallinity, evidenced by more intense peaks.
- **Observations:** The lower crystallinity in Mn-promoted samples is attributed to Mn incorporation into the Fe₃O₄ lattice during hydrothermal synthesis, which enhances CO chemisorption and facilitates the formation of the active χ-Fe₅C₂ phase. Sodium further promotes crystallinity and phase purity, with the impregnation method preserving the beneficial Fe–Mn interaction.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were evaluated using Ar adsorption–desorption (BET analysis), providing insight into the catalysts’ textural properties and their impact on reactant diffusion.
- **Findings:** The BET surface areas ranged from 9.8 to 27.1 m²/g, and pore volumes were less than 0.1 cm³/g across all samples. These values indicate that the microspheres are nearly solid with a dense, nonporous interior.
- **Observations:** The relatively low surface area and pore volume are consistent with the formation of dense microspheres, which is advantageous for stability and may influence catalytic selectivity.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was used to assess the reducibility of the catalysts, which is critical for activating the iron phase.
- **Findings:** Two main reduction peaks were observed: one at ~300 °C (surface Fe₂O₃ reduction) and another between 550–650 °C (bulk Fe₃O₄ to FeO/α-Fe). Mn incorporation increased the reduction temperature, indicating suppressed reducibility due to strong Fe–Mn interaction. Sodium addition, particularly by impregnation, lowered the reduction temperature, attributed to its electron-donating effect.
- **Observations:** The synergistic effect of Mn and Na optimizes the reduction process, facilitating the formation of the active carbide phase.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) characterized the morphology and internal structure of the catalysts.
- **Findings:** SEM images revealed highly uniform, spherical microspheres with diameters of 300–400 nm. HRTEM showed that these spheres are assembled from nanocrystals of 10–20 nm. Mn-containing samples exhibited smaller, more uniform spheres with higher surface areas.
- **Observations:** The use of PVP as a surfactant and the controlled hydrothermal synthesis contributed to the observed uniformity and size control, which are beneficial for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Distribution and Electronic Structure
- **Description:** Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) provided information on elemental distribution and electronic states.
- **Findings:** EDS mapping demonstrated even distribution of Mn throughout the microspheres and uniform surface distribution of Na when introduced by impregnation. XPS revealed that both Mn and Na act as electron donors, shifting Fe 2p binding energies to lower values and increasing electron density around Fe. O 1s spectra indicated the presence of oxygen vacancies, especially in Mn-modified samples.
- **Observations:** The electronic modifications induced by Mn and Na enhance CO adsorption and promote the formation of active sites.

#### 6.1.6 Phase Transformation under Reaction Conditions
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES) was employed to identify iron phases after reaction, crucial for correlating structure with catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** Post-reaction MES showed predominant transformation of Fe₃O₄ to the active χ-Fe₅C₂ phase, particularly in Mn15Na32im (92.9% χ-Fe₅C₂, 7.1% Fe₃O₄). The absence of Fe³⁺ (spm) in Na32 and Mn15Na32im indicates nearly complete activation.
- **Observations:** The high content of χ-Fe₅C₂ in Mn and Na co-promoted catalysts directly correlates with superior Fischer–Tropsch performance, confirming the critical role of promoter synergy in active phase formation.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{N_{\text{in, CO}} - N_{\text{out, CO}}}{N_{\text{in, CO}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \(N_{\text{in, CO}}\) and \(N_{\text{out, CO}}\) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (320 °C, 1.5 MPa, H₂/CO = 3, GHSV = 11,000 mL/(h·g_cat)), the Mn15Na32im catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 97.21%. For comparison, Mn15 (Mn only) exhibited 77.47%, Mn15Na16im (lower Na) reached 90.12%, and Mn15Na32 (one-pot Na addition) showed 88.3%.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increases with Na addition, especially when introduced by impregnation. The synergistic effect of Mn and Na, particularly via impregnation, maximizes CO conversion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{N_{\text{out, CO}_2}}{N_{\text{in, CO}} - N_{\text{out, CO}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity values are 28.91% for Mn15, 30.34% for Mn15Na16im, and increase with higher Na content. The trend continues for Mn15Na32im, though the exact value is not specified.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity rises with increasing Na, reflecting enhanced water–gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{N_{\text{out, CH}_4}}{N_{\text{in, CO}} - N_{\text{out, CO}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity decreases with Na addition: 16.98% for Mn15, 10.39% for Mn15Na16im, and 2.45% for Mn15Na32im.
- **Observations and Trends:** Sodium, especially via impregnation, effectively suppresses methane formation, favoring olefin production.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn15Na32im catalyst achieves a C₂–C₄ (light olefin) selectivity of 33.02%, with C₃⁺ selectivity increasing alongside Na content.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced C₃⁺ selectivity is observed with optimal Mn and Na promotion, correlating with suppressed methane and increased olefin yields.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model. Higher α values favor longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the product distribution for Mn15Na32im (high light olefin selectivity, low methane) suggests a moderate α, favoring short-chain olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** The combination of Mn and Na tunes the α factor to optimize light olefin production.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  r_d = \frac{X_{\text{CO,initial}} - X_{\text{CO,final}}}{X_{\text{CO,initial}}} \times \frac{100}{t}
  \]
  where \(t\) is the reaction time (h).
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn15Na32im catalyst exhibits a deactivation rate of 0.026% per hour, indicating excellent stability. Catalysts with Na added by the one-pot method show higher deactivation rates.
- **Observations and Trends:** Na impregnation yields superior catalyst stability compared to the one-pot method.

#### 6.2.7 Olefin-to-Paraffin Ratio (O/P)
- **Description:** The O/P ratio reflects the selectivity for olefins over paraffins in the product distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** Mn15Na32im achieves an O/P ratio of 5.33, demonstrating strong suppression of secondary hydrogenation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher O/P ratios are linked to the synergistic effect of Mn and Na, with impregnation maximizing olefin selectivity.

**Summary:** The sodium-modified Fe–Mn microsphere catalyst, especially Mn15Na32im, demonstrates outstanding CO conversion, high light olefin selectivity, low methane formation, and excellent stability. The impregnation method for Na introduction is critical for maximizing performance, with the synergistic action of Mn and Na optimizing both activity and selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
