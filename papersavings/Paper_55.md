## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts are prepared via a controlled precipitation technique. The following experimental procedure is extracted from the article, ensuring reproducibility and precision.

#### Precursor Mixing and Co‑precipitation
1. Dissolve the metal precursors:
   - Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Cu(NO₃)₂·3H₂O (primary Fe source with Cu as the reduction promoter) are dissolved in 60 mL of water.
   - Tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS) is dissolved in 40 mL of propanol to introduce Si as a structural promoter.
2. Mix the two solutions and heat to 83 ± 3 °C. A pH meter monitors the solution during subsequent steps.

#### Precipitation–Deposition
3. Introduce the precipitation step by continuously adding preheated aqueous NH₄OH (approximately 2.7 M) until achieving a pH between 8 and 9. This careful, continuous addition ensures uniform precipitation and optimal formation of the Fe oxide-based structure.  
4. Immediately following precipitation, observe the formation of a precipitate – a critical indicator of effective co‑precipitation and deposition of the components, including any added third metal promoters.

#### Drying and Calcination
5. After an aging period of approximately 17 h at room temperature to allow for precipitate maturation, wash the precipitate thoroughly with 1.3–1.5 L deionized water until pH neutralization (approximately 7–8) is reached.
6. Dry the washed material at 110 °C for 18–24 h to remove residual moisture.
7. Calcine the dried precursor in static air at 300 °C for 5 h to develop the active catalyst phase.

#### Post‑treatment and Final Preparation
8. Sieve the calcined catalyst to achieve particle sizes of less than 90 µm.
9. Prior to reaction evaluation, reduce the catalyst in situ under a flow of H₂ at 280 °C for 12 h, employing a controlled ramp rate of 2 °C/min.

This step‐by‐step procedure, including detailed reagent concentrations, reaction conditions, and specific equipment usage (e.g., pH meter, heating apparatus, muffle furnace), provides a clear guide for replication. The method’s scalability and reproducibility are further underscored by the precise control of temperature (83 ± 3 °C during mixing and 280 °C during reduction) and pH (maintained at 8–9 during precipitation), ensuring the successful incorporation of third metal promoters as reported.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section the reagent handling is described following three key steps—solid reagent weighing, liquid transfer, and subsequent mixing—to ensure reproducibility of both the benchmark Fe‐based catalyst (100Fe/5Cu/17Si) and a representative promoted variant (e.g., FeCr).

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 100Fe/5Cu/17Si  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed according to the stoichiometric requirement for the Fe oxide formulation.  
- **Cu(NO₃)₂·3H₂O**: Weighed to achieve the 5Cu promoter level.  
- **Tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS)**: Weighed in proportion to yield a 17Si loading.

##### 3.1.1.2 FeCr  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed following the modified formulation (95Fe in the promoted catalyst).  
- **Cu(NO₃)₂·3H₂O**: Weighed to maintain the 5Cu content.  
- **Cr Precursor (nitrate or alkoxide)**: Weighed to provide a 5% addition in the promoted formulation.  
- **Tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS)**: Weighed as the source of Si for structural stabilization.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 100Fe/5Cu/17Si  
- **Deionized Water**: Transferred (~60 mL) using a graduated cylinder for dissolving the Fe and Cu precursors.  
- **Propanol**: Transferred (~40 mL) using a pipette or graduated cylinder to dissolve TEOS.

##### 3.1.2.2 FeCr  
- **Deionized Water**: Transferred (~60 mL) for precursor dissolution.  
- **Propanol**: Transferred (~40 mL) for TEOS dissolution.  
- **Aqueous NH₄OH**: Added gradually (preheated to maintain temperature) until the pH is adjusted to between 8 and 9.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 100Fe/5Cu/17Si  
- **Precursor Solution**: Mixed by combining the aqueous solution with the TEOS–propanol solution and then heated to 83 ± 3 °C while stirring continuously to ensure homogeneity.

##### 3.1.3.2 FeCr  
- **Combined Precursor Mixture**: Mixed under controlled heating (83 ± 3 °C) with continuous stirring. The gradual addition of aqueous NH₄OH is performed during this stage to induce precipitation, and the resulting suspension is further aged at room temperature for approximately 17 hours.

These detailed procedures—organized by catalyst formulation—allow for consistent preparation of both benchmark and promoted Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, ensuring that each critical step is performed under reproducible conditions.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe-Based Fischer–Tropsch Catalysts (Benchmark: 100Fe/5Cu/17Si & Promoted Variants)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 
  - Fe precursor: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (0.6 M solution)
  - Cu precursor: Cu(NO₃)₂·3H₂O
  - Structural promoter: Tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS), dissolved in 40 mL propanol
  - For promoted variants, additional metal nitrates/alkoxide complexes (Cr, Mn, Mo, Ta, V, W, Zr) are incorporated at a 5% stoichiometric ratio.
- **Conditions**: 
  - Fe and Cu nitrates are dissolved in 60 mL deionized water.
  - Maintain vigorous stirring while mixing both solutions.
  - The mixed solution is heated to 83 ± 3 °C.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve Fe and Cu salts in water and TEOS in propanol separately.
  - Combine the solutions to yield a 100 mL precursor mixture.
  - Continuously add preheated aqueous NH₄OH (approximately 2.7 M) under vigorous stirring to adjust the pH steadily to a target range of 8–9, as confirmed by a calibrated pH meter.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Process**:
  - Once pH is stabilized, allow the precipitate to age at room temperature for 17 hours.
  - Thoroughly wash the aged precipitate with 1.3–1.5 liters of deionized water until wash pH measures between 7 and 8, ensuring the removal of excess ammonia.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: 
  - Dry the washed precipitate in an oven at 110 °C for 18–24 hours.
- **Calcination**:
  - Heat in static air with a 2 °C/min ramp to a final temperature of 300 °C and hold for 5 hours.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Process**:
  - Prior to catalytic evaluation, sieve the catalyst to <90 µm.
  - Reduce in situ under hydrogen at 280 °C for 12 hours with a 2 °C/min ramp to form active carbided Fe phases.

**Key Steps & Cautions**:  
Maintain consistent stirring, precise temperature control, and continuous pH monitoring during precipitation. Each step from precursor preparation to activation is critical for reproducibility and the successful integration of promotors into the Fe-based catalyst formulation, ensuring both desired dispersion and enhanced catalytic performance.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was performed to determine the crystalline structure of the Fe-based catalysts. The method confirmed that the catalysts are essentially amorphous with crystallite sizes below 4–5 nm.
- **Instruments and Models**: Scintag 2000 X‑ray diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Utilized monochromatized Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA.
- **Test Parameters**: Scanning was conducted over a 2θ range of 10° to 90° at a scanning rate of 0.005° per second.
- **Key Notes**: Samples were previously calcined to remove moisture and volatile species; the amorphous nature of the catalysts can limit the detection of specific oxide phases.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: This method was applied to assess the textural properties of the catalysts through N₂ physisorption.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2020 automated system.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Analysis was performed at 77 K.
- **Test Parameters**: A 0.3 g catalyst sample was degassed at 100 °C for 1 hour, then heated at 10 °C/min to 300 °C and held for 2 hours prior to measurement.
- **Key Notes**: Although the relative pressure range was not specified, the method confirmed high surface areas (295–354 m²/g).

### 4.3 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR was used to evaluate the reduction behavior and confirm the conversion of Fe oxides.
- **Instruments and Models**: Altamira AMI‑1 system in a micro fixed-bed reactor equipped with a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: A 0.1 g calcined catalyst sample was exposed to a 5% H₂/Ar gas flow.
- **Test Parameters**: Temperature ramped at 2 °C/min up to 800 °C, revealing two main reduction peaks at approximately 215 °C and 600 °C.
- **Key Notes**: Proper uniform catalyst loading and slow ramping are essential for reproducible reduction profiles.

### 4.4 CO Chemisorption
- **Operational Description**: This technique quantifies active Fe sites by measuring CO uptake.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2010 automated system.
- **Characterization Conditions**: The catalyst was evacuated to 10⁻⁶ mm Hg at 100 °C, reduced under flowing H₂ at 280 °C for 12 hours, then re-evacuated at 280 °C for 60 minutes.
- **Test Parameters**: Measurements were conducted at 35 °C to calculate turnover frequency (TOFchem).
- **Key Notes**: Stringent sample pretreatment ensures accuracy in active site determination.

### 4.5 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy Dispersive X‑ray (EDX) Analysis
- **Operational Description**: SEM and EDX were employed to study catalyst morphology and elemental distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: Hitachi FESEM‑S4800.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Analysis performed at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV with a working distance of 14 mm.
- **Test Parameters**: Granule sizes between 10–60 µm were observed.
- **Key Notes**: Careful sample mounting is critical to avoid misinterpretation of morphological features.

### 4.6 Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP‑OES)
- **Operational Description**: ICP‑OES was used for verifying the elemental composition of the catalysts.
- **Instruments and Models**: Analysis performed by Galbraith Laboratories Inc.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Fresh calcined catalysts were analyzed.
- **Test Parameters**: Reported compositions were confirmed within ±10% accuracy.
- **Key Notes**: This method complements other techniques by providing bulk elemental analysis essential for catalyst formulation verification.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation of the Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is a critical step aimed at converting the calcined Fe oxide precursor into its active metallic and carbidic forms. This activation is carried out through an in situ reduction process that directly prepares the catalyst for efficient CO hydrogenation during subsequent reaction testing.

#### 5.1.1 Pre-Activation Preparation
Before the reduction process, the freshly calcined catalyst is sieved to obtain particles smaller than 90 µm. This sieving step is essential for ensuring uniform particle size, which aids in achieving consistent temperature distribution and effective reduction throughout the catalyst bed.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is pivotal for initiating the reduction of Fe oxide species to their active forms, thereby exposing the catalytic sites necessary for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 280 °C.
- **Observations:** Maintaining this temperature ensures complete reduction of the Fe oxide phases while preventing sintering, thereby promoting high catalytic activity.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The use of a reducing gas is integral to driving the in situ reduction reaction, converting the oxidic phases of iron into active metallic or carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Hydrogen (H₂) is employed as the activating gas.
- **Observations:** H₂ effectively reduces the Fe₂O₃ phase, facilitating the creation of active sites. Its role is critical as it ensures the transformation of the oxidic species into a form that is responsive to syngas exposure during the catalytic reaction.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Ramp Rate and Duration
- **Ramp Rate:** 
  - **Definition:** The temperature ramp rate determines the rate at which the catalyst reaches the activation temperature, crucial for controlled reduction.
  - **Experimental Conditions:** A ramp rate of 2 °C/min is implemented.
  - **Observations:** This gradual increase prevents thermal shock and promotes uniform reduction without inducing sintering.
- **Activation Time:** 
  - **Definition:** The duration of the activation ensures that the reduction reaction is complete and stable active sites are formed.
  - **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is held at 280 °C under H₂ for 12 hours.
  - **Observations:** Extended activation confirms full conversion of Fe oxide species, which is directly correlated with enhanced CO conversion rates and overall catalytic performance.

Through these carefully controlled activation parameters, the catalyst achieves optimal dispersion of Fe species and effective formation of the active phases essential for high Fischer–Tropsch activity.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch reaction is performed at a constant temperature of 280 °C. This temperature is critical for activating the syngas and facilitating the formation of active carbide phases on the iron-based catalyst. Under these conditions, the catalyst exhibits the optimal balance between conversion efficiency and product selectivity.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The reaction is conducted at a total pressure of 1.8 atm. This pressure is selected to enhance the partial pressures of H₂ and CO, thereby increasing their concentration in the reactor. Such conditions promote the desired hydrocarbon synthesis while ensuring that the reaction remains within the optimal kinetic regime.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The syngas feed is prepared with a H₂/CO molar ratio of 2:1. This ratio is instrumental in directing the product selectivity toward enhanced olefin formation while controlling methane production. Adjustments to this ratio are known to influence hydrocarbon chain growth, and the chosen value reflects a balance that supports reproducible catalytic performance.

#### 5.2.4 Syngas Flow Rate and Reactor Configuration  
The total syngas flow rate is maintained at 60 cc/min, with the feed composed of 5 cc/min of 95% CO mixed with 5% Ar and 10 cc/min of H₂, balanced with He. This precise control of flow rate ensures a consistent space-time velocity critical for prolonged catalyst stability and measurable conversion metrics. The reaction is carried out in a quartz microreactor, which provides excellent thermal management and minimizes contamination. Quartz is particularly suitable due to its ability to withstand high temperatures and ensure accurate regulation of both temperature and pressure during the catalytic evaluation.

Overall, these defined parameters—280 °C, 1.8 atm, a 2:1 H₂/CO ratio, and a controlled syngas flow rate in a quartz microreactor—are essential for ensuring the experimental reproducibility and reliability of the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis evaluations described in the article. Observations indicate that under these conditions, the catalyst maintains high activity with an induction period of approximately 15 minutes for CO hydrogenation and 15–30 minutes for the water–gas shift reaction.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were assessed using X-ray diffraction (XRD). This technique is critical for determining the degree of crystallinity, average crystallite size, and the presence of amorphous or crystalline phases, which directly influence catalyst dispersion and reactivity.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns, collected with a Scintag 2000 diffractometer (Cu Kα, 40 kV, 40 mA), revealed that all catalysts are XRD-amorphous, with no discernible peaks for Fe, Cu, or other metal oxides. The average Fe oxide crystallite size was determined to be less than 4–5 nm, indicating high dispersion.
- **Observations:** The amorphous nature and small crystallite size are attributed to the synthesis method and the use of Si as a structural promoter, which together prevent sintering and promote uniform metal distribution.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were evaluated by N₂ physisorption at 77 K using the BET method. These parameters are essential for understanding catalyst accessibility and potential for reactant diffusion.
- **Findings:** BET surface area values ranged from 295 to 354 m²/g. The benchmark 100Fe catalyst exhibited a surface area of 329 m²/g, while promoted catalysts such as FeCr, FeMn, and FeZr showed higher values (351, 354, and 350 m²/g, respectively). FeW had the lowest surface area at 295 m²/g. Pore volumes were 0.34 cm³/g for 100Fe and 0.29–0.33 cm³/g for promoted variants, with average pore sizes decreasing from 42 Å (100Fe) to 33–38 Å (promoted catalysts).
- **Observations:** The increased surface area and reduced pore size in most promoted catalysts suggest improved porosity and enhanced reactant diffusion, which are beneficial for catalytic performance. FeW is an exception, showing less improvement in these properties.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Redox Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature Programmed Reduction (TPR) was performed using an Altamira AMI-1 system to assess the reducibility of Fe species, a key factor in catalyst activation and performance.
- **Findings:** TPR profiles displayed two main peaks: the first (∼215 °C) corresponds to Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄, and the second (∼600 °C) to Fe₃O₄ → Fe. The presence of Cu facilitated Fe reduction. Third metal promoters influenced the temperature of the first reduction peak: Cr lowered it (to ~207 °C), while Mn and V increased it (to 241 °C and 249 °C, respectively). The percentage of Fe reduced under standard pretreatment (280 °C, 12 h) was consistent (32–39%) across all catalysts.
- **Observations:** Shifts in reduction temperatures reflect the interaction between Fe and promoters, affecting the ease of Fe activation and the formation of active carbide phases.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Dispersion
- **Description:** Elemental analysis was conducted by ICP-OES to verify catalyst composition, while CO chemisorption (Micromeritics ASAP 2010) quantified active Fe dispersion.
- **Findings:** ICP-OES confirmed that elemental compositions matched target values within ±10% accuracy. CO chemisorption showed increased Fe dispersion for most promoted catalysts (e.g., FeCr: 234 µmol/g, 5.4% dispersion; 100Fe: 120 µmol/g, 2.6% dispersion), except FeW, which did not improve dispersion.
- **Observations:** Enhanced Fe dispersion correlates with higher catalytic activity, particularly for Cr-, Mn-, and Zr-promoted catalysts.

#### 6.1.5 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** SEM, coupled with EDX, was used to examine catalyst morphology, granule size, and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** SEM images revealed faceted, irregularly shaped granules with uneven surfaces, ranging from 10–60 µm in diameter. EDX mapping demonstrated uniform distribution of Fe, Cu, Si, and promoter elements across the catalyst particles.
- **Observations:** The consistent morphology and homogeneous elemental distribution support the effectiveness of the synthesis method in producing well-dispersed, structurally robust catalysts.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (280 °C, 1.8 atm, H₂/CO = 2:1), the benchmark 100Fe/5Cu/17Si catalyst exhibits moderate CO conversion, with maximum formation rates of 0.50 µmol C/g/s for CO₂ and 0.78 µmol C/g/s for total hydrocarbons. Promoted catalysts, particularly those containing Cr, Mn, and Zr, achieve 2–3 times higher CO hydrogenation and water–gas shift (WGS) activities compared to the benchmark. For example, FeMn demonstrates a notably higher intrinsic site activity and sustained CO conversion over time.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of carbide-forming promoters enhances CO conversion, with FeMn, FeCr, and FeZr showing the most significant improvements. FeW, in contrast, displays lower activity due to reduced Fe dispersion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating WGS activity:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Promoted catalysts (Cr, Mn, Zr) exhibit CO₂ formation rates 2–3 times higher than 100Fe, consistent with enhanced WGS activity. The selectivity remains stable across catalyst types, with no significant shift in hydrocarbon distribution.
- **Observations and Trends:** The increased CO₂ selectivity for FeCr and FeZr aligns with the known WGS-promoting properties of Cr and Zr oxides. Mn promotion further stabilizes active carbonaceous species, supporting higher CO₂ production.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the percentage of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Across all catalysts, CH₄ selectivity remains relatively constant, with no substantial variation upon promoter addition.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of third metal promoters does not significantly affect methane selectivity, indicating that hydrocarbon product distribution is largely preserved.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming C₃ and higher hydrocarbons:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The selectivity for C₃⁺ hydrocarbons is similar across all catalyst formulations, with only minor increases in C₂–C₄ olefin selectivity observed over time.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrocarbon distribution remains stable, with slight increases in light olefins (e.g., ethylene) at extended time-on-stream, attributed to catalyst deactivation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth versus termination, influencing product distribution. It is derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model.
- **Experimental Data:** Initially, α is higher for promoted catalysts (except FeW), but after 5 h on stream, all converge to α ≈ 0.35.
- **Observations and Trends:** While promotion can transiently increase α, the long-term chain growth probability is not significantly altered by third metal addition.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** All catalysts exhibit an initial induction period (15–30 min) before reaching maximum activity, followed by a decline to steady-state. FeMn maintains higher and more stable activity than FeCr and FeZr, indicating superior stability.
- **Observations and Trends:** Deactivation is linked to a reduction in active Fe sites and increased carbon deposition, with Mn promotion notably enhancing catalyst longevity.
