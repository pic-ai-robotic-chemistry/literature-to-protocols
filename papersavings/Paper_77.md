## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe–Zr–Ce–K catalyst series is achieved through a microwave assisted homogeneous precipitation process, followed by incipient wetness impregnation and subsequent thermal treatments. The following outlines a step‐by‐step procedure as reported:

#### • Microwave Assisted Homogeneous Precipitation Method

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Reagents: Fe, Zr, and Ce precursors are mixed in controlled molar ratios (e.g., 50:10:1, 35:7:1, etc.) with urea added at a molar ratio of 1:6 relative to the metal components.  
   - Equipment: Standard laboratory mixing vessel with magnetic stirring.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - Process: The homogeneous mixture is subjected to microwave irradiation, achieving a temperature range of 170–180 °C.  
   - Outcome: Rapid nucleation leads to the formation of uniform, microspherical catalyst particles, as indicated by uniform precipitate formation.

3. **Drying**  
   - After precipitation, the catalyst is impregnated with an aqueous K₂CO₃ solution through incipient wetness impregnation.  
   - Drying Conditions: The K₂CO₃‑impregnated catalyst is dried at 120 °C overnight to remove moisture and consolidate the catalyst structure.

4. **Calcination (When Applicable)**  
   - Although specific calcination parameters are not explicitly detailed, standard protocols would involve a thermal treatment step to stabilize the precursor phases prior to reduction.  
   - Note: The drying step at 120 °C functions to partly consolidate the formed structure, minimizing the need for an independent high‑temperature calcination step in this procedure.

5. **Post‑treatment: In Situ Reduction**  
   - Conditions: The dried catalyst undergoes in situ reduction at 400 °C for 8 h under a 30% H₂ atmosphere (GHSV of 1000 h⁻¹ at 0.1 MPa).  
   - Significance: This step is vital to convert Fe₂O₃ to the active metallic and Fe₃O₄ phases.

### Comparative Insights  
- **Advantages:**  
  • Microwave assisted precipitation ensures rapid, uniform heating and microspherical particle formation, enhancing reproducibility and catalyst dispersion.  
- **Limitations:**  
  • While the method provides high uniformity and rapid nucleation, precise control of reaction parameters (e.g., pH and temperature) is critical and may require specialized microwave reactor equipment.

This detailed workflow ensures reproducibility and offers critical insight into the synthesis strategy for tailoring catalyst properties in CO₂ hydrogenation to light olefins.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

For the Fe–Zr–Ce–K catalyst series (50Fe–10Zr–1Ce, 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce, 15Fe–3Zr–1Ce, and 5Fe–1Zr–1Ce), the synthesis is organized into a systematic workflow to ensure reproducibility.

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 
  - Iron salts (e.g., Fe(NO₃)₃) at molar ratios corresponding to Fe content.

  - Microwave reactor is utilized to maintain 170–180 °C, ensuring rapid and uniform precipitation.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the metal salts in water, followed by addition of urea.
  - Microwave irradiation facilitates homogeneous precipitation, where urea decomposition gradually releases ammonia to buffer the solution at a near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH.
  - The precipitate is collected by centrifugation.
- **Key Steps**: 
  - Ensuring correct reagent ratios and controlled microwave heating.
  - Consistent pH stabilization is critical to prevent excessive crystallite growth.

- **Reagents**: 
  - The as-prepared metal hydroxide precursor is combined with any additional ligands or supports.
- **Conditions**:
  - The components are thoroughly mixed under ambient conditions.

  - Dry the material in an oven at 120 °C for 12 hours, ensuring even moisture removal.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst pre-treatment for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is performed through an in situ reduction process designed to convert the precursor iron phases into their active forms. This process is meticulously controlled to achieve reproducibility and optimal catalyst performance.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for providing sufficient thermal energy to initiate and drive the reduction of iron oxides (Fe₂O₃) to Fe₃O₄ and eventually to metallic Fe.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 400 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature has been demonstrated to facilitate complete and uniform conversion without inducing excessive sintering, thereby ensuring a high dispersion of the active metallic sites.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The activation pressure governs the gas–solid contact and directly influences the reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is carried out at 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at this relatively low pressure ensures effective gas diffusion, promoting even reduction across the catalyst batch.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
- **Definition:** A properly composed activation gas is essential for maintaining a controlled reducing environment which gradually converts iron oxides to the active phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A reducing atmosphere containing 30% H₂ (with the balance being an inert carrier) is employed.
- **Observations:** The 30% H₂ composition is optimized to achieve the stepwise transition from Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ and then to metallic Fe, ensuring the integrity of the active phase formation for Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity (GHSV)
- **Definition:** The Gas Hourly Space Velocity (GHSV) determines the flow rate of the activation gas per catalyst volume, ensuring uniform exposure.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 1000 h⁻¹ is maintained during activation.
- **Observations:** This controlled flow rate promotes uniform gas–catalyst contact, enhancing the efficiency of the reduction process.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is critical to complete the reduction without promoting undesirable sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under activation conditions for 8 hours.
- **Observations:** An 8-hour treatment has been found to be sufficient for complete phase transformation while maintaining structural stability, thus preparing the catalyst effectively for subsequent CO₂ hydrogenation in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the Fe–Zr–Ce–K catalyst series were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD). This technique identifies phase composition, crystallite size, and the influence of promoters on structural properties, which are critical for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that the as-prepared catalysts predominantly contain the α-Fe₂O₃ (hematite) phase, with characteristic peaks such as the (104) reflection. Upon increasing Ce content, the intensity of α-Fe₂O₃ and tetragonal ZrO₂ peaks diminished, indicating reduced crystallinity and smaller domain sizes. The average crystallite size of α-Fe₂O₃, calculated via the Scherrer equation, decreased from 28.6 nm (50Fe–10Zr–1Ce) to 15.8 nm (5Fe–1Zr–1Ce) as Ce content increased.
- **Observations:** The addition of CeO₂ acts as a structural promoter, restraining Fe₂O₃ crystallite growth and generating more surface defects, which are beneficial for CO₂ activation and catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were assessed using N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms and BET analysis. These parameters influence reactant diffusion and the accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** All samples exhibited type IV isotherms with H3-type hysteresis loops, confirming mesoporosity. The 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce catalyst displayed a high BET surface area of 78 m²/g. Mesopore volume was significantly larger than micropore volume, with a mesopore diameter of approximately 2.4 nm.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and mesoporous structure, especially in Ce-containing samples, facilitate improved reactant diffusion and enhance catalytic efficiency.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Metal–Support Interaction
- **Description:** H₂-temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was employed to evaluate the reducibility of iron species and their interaction with the support. Reducibility affects the formation of active phases during reaction.
- **Findings:** For Fe₂O₃, reduction peaks appeared at 280–420 °C (Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄) and 450–800 °C (further reduction to Fe). With Ce addition, high-temperature reduction peaks shifted to higher temperatures and increased in area, indicating stronger Fe–CeO₂ interaction and higher reduction temperatures.
- **Observations:** CeO₂ moderates the Fe–ZrO₂ interaction, making Fe species easier to reduce and improving their dispersion, which is advantageous for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to examine catalyst morphology and particle size distribution, which impact surface area and active site accessibility.
- **Findings:** SEM images confirmed the formation of uniform, microspherical nanoparticles with sizes ranging from 25 to 60 nm. The microwave-assisted homogeneous precipitation method, combined with Ce addition, promoted rapid nucleation and uniform particle growth.
- **Observations:** The uniform morphology and reduced particle size with higher Ce content contribute to increased surface defects and improved catalytic properties.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Composition and Electronic Structure
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) provided insights into surface elemental composition and the electronic environment of iron species, which influence catalyst reducibility and activity.
- **Findings:** The Fe 2p₃/₂ and Fe 2p₁/₂ binding energies were observed at 710.9 eV and 724.5 eV, characteristic of Fe³⁺ in Fe₂O₃. A satellite peak at 718.9 eV further confirmed Fe₂O₃ presence. Increasing Ce content led to a decrease in Fe 2p binding energies, indicating enhanced electron density at the iron nucleus due to electron donation from CeO₂. Surface atomic ratios for 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce were Fe: 11.38%, Zr: 3.65%, Ce: 1.03%, O: 46.62%, and C: 37.32%.
- **Observations:** The electronic interaction between Fe and CeO₂, evidenced by binding energy shifts, weakens Fe–ZrO₂ interaction and facilitates Fe reduction, supporting the role of CeO₂ as a structural and electronic promoter.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ feed transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 320 °C, 2 MPa, H₂/CO₂ = 3/1, and GHSV = 1000 h⁻¹, the 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce–K catalyst achieved a CO₂ conversion of 57.34%. Other formulations with varying Fe/Zr/Ce ratios exhibited lower conversions, with Ce-free samples showing significantly reduced activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Introducing Ce into Fe–Zr–K catalysts markedly enhances CO₂ conversion. The conversion increases with Ce content up to an optimal level (35Fe–7Zr–1Ce–K), beyond which further Ce addition does not yield additional improvement.

#### 6.2.2 Product Selectivity

##### 6.2.2.1 C₂–C₄ Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity toward C₂–C₄ olefins is defined as the percentage of converted CO₂ forming C₂–C₄ olefins:
  \[
  \text{C}_2\text{–C}_4 \text{ Olefin Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4\,\text{olefins}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce–K catalyst exhibited a C₂–C₄ olefin selectivity of 55.62%. The olefin/paraffin (O/P) ratio in this fraction reached 7.08, indicating a strong preference for olefin formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Ce addition significantly boosts light olefin selectivity, with the O/P ratio peaking at intermediate Ce content. Higher Ce content suppresses heavier hydrocarbon (C₅⁺) formation, further favoring light olefins.

##### 6.2.2.2 CO Selectivity
- **Description:** CO selectivity reflects the fraction of converted CO₂ yielding CO via the reverse water–gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce–K, CO selectivity was approximately 3%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher CO₂ conversion correlates with lower CO selectivity, indicating efficient subsequent conversion of CO to hydrocarbons.

##### 6.2.2.3 C₅⁺ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO₂ forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce–K catalyst showed a C₅⁺ selectivity of 15.87%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Ce content monotonically decreases C₅⁺ selectivity, reflecting a lower chain growth probability and improved light olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of hydrocarbon chain propagation versus termination, influencing product distribution. It is inferred from the hydrocarbon distribution profile.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not numerically reported, the trend is clear: the α factor decreases with increasing Ce content, as evidenced by reduced C₅⁺ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Ce addition tunes the catalyst to favor shorter-chain olefins, lowering the α factor and suppressing heavier hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO₂ conversion over time during extended operation (time-on-stream, TOS).
- **Experimental Data:** Performance was tracked over 96 hours with 12-hour sampling intervals. No explicit deactivation rate is provided, but the catalyst maintained stable CO₂ conversion and selectivity throughout the test period.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe–Zr–Ce–K catalysts demonstrate robust stability under reaction conditions, with negligible loss in activity or selectivity over prolonged operation.

#### 6.2.5 Additional Performance Indicators

##### 6.2.5.1 Olefin/Paraffin Ratio (O/P)
- **Description:** The O/P ratio in C₂–C₄ products quantifies the selectivity for olefins over paraffins:
  \[
  \text{O/P} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4\,\text{olefins}}}{F_{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4\,\text{paraffins}}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce–K catalyst achieved an O/P ratio of 7.08.
- **Observations and Trends:** This high O/P ratio underscores the catalyst’s effectiveness in selectively producing valuable light olefins.

---
**Summary:** The Fe–Zr–Ce–K catalyst series, particularly 35Fe–7Zr–1Ce–K, exhibits high CO₂ conversion, superior selectivity toward C₂–C₄ olefins, low CO and C₅⁺ selectivities, and excellent operational stability. Ce addition is pivotal in optimizing these performance metrics, offering a promising strategy for efficient CO₂ hydrogenation to light olefins.
