### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** The low-α potassium promoted iron catalyst, with a Fe : Si : K atomic ratio of 100 : 5.1 : 1.25, achieved a stable CO conversion of 80% after a 280-hour induction period at 230 °C when activated with pure CO. Under identical conditions, H₂ activation resulted in a stable CO conversion of 42% after 120 hours, while syngas activation yielded 52% after 320 hours.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO activation consistently produced the highest CO conversion, nearly doubling the value obtained with H₂ activation. The induction period required to reach stable conversion was shortest for H₂ activation but resulted in lower overall activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO activation led to the highest water–gas shift activity and thus the greatest CO₂ production among the tested activation gases. While specific numerical values are not provided, the trend is clear across activation conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The activation temperature had minimal effect on CO₂ selectivity for CO-activated catalysts, indicating that the activation gas type is the dominant factor.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For CO-activated catalysts, CH₄ selectivity was approximately 8% across the activation temperature range (230–270 °C), with a minimum of 7.6% at 250 °C. H₂ and syngas activation resulted in higher methane selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower methane selectivity with CO activation indicates a greater proportion of CO is converted to longer-chain hydrocarbons, enhancing hydrocarbon productivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_3^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO activation consistently resulted in higher C₃⁺ selectivity, as evidenced by increased yields in gasoline (C₅–C₁₁), diesel (C₁₂–C₁₈), and C₁₉⁺ fractions. H₂ activation favored shorter-chain products.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of potassium and silica, combined with CO activation, promoted chain growth and suppressed methane formation, leading to higher selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of hydrocarbon chain growth versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution. Higher α values indicate a preference for longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Data:** CO activation led to higher α values, correlating with increased selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons. H₂ activation resulted in lower α values and favored methane and short-chain products.
- **Observations and Trends:** The formation of the Fe₅C₂ (Hägg carbide) phase during CO activation is associated with higher α values and improved long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The CO-activated catalyst maintained a stable CO conversion of 84% at 200 hours, with only slight deactivation after 600 hours. In contrast, syngas- and H₂-activated catalysts exhibited more pronounced deactivation and lower stable conversions.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO activation provided the highest catalyst stability and lowest deactivation rate, with service life extending up to 350 days, compared to 200 days for syngas-pretreated catalysts.
