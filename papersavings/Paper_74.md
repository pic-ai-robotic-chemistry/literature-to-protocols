## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the 100Fe/5.1Si/2Cu/xK catalysts is executed using a two‐stage process that combines a precipitation method for the base Fe/Si support with sequential impregnation for the promoters. Below is a detailed breakdown of the experimental procedures:

#### • Base Catalyst via Precursor Mixing and Precipitation–Deposition
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Dissolve the iron and silicon precursors in a solvent under vigorous stirring.  
   - Maintain reagent concentrations as reported, ensuring a homogeneous solution.
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Add a precipitating agent dropwise to the solution to induce the formation of a precipitate.  
   - Observe the formation of a colored precipitate, which confirms the onset of catalyst formation.  
   - Allow for any deposition phenomena to occur prior to proceeding.

3. Washing and Collection  
   - Collect the precipitate using standard separation techniques.  
   - Rinse thoroughly to remove residual ions, ensuring purity.

#### • Sequential Impregnation of Promoters and Post‑processing
1. Cu Impregnation  
   - Impregnate the preformed Fe/Si support with a Cu precursor solution (Cu(NO₃)₂·2.5H₂O) at a constant atomic ratio (~2).  
   - Use a rotary evaporator for vacuum drying at 80 °C; gradually raise the temperature from 80 °C to 95 °C to enhance solvent removal without thermal shock.
2. K Impregnation  
   - Following Cu impregnation, introduce the K precursor (KNO₃, 99.9% purity) with amounts adjusted to yield atomic loadings of approximately 1.2, 1.8, 3.1, and 4.4% for x = 1, 2, 3, and 5, respectively.
3. Drying and Calcination  
   - After sequential impregnation, dry the catalyst thoroughly.  
   - Calcine the catalyst under an air flow at 350 °C for 4 hours.
4. Sieving  
   - Post‑calcination, sieve the catalyst to obtain a particle size distribution of 45–90 μm.

### Comparative Analysis
• The precipitation method offers simplicity and straightforward scalability, although detailed control over promoter distribution is limited.  
• Sequential impregnation, while adding extra processing steps, provides enhanced precision in Cu and K deposition, ultimately leading to improved catalyst performance.  

This structured protocol, with explicit process parameters and observations, ensures reproducible and accurate catalyst preparation as required for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis evaluation.

- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 270 °C, which facilitates the in‐situ reduction by CO.
- **Observations:** Operating at 270 °C provides a balanced environment that effectively exposes the active sites while preventing sintering, thereby promoting stable catalytic behavior during the reaction.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure

- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is performed at 1 atmosphere (1 atm), which mimics mild reaction conditions to ensure controlled reduction.
- **Observations:** A pressure of 1 atm ensures reproducible reduction kinetics, leading to reliable formation of active iron phases without excessive agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas

- **Experimental Conditions:** A pure carbon monoxide (CO) atmosphere is employed during activation.
- **Observations:** CO efficiently reduces the iron species, converting them into active metallic or carbidic phases. This environment is crucial for tailoring the active site dispersion and ensuring low methane selectivity in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is essential to achieve complete reduction without overexposure that might lead to detrimental sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is subjected to the activation process for a period of 24 hours.
- **Observations:** A 24-hour treatment period is sufficient to guarantee full activation. This prolonged exposure ensures thorough reduction while maintaining high catalyst stability and reproducibility in CO conversion performance.

Through this systematically structured activation protocol—leveraging a well-defined temperature, pressure, gas composition, and time—the process effectively prepares the 100Fe/5.1Si/2Cu/xK catalysts for optimal performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural features of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Rigaku Diffractometer (DMAX-B) employing Cu Kα radiation (1.54 Å). XRD is essential for identifying the presence and stability of active phases relevant to Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the reduced 100Fe/5.1Si/2Cu/xK catalysts (x = 1, 2, 3, 5) consistently show strong reflections at 2θ = 35.5° and 43.5°, corresponding to Fe₃O₄ (magnetite), and a smaller peak at 40°, attributed to Fe₂C₅ (iron carbide). The expected iron carbide peak at 42.6°–45° is not observed. Crystallite sizes of iron carbides, calculated using the Scherrer equation, are 20.5 nm (1 K), 16.3 nm (2 K), 15.0 nm (3 K), and 17.1 nm (5 K).
- **Observations:** The presence of Fe₃O₄ and a small, stable iron carbide phase across all K loadings indicates that the active sites for CO dissociation and chain growth are maintained. The uniformity in crystallite size suggests that K loading does not significantly alter the bulk structure.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES) was used to verify the elemental composition of the catalysts, ensuring consistency with nominal formulations and precise promoter loading.
- **Findings:** ICP-OES analysis confirms that Fe, Si, and Cu contents remain constant across all samples, while K content increases with x: 1.2 at.% (1 K), 1.8 at.% (2 K), 3.1 at.% (3 K), and 4.4 at.% (5 K).
- **Observations:** The sequential impregnation method effectively controls K loading without affecting other elemental concentrations, enabling systematic investigation of K’s influence on catalytic properties.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area
- **Description:** The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was employed to determine the specific surface area, a key parameter for catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** All Fe-K catalysts exhibit a BET surface area of approximately 110 m²/g.
- **Observations:** The high and consistent surface area across all samples provides a large number of active sites, supporting efficient Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.4 Iron Phase Composition and Stability
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was utilized to identify and quantify iron phases present after reaction and to assess phase stability under varying CO conversion conditions.
- **Findings:** Three stable iron phases are detected: χ-Fe₅C₂ (carbide), ε′-Fe₂.₂C (carbide), and Fe₃O₄ (magnetite). The relative proportions of these phases remain nearly unchanged after extended operation at both high (80%) and low (30%) CO conversion.
- **Observations:** The persistence of these phases under reaction conditions demonstrates the structural robustness of the catalysts, ensuring that observed catalytic trends are due to surface chemical effects rather than bulk phase changes.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = 100 \times \frac{N_{\text{CO,in}} - N_{\text{CO,out}}}{N_{\text{CO,in}}}
  \]
  where \(N_{\text{CO,in}}\) and \(N_{\text{CO,out}}\) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** The 100Fe/5.1Si/2Cu/xK catalysts (x = 1, 2, 3, 5) were evaluated over a broad CO conversion range of 4–85% under 260–270 °C, 1.3 MPa, and H₂/CO = 0.67.
- **Observations and Trends:** This wide conversion range enabled assessment of catalyst behavior at both low and high conversions, providing insight into selectivity trends and catalyst stability.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{N_{\text{CO,in}} - N_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 260 °C, CH₄ selectivity decreased from 5.3% for the 1K catalyst to 2.1% for the 5K catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing K loading significantly suppresses CH₄ formation, indicating enhanced CO dissociation and chain growth. The effect of CO conversion on CH₄ selectivity is nearly eliminated at the highest K loading.

#### 6.2.3 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{N_{\text{CO,in}} - N_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Higher K loading (up to x = 3) led to increased C₃⁺ selectivity, as evidenced by product distributions favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The suppression of CH₄ selectivity with increased K directly correlates with enhanced C₃⁺ selectivity, reflecting improved chain growth.

#### 6.2.4 Oxygenate Selectivity
- **Description:** Oxygenate selectivity is the percentage of converted CO forming oxygenated products (mainly alcohols, with ethanol dominant).
- **Experimental Data:** Oxygenate selectivity remained below 3% across all catalysts, exhibiting a volcano-shaped dependence on CO conversion—peaking at 20–50% CO conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing K loading up to x = 3 enhanced oxygenate selectivity, with the highest values at intermediate CO conversions. The selectivity trend is consistent with a CO insertion mechanism.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory (ASF) model, quantifies the probability of chain growth versus termination (0 < α < 1).
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values for oxygenates increased from 0.32 at 4.3% CO conversion to 0.79 at 16.1%, then slightly decreased at higher conversions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher K loading and intermediate CO conversions favor longer-chain oxygenate formation, as reflected by increased α values.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring phase composition and performance reproducibility over time.
- **Experimental Data:** Mössbauer and XRD analyses confirmed that the phase composition (Fe₃O₄, χ-Fe₅C₂, ε′-Fe₂.₂C) remained nearly constant across the CO conversion range. Repeated runs yielded consistent results, with mass balances maintained at 100 ± 2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalysts demonstrated robust stability, with negligible deactivation and reproducible selectivity under all tested conditions.
