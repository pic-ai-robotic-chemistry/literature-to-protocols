## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details two distinct synthesis routes for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, emphasizing reproducibility through strict control of reaction parameters and equipment.

#### • In Situ Deposition Method

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Fine Fe powder is introduced into a steel cylinder purged with syngas.  
   - The system is pressurized to 10 MPa and maintained at 60–80 °C for approximately one month to generate Fe(CO)x.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - The Fe(CO)x-containing syngas is continuously fed into a fixed-bed reactor pre-loaded with SiO₂.  
   - Under Fischer–Tropsch conditions (280–320 °C, 1 MPa, H₂/CO/N₂ = 48.5/48.5/3), the iron carbonyl decomposes directly into metallic Fe which is simultaneously carburized into the active χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase.
   - No separate drying or calcination steps are required, minimizing potential interference from Fe oxide–SiO₂ interactions.

3. Observations  
   - Rapid deposition and immediate carburization; high χ‐Fe₅C₂ content (up to 88.5%) is obtained.
   - Process integration ensures catalyst synthesis and reaction occur simultaneously, circumventing the need for pre‑reduction.

#### • Conventional Impregnation Method

1. Precursor Mixing  
   - An aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O is prepared and uniformly mixed with the SiO₂ support.

2. Drying  
   - The impregnated support is dried in a rotary evaporator under vacuum at 80 °C for 2 hours, followed by drying at 120 °C overnight.
  
3. Calcination  
   - The dried catalyst is calcined in air at 400 °C for 3 hours, facilitating the formation of iron oxides.
  
4. Post‑Treatment (Reduction)  
   - The calcined material is reduced at 350 °C in a flow of H₂ at 0.2 MPa to convert Fe oxides; however, strong Fe oxide–SiO₂ interactions often lead to incomplete reduction and formation of Fe₃O₄.
  
5. Observations  
   - Sequential post‑treatments lead to lower active χ‐Fe₅C₂ content (14.5%), due to the sluggish reduction and subsequent carburization.

The comparative analysis demonstrates that while the in situ deposition method simplifies synthesis and enhances active phase formation, the conventional impregnation route offers ease of scale but may suffer from incomplete reduction. These process parameters and equipment details ensure that another researcher can reliably replicate the procedures as reported.

- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for achieving catalyst reduction and subsequent carburization into the active χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase. Elevated temperatures promote the decomposition of precursors but must be controlled to avoid excessive sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:**  
  • For the in situ deposition method, Fischer–Tropsch reaction conditions are leveraged for activation, typically at either 280 °C or 320 °C. These temperatures facilitate the direct decomposition of Fe(CO)x and immediate carburization of metallic Fe on the SiO₂ support.  
  • For the conventional impregnation method, catalyst activation is achieved by reducing the calcined catalyst at 350 °C in a hydrogen atmosphere.  
- **Observations:** In the in situ process, operating at 320 °C enhances carburization efficiency compared to 280 °C, though higher temperatures accelerate sintering of the fine Fe nanoparticles. #### Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Pressure during the activation phase influences gas-solid interactions and affects the kinetics of reduction and carburization.

- **Observations:** The balanced reductive environment provided by the H₂/CO = 1.0 mixture in the in situ method ensures immediate and efficient carburization, while the selected flow rate maintains adequate catalyst exposure. - **Definition:** Activation time is critical to ensure complete transformation without excessive catalyst sintering.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Textural Properties: Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** The surface area, pore volume, and pore size of the catalysts and supports were evaluated using N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms at −196 °C, with BET and BJH analyses providing quantitative textural data. These parameters are critical for understanding catalyst dispersion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** The pristine SiO₂ support exhibited a high surface area of 185.6 m²/g, a pore volume of 1.16 cm³/g, and an average pore size of 18.9 nm. Upon Fe loading, the 5.3Fe/SiO₂ catalyst maintained a similar surface area (189.1 m²/g) but showed reduced pore volume (0.93 cm³/g) and pore size (15.5 nm). In contrast, in situ deposited Fe catalysts (100Fe and 99.2Fe₀.₈K) displayed significantly lower surface areas (51.3–51.7 m²/g) and pore volumes (0.20–0.22 cm³/g), with average pore sizes of 13.1 nm.
- **Observations:** The decrease in surface area and porosity after Fe deposition, especially for in situ methods, reflects the partial filling or blockage of SiO₂ pores by Fe nanoparticles, which can influence catalyst dispersion and reactivity.

#### 6.1.2 Crystal Phase Composition and Evolution
- **Description:** X-ray diffraction (XRD) and ^57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy were employed to identify and quantify the crystalline phases and iron species present in the catalysts at various synthesis and reaction stages. These techniques are essential for correlating phase composition with catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of in situ deposited Fe/SiO₂ catalysts revealed strong diffraction peaks for the active χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase (notably at 2θ = 43.2°, 43.4°, and 44.1°, corresponding to the (021) and (510) facets), with no detectable Fe₃O₄ in spent catalysts. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified χ‐Fe₅C₂ content up to 88.5% in in situ catalysts, while impregnated 5.3Fe/SiO₂ showed only 14.5% χ‐Fe₅C₂ and 47.0% Fe₃O₄ after reaction. The addition of K as a promoter in 99.2Fe₀.₈K catalysts further enhanced the formation of χ‐Fe₅C₂, with XRD and Mössbauer confirming the near-complete conversion of Fe₃O₄ to χ‐Fe₅C₂ at 320 °C.
- **Observations:** The synthesis route critically determines the phase evolution: in situ deposition directly yields the active carbide phase, while conventional impregnation leads to incomplete reduction and lower active phase content. The presence of χ‐Fe₅C₂ is directly linked to higher Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Dispersion of Fe Nanoparticles
- **Description:** High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and elemental mapping were used to assess the size, distribution, and uniformity of Fe nanoparticles on SiO₂. Morphological control is vital for maximizing active site exposure and minimizing sintering.
- **Findings:** In situ deposited catalysts exhibited two populations of Fe nanoparticles: fine, uniformly dispersed particles and larger, discretely located aggregates. Smaller SiO₂ support particles (75–125 μm) favored the formation of uniformly dispersed fine Fe nanoparticles, while larger supports (420–840 μm) led to larger Fe aggregates. In contrast, impregnated 5.3Fe/SiO₂ catalysts showed better overall dispersion and resistance to sintering, attributed to stronger Fe oxide–SiO₂ interactions.
- **Observations:** The in situ method enables high initial dispersion but is prone to sintering due to weaker metal–support interactions, impacting long-term stability. The choice of support particle size and synthesis method thus directly affects catalyst morphology and performance.

#### 6.1.4 Carbon Deposition and Thermal Stability
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric (TG) analysis was conducted to monitor carbon deposition and phase changes in spent catalysts by measuring weight changes during heating in an O₂ atmosphere.
- **Findings:** The 100Fe-320 °C catalyst showed a rapid weight increase (oxidation of χ‐Fe₅C₂ to Fe₂O₃) followed by significant weight loss at ~334 °C, attributed to the oxidation of deposited carbon. The K-promoted 99.2Fe₀.₈K-320 °C catalyst exhibited similar behavior, with carbon oxidation starting at a lower temperature (246 °C), indicating that K facilitates carbon removal. Minimal carbon deposition was observed at lower reaction temperatures (e.g., 100Fe-280 °C).
- **Observations:** TG results highlight the influence of reaction temperature and promoter addition on carbon deposition and removal, which are important for catalyst regeneration and longevity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The in situ deposited Fe/SiO₂ catalyst exhibited high initial CO conversion, reaching approximately 60% after 60 hours on stream under standard Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) conditions (280–320 °C, 1.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.0). In contrast, the conventional impregnated 5.3Fe/SiO₂ catalyst showed significantly lower CO conversion under identical conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The rapid formation of the active χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase in the in situ catalyst accounts for its superior initial activity. However, CO conversion for this catalyst declines over time due to sintering, while the impregnated catalyst, with slower carburization, displays a gradual increase in conversion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The in situ deposited catalyst, rich in χ‐Fe₅C₂, demonstrated lower CO₂ selectivity compared to the impregnated catalyst, which contains a higher fraction of Fe₃O₄.
- **Observations and Trends:** Elevated Fe₃O₄ content in the impregnated catalyst promotes the water-gas shift reaction, increasing CO₂ formation. In contrast, the χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase in the in situ catalyst favors hydrocarbon production, suppressing CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Both catalyst types exhibited similar CH₄ selectivity, with no substantial differences observed between the in situ and impregnated samples.
- **Observations and Trends:** The main selectivity differences between catalysts are observed in higher hydrocarbons and CO₂, rather than methane.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_3^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The in situ deposited catalyst, with a high χ‐Fe₅C₂ content (up to 88.5%), achieved higher C₃⁺ selectivity than the impregnated catalyst (14.5% χ‐Fe₅C₂, 47% Fe₃O₄).
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced chain growth and longer hydrocarbon formation are directly linked to the prevalence of the χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, represents the probability of chain growth:
  \[
  W_n = n(1-\alpha)^2 \alpha^{n-1}
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** Catalysts with higher χ‐Fe₅C₂ content (in situ deposited) exhibited higher α values, indicating a greater tendency for long-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor correlates positively with the proportion of active carbide phase, reflecting improved selectivity toward heavier hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion or iron time yield (FTY) over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The in situ deposited catalyst showed a high initial FTY (21.3 gHC·gFe⁻¹·h⁻¹ at 1 h), which rapidly decreased to 1.3 gHC·gFe⁻¹·h⁻¹ after 60 h. The impregnated catalyst maintained a lower but more stable FTY (1.1 gHC·gFe⁻¹·h⁻¹ at 60 h).
- **Observations and Trends:** Rapid deactivation in the in situ catalyst is attributed to sintering from weak metal–support interactions, while the impregnated catalyst’s stronger interactions confer greater stability despite lower initial activity.
