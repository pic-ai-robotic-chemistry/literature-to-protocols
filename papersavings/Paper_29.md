## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines the incipient wetness impregnation method used for synthesizing Co–Ni nanoparticles supported on reducible oxides (TiO₂, Nb₂O₅) and nonreducible α-Al₂O₃. This technique is valued for its simplicity, scalability, and cost‑effectiveness compared to more complex methods such as sol‑gel or co‑precipitation.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation Procedure

1. • Support Pre‑treatment  
  – Dry the catalyst support under vacuum at 80 °C for 1 hour using a fixed bed reactor. This step removes moisture and ensures uniform impregnation.  

2. • Precursor Mixing / Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
  – Prepare an aqueous solution of cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) and nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) in Milli‑Q water.  
  – Maintain the desired Ni/(Ni + Co) ratios (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 atom/atom). For formulations incorporating Zr promoters, include Zr(NO₃)₄·5H₂O alongside the metal nitrates.  
  – Impregnate the pre‑dried support with this solution at room temperature, ensuring uniform distribution; no separate Precipitation–Deposition step is applied.

3. • Drying Process  
  – Dry the impregnated support at 60 °C for 2 hours with a controlled heating rate of 5 °C·min⁻¹. This controlled drying minimizes agglomeration of the precursors and preserves homogeneity.

4. • Calcination  
  – Calcine the dried catalyst at 350 °C for 2 hours using a heating rate of 3 °C·min⁻¹. This step is crucial for removing residual solvent, forming the desired oxide phases, and enhancing metal dispersion.

### Comparative Analysis

• Advantages:  
 – The incipient wetness method offers reproducible metal loading and uniform nanoparticle distribution, thereby ensuring consistent catalyst properties.  
• Considerations:  
 – Although simpler and more cost‑effective, careful control of drying and calcination conditions is critical to maintain the optimal catalyst structure and activity.

This structured method, with its precise numerical values and environmental controls, enables reliable replication and consistency in producing Fischer–Tropsch catalysts with tailored performance characteristics.

- **Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed the required amount with the same precision instrument to ensure the correct Ni/(Ni + Co) ratio.  

##### 3.1.1.2 Catalyst B – Co–Ni on Nonreducible α-Al₂O₃

- **Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed the requisite mass under identical conditions to achieve the desired atomic ratio.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Catalyst A – Co–Ni on Reducible Oxides  
- **Milli‑Q Water**: Poured the measured volume of Milli‑Q water into a calibrated beaker to dissolve the nitrates, preparing the impregnation solution at room temperature.

##### 3.1.2.2 Catalyst B – Co–Ni on Nonreducible α‑Al₂O₃  
- **Milli‑Q Water**: Transferred the required volume using a graduated cylinder, ensuring precise dosing for the impregnation step.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Catalyst A – Co–Ni on Reducible Oxides  
- **Precursor Solution**: Mixed the aqueous nitrate solution with the pre‐dried support material by stirring uniformly at ambient conditions for 30 minutes. This step promotes homogeneous dispersion of metal precursors prior to drying.

##### 3.1.3.2 Catalyst B – Co–Ni on Nonreducible α‑Al₂O₃  
- **Precursor Solution**: Stirred the combined mixture at room temperature for 30 minutes under atmospheric conditions, ensuring full penetration of the solution into the support’s porous network.

  • The prepared aqueous precursor solution is combined with pretreated support material (TiO₂, Nb₂O₅, or α-Al₂O₃).
- **Conditions**:  
  • Support is dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 1 hour prior to impregnation.
  • Impregnation is performed at room temperature.

  • Impregnate the dried support with the precursor solution, ensuring uniform distribution.

- **Key Steps**:

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is conducted as a pre‐reaction treatment to convert cobalt and nickel oxides into their metallic active states. This in situ reduction is carefully executed to ensure a uniform exposure of the catalyst to the reducing gas while maintaining optimal dispersion and preventing sintering.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
The catalysts are activated at 350 °C. This temperature is critical for achieving full reduction of the metal oxides while avoiding excessive thermal stress that could encourage particle sintering. The selected temperature is distinct from higher-temperature thermal programs used in characterization, ensuring that the reduction process effectively exposes the active sites necessary for the reaction.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
Activation is performed under an ambient pressure of 1 bar. This controlled setting guarantees a stable environment for the reduction process, bridging conditions between catalyst preparation and the subsequent reaction phase. Operating at 1 bar facilitates efficient reduction kinetics without inducing morphological changes that might detract from catalyst performance.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition and Space-Time Velocity
Two reducing gas compositions are employed depending on the experimental setup. For low-pressure tests, the catalyst is exposed to an Ar/H₂ mixture in a 2:1 volume ratio, while high-pressure experiments utilize a 25 vol % H₂ in He mixture. The flow is adjusted to deliver a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 60,000 h⁻¹, ensuring rapid and uniform contact between the reducing gas and catalyst particles. This uniformity helps maintain the integrity of the metal nanoparticles and supports effective reduction.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
For low-pressure operation, the activation is maintained at 350 °C for 2 hours, a duration that provides complete oxide reduction with minimal sintering. In contrast, high-pressure experiments extend the reduction to 8 hours under similar temperature conditions, adapting to the distinct reactor dynamics. In both cases, the controlled time frame is validated by subsequent hydrogen chemisorption results, which confirm the effective transformation of the catalyst without undesirable agglomeration.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
• **Definition:** This parameter indicates the temperature at which the Fischer–Tropsch reaction is conducted. It is critical for activating syngas and influencing catalyst performance and product selectivity.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** Two reactor configurations were used. In the quartz glass plug-flow reactor, the temperature was maintained at 220 °C. In the stainless steel reactor, the temperature was varied between 220 °C and 260 °C.  
• **Observations:** At 220 °C, catalysts supported on reducible oxides (TiO₂ and Nb₂O₅) demonstrated high selectivity toward long-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺), whereas an increase toward 260 °C led to a moderate rise in methane formation, indicating a trade-off between hydrocarbon chain growth and methane selectivity.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
• **Definition:** Reaction pressure affects the concentration of syngas and is a key factor in determining the catalytic activity and selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The quartz glass plug-flow reactor experiments were performed at a low pressure of 1 bar. In contrast, stainless steel reactor experiments were carried out under high-pressure conditions at 20 bar.  
• **Observations:** The high-pressure environment (20 bar) notably enhanced the activity and selectivity of cobalt-rich catalysts, particularly on reducible oxide supports, leading to improved yields of long-chain hydrocarbons. Conversely, catalysts supported on nonreducible α-Al₂O₃ exhibited higher methane selectivity and a tendency toward deactivation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
• **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is essential for balancing the reaction stoichiometry in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, thereby influencing the distribution of hydrocarbon products.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** A constant H₂/CO ratio of 2 (v/v) was maintained throughout all catalytic tests.  
• **Observations:** Maintaining the H₂/CO ratio at 2 promoted the formation of long-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺) while controlling excessive methane production, thereby optimizing the overall catalytic performance.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and crystallite size of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD). This technique is essential for identifying the metallic or oxide phases present and for estimating nanoparticle size, which influences catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** XRD confirmed the presence of metallic phases in the reduced Co–Ni catalysts. For Ru-based reference catalysts, the phase remained metallic Ru (JCPDS, 06-0663) regardless of Zr promoter addition, with no crystalline ZrO₂ detected. Crystallite sizes, calculated from XRD data, were reported for various samples (e.g., 8.5 nm for fresh 0Zr/Ru, 6.0 nm for reduced 0Zr/Ru). For Co–Ni catalysts, XRD verified alloy formation and provided crystallite size estimates, though specific peak positions for Co–Ni were not detailed.
- **Observations:** The incipient wetness impregnation method, followed by calcination and reduction, yielded smaller crystallite sizes with increasing Ni content, particularly on reducible supports.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were measured using N₂ physisorption at –196 °C, employing the BET method for surface area and BJH analysis for porosity. These parameters affect reactant accessibility and catalyst dispersion.
- **Findings:** For Zr-promoted Ru catalysts, BET surface areas ranged from 280.0 to 287.7 m²·g⁻¹. Samples were degassed at 300 °C for 10 hours prior to measurement. Pore volume and average pore diameter were determined by BJH analysis under the same conditions.
- **Observations:** The surface area of the supports remained largely unchanged with varying metal ratios, but metal dispersion increased with higher Ni content, especially on α-Al₂O₃.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction Behavior (TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was used to assess the reducibility of the catalysts, providing insight into the ease of reduction of metal oxides to their active metallic forms.
- **Findings:** Monometallic cobalt catalysts exhibited two distinct H₂ uptake peaks: the first (225–250 °C) for Co₃O₄→CoO and the second (up to 350–500 °C) for CoO→Co. Bimetallic Co–Ni catalysts showed reduction peaks shifted to lower temperatures, especially on TiO₂ and Nb₂O₅, indicating that Ni promotes cobalt reduction.
- **Observations:** The facilitation of reduction by Ni was more pronounced on reducible supports, correlating with improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning transmission electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDX) were employed to analyze nanoparticle size distribution and elemental mapping.
- **Findings:** TEM analysis (≥500 particles/sample) revealed that increasing Ni content led to smaller, more homogeneously alloyed nanoparticles. STEM-EDX confirmed uniform Co–Ni distribution post-reduction, with a tendency for cobalt surface enrichment on reducible supports under CO atmosphere.
- **Observations:** The smallest particles were observed at intermediate Ni/(Ni + Co) ratios (0.5–0.75), and strong metal–support interaction (SMSI) was evident on TiO₂ and Nb₂O₅, partially covering metal surfaces.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition and Surface Analysis
- **Description:** Elemental composition was validated by XRF and ICP-OES, while surface composition and CO adsorption properties were probed by FTIR of adsorbed CO and H₂ chemisorption.
- **Findings:** The intended Co/Ni ratios were maintained before and after catalysis, confirming catalyst stability. FTIR spectra of adsorbed CO at 220 °C revealed distinct linear-CO bands for Co and Ni, with the ratio of band areas reflecting surface composition. On reducible supports, higher CO stretching frequencies indicated weaker CO binding and a shift in the rate-determining step for FT synthesis.
- **Observations:** SMSI effects on reducible supports led to discrepancies between theoretical and experimental H₂ uptake, indicating partial coverage of metal nanoparticles by support suboxides.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch conditions (220 °C, 1 bar, H₂/CO = 2), Co–Ni catalysts supported on reducible oxides (TiO₂, Nb₂O₅) exhibit significantly higher CO conversion compared to those on nonreducible α-Al₂O₃. For reference, Ru-based catalysts with Zr promotion achieve CO conversions up to 45.4% (Zr-Ru/SiO₂-0Na) versus 24.5% (Ru/SiO₂-0Na). While exact numerical values for Co–Ni systems are not specified, TiO₂- and Nb₂O₅-supported catalysts consistently outperform α-Al₂O₃-supported analogues, which display markedly lower conversion and rapid deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Ni content up to Ni/(Ni + Co) = 0.75 on reducible supports enhances activity, with the highest conversion observed at intermediate Ni ratios (0.25–0.5). On α-Al₂O₃, activity remains low regardless of composition.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Across all tested catalysts, CO₂ selectivity remains negligible, particularly for Ru-based and Co–Ni systems, regardless of support.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low CO₂ selectivity indicates minimal water–gas shift activity, with no significant dependence on support or metal composition.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity is highest for α-Al₂O₃-supported catalysts, increasing with Ni content. On reducible supports, CH₄ selectivity remains low at intermediate Ni/(Ni + Co) ratios but rises with higher Ni content or temperature.
- **Observations and Trends:** Reducible supports suppress methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons, while nonreducible supports and high Ni content promote methane.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity denotes the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** On TiO₂ and Nb₂O₅, C₅⁺ selectivity reaches ~80 wt% at 1 bar for intermediate Co–Ni ratios. α-Al₂O₃-supported catalysts show much lower C₅⁺ selectivity, with product distribution shifting toward methane and light hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal C₅⁺ selectivity is achieved at Ni/(Ni + Co) = 0.25–0.5 on reducible supports. Higher temperature or excessive Ni content reduces C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth versus termination, influencing hydrocarbon length. Higher α values favor long-chain products.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, high C₅⁺ selectivity (~80 wt%) on reducible supports at intermediate Ni content implies α values approaching those of monometallic Co catalysts. Trends indicate α: 0.25 > 0.50 > 0.0 > 0.75 (Nb₂O₅) and 0.25 > 0.0 > 0.50 > 0.75 (TiO₂).
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate Ni addition increases α, but excessive Ni or higher temperature lowers it, shifting selectivity toward lighter products.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by the change in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time}} \ (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** On α-Al₂O₃, monometallic Co catalysts lose ~25% activity, while monometallic Ni catalysts lose up to 60% during the reaction. In contrast, TiO₂- and Nb₂O₅-supported catalysts maintain stable activity over extended periods.
- **Observations and Trends:** Reducible supports confer superior stability and resistance to deactivation, while nonreducible supports are prone to rapid activity loss, especially with higher Ni content.
