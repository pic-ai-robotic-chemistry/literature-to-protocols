## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalysts are synthesized primarily by a microemulsion methodology to yield homogeneous mixed oxides, with an alternative conventional precipitation method used to prepare the p‑100Fe reference sample. Both methods follow a structured sequence of steps that ensure reproducibility and consistent catalyst properties.

#### • Microemulsion Synthesis Method
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Reagents: Iron nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃) and ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH, assay 28.0–30.0%) are combined with an appropriate manganese precursor to allow Mn incorporation into the hematite lattice.  
   - Conditions: The transparent mixture is stirred and allowed to remain stable for at least 24 hours.
   
2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - Process: Following mixing, the mixture is allowed to decant overnight. A controlled precipitation–deposition step is performed where the support precursor is added dropwise with continuous stirring, ensuring homogeneous deposition before solid recovery.  
   - Recovery: The precipitate is recovered by centrifugation and washed using distilled water and ethanol to remove impurities.

3. **Drying**  
   - Conditions: Drying is performed overnight at 383 K for 16 hours.

4. **Calcination**  
   - For unpromoted samples: Calcination is conducted in air at 773 K for 6 hours to achieve a stable hematite phase.  
   - For promoted samples: Following the initial calcination, promoters (potassium, sodium, and/or copper via carbonate or nitrate precursors) are added by wet impregnation. After impregnation, the samples are dried at 383 K for 16 hours and subsequently calcined at 773 K for 2 hours.

#### • Conventional Precipitation Method (p‑100Fe)
- This reference synthesis involves precursor mixing with NH₄OH to precipitate iron oxides, followed by similar drying (383 K, 16 hours) and calcination (773 K for 6 hours) steps, but lacks the nano‐confinement effects and homogeneous mixing achieved through the microemulsion process.

### Comparative Analysis
The microemulsion method offers advantages in terms of higher surface area and uniform elemental distribution, enhancing catalytic performance compared to the conventional technique. The detailed sequence—from mixing and deposition through drying and calcination—is designed to ensure reproducibility and ease of scaling, while maintaining rigorous control over reaction conditions and promoter loadings.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 95Fe Catalyst  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 10.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:

  • Perform co-precipitation at ambient temperature using the microemulsion reactor setup  
- **Process**:  
  • Combine precursor solutions ensuring that promoters are added after initial precursor synthesis for promoted catalysts (e.g., 95Fe-K, 95Fe-Na, 95Fe-Cu, 95Fe-CuK).

##### Step 3: Purification  
- **Process**:

• Monitor the pH via NH₄OH addition to avoid uncontrolled precipitation.  
• Follow the defined reagent addition sequence to optimize promoter dispersion and avoid agglomeration.

## 4. Characterization Methods

- **Instruments and Models**: Seifert 3000 XRD diffractometer equipped with a PW goniometer featuring Bragg–Brentano u/2u geometry, an automatic slit, and a bent graphite monochromator.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Diffraction patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 5–95°.
- **Test Parameters**: A step size of 0.058° with a scanning time of 2 seconds per step.
- **Key Notes**: Although specific sample pre-treatment is not detailed, the catalysts are prepared in a form suitable for XRD, and proper sample mounting is vital to maintain alignment and avoid measurement errors.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: Nitrogen adsorption isotherms were measured to determine the specific surface area and porosity of the catalysts.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2000 instrument.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed at 77 K after degassing the samples at 413 K for 12 hours.
- **Test Parameters**: Standard nitrogen adsorption procedures were followed.
- **Key Notes**: The degassing step is essential to remove all adsorbed species, ensuring that the BET measurements accurately reflect the catalysts’ accessible surface area.

### 4.3 H₂‑Temperature Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR was employed to assess the reduction characteristics of the catalysts, tracing the transformation of Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ and ultimately to metallic Fe. An observed shoulder peak near 800 K indicates the influence of incorporated manganese on the reduction kinetics.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics 3000 apparatus equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).
- **Characterization Conditions**: The sample (20–35 mg) underwent a pretreatment in dry air at 383 K for 1 hour before being heated under a 10 vol.% H₂/Ar flow.
- **Test Parameters**: Temperature ramp from room temperature to 1173 K at 10 K/min with the effluent gas cooled via a cold trap prior to TCD analysis.
- **Key Notes**: These conditions enable clear differentiation between reduction steps and highlight subtle effects due to manganese.

### 4.4 Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (SEM‑EDS)
- **Operational Description**: SEM‑EDS analyses were performed to map elemental distribution and/or surface morphology, particularly to detect agglomerates in potassium-promoted catalysts.
- **Instruments and Models**: Philips XL30 SEM coupled with an EDAX DX4i analyser.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Analysis was carried out at an accelerating voltage of 20 keV.
- **Test Parameters**: Prior to imaging, powder samples were sputter coated with gold using a SC502 Sputter Coater.
- **Key Notes**: While SEM‑EDS effectively confirms catalyst dispersion, sodium detection remains limited due to inherent EDS sensitivities.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for reducing the iron oxide precursors and forming the active carbide sites necessary for effective CO adsorption during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are activated in situ at 553 K for 1 hour under a controlled syngas atmosphere.  
- **Observations:** At this temperature, copper is reduced to its metallic state, which aids in the subsequent reduction of hematite to magnetite and promotes the partial carbide formation required for high catalytic activity.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the gas-catalyst interaction during the pre-reaction treatment, ensuring that the reduction process closely mimics the conditions used during the actual reaction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is carried out at atmospheric pressure. Following activation, the reactor is pressurized to 1.01 MPa to simulate the Fischer–Tropsch reaction environment.  
- **Observations:** This two-step pressure protocol ensures that the catalyst is uniformly reduced before it is subjected to higher reaction pressures, thereby optimizing performance without inducing excessive catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
- **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas is vital for controlling the reduction kinetics and facilitating balanced carbide formation.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation gas is a syngas mixture composed of 31% CO, 62% H₂, and 7% N₂.  
- **Observations:** This mixture effectively reduces the iron oxide phases while inducing the formation of carbide sites, particularly enhancing the performance of copper-promoted catalysts by increasing the number of active sites.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) regulates the residence time of the activation gas over the catalyst, ensuring consistent and complete activation across the catalyst bed.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 0.0023 L/(gcat·s) is maintained during the activation step.  
- **Observations:** This controlled flow rate prevents the occurrence of hot spots and guarantees a uniform reduction environment throughout the catalyst bed.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process is optimized to achieve full catalyst transformation without over-reduction or significant sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to the activation conditions for 1 hour.  
- **Observations:** This duration is sufficient to develop active carbide species, while careful control of temperature and flow conditions minimizes the risk of sintering, thereby preserving catalyst stability and performance.

- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 2 was employed during the catalytic tests.
- **Observations:** The chosen ratio is pivotal in steering the reaction pathway toward desirable products. The article indicates that with this ratio, there is a balanced promotion of methane formation and olefin production, particularly when promoters like alkali metals are used.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, also expressed as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), defines the rate at which syngas is introduced relative to the catalyst mass, thereby influencing contact time and conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 0.0023 L g⁻¹ s⁻¹ was maintained during the tests.
- **Observations:** Although high GHSV can sometimes lead to reduced contact time and lower CO conversion, the experimental design, including catalyst dilution strategies, was implemented to mitigate hot spots and maintain catalyst performance throughout the reaction.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), using a Seifert 3000 diffractometer (5–95° 2θ, 0.05° step size, 2 s per step, Bragg–Brentano geometry). XRD is essential for identifying crystalline phases and assessing the incorporation of dopants.
- **Findings:** All calcined samples, including microemulsion-prepared (100Fe, 95Fe, 95Fe-K, 95Fe-Na, 95Fe-Cu, 95Fe-CuK) and conventionally prepared (p-100Fe), exhibited only reflections corresponding to hematite (Fe₂O₃, JCPDS 84–307). No distinct peaks for isolated manganese oxide phases were detected, indicating successful incorporation of Mn into the hematite lattice. The lattice parameters remained largely unchanged with Mn addition, consistent with the known ability of hematite to accommodate up to 15 at.% Mn.
- **Observations:** Calcination at 773 K stabilized the hematite phase, and subsequent promoter addition (K, Na, Cu) did not alter the crystal structure, as their concentrations were below XRD detection limits.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Particle Size
- **Description:** Specific surface area was measured by N₂ adsorption using the BET method (Micromeritics ASAP 2000). Crystallite size was estimated from XRD peak broadening via the Debye–Scherrer equation. These parameters are critical for catalytic performance, influencing dispersion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** Microemulsion-prepared samples exhibited higher BET surface areas (14–30 m²/g) than conventionally precipitated p-100Fe. The iron–manganese oxides (e.g., 95Fe) had higher surface areas than pure iron (100Fe), attributed to smaller crystallite sizes (27 nm for Mn-containing vs. 38 nm for Mn-free samples). Potassium slightly decreased surface area, sodium increased it, and copper had minimal effect.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface area and reduced particle size in microemulsion-derived catalysts reflect improved dispersion, which is beneficial for Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was performed to assess the reducibility of iron species, using a Micrometrics 3000 instrument with TCD detection. Reducibility impacts the formation of active phases under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** Two main reduction peaks were observed: Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ and Fe₃O₄ to Fe⁰. For 100Fe, the area ratio of these peaks was 8.4, matching theoretical expectations. In Mn-containing samples (95Fe), a shoulder near 800 K indicated the reduction of a FexMn₁₋ₓO mixed oxide, and the overall reduction ended at a higher temperature, showing that Mn retards iron oxide reduction. Copper addition shifted the reduction temperature 150 K lower, facilitating reduction.
- **Observations:** The presence of Mn leads to the formation of mixed oxides and slower reduction, while Cu promotion enhances reducibility, both of which influence catalytic behavior.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Elemental Distribution (SEM-EDS)
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were used to examine particle morphology and elemental distribution. These techniques reveal homogeneity and the presence of surface features.
- **Findings:** SEM-EDS confirmed a homogeneous distribution of Fe and Mn in microemulsion-prepared catalysts. Potassium-promoted samples exhibited surface agglomerates poor in Mn, while sodium could not be detected by EDS due to instrumental limitations.
- **Observations:** The microemulsion method yields well-dispersed, homogeneous mixed oxides, though potassium promotion can induce surface inhomogeneities.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch conditions (573 K, 1.01 MPa, H₂/CO = 2), the microemulsion-prepared 100Fe catalyst achieves a CO conversion of 34.8%, surpassing the conventionally precipitated p‑100Fe (27.7%). The addition of promoters further influences conversion: 95Fe-K and 95Fe-Na exhibit increased CO₂ conversion, while copper addition (95Fe-Cu) enhances both CO and CO₂ conversions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher surface area from microemulsion synthesis correlates with increased CO conversion. Promoter incorporation, particularly copper, further boosts activity, demonstrating the synergistic effect of synthesis method and promoter selection.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity increases with alkali promotion: 95Fe-K and 95Fe-Na reach 31.1% and 33.6%, respectively, compared to 21.3% for unpromoted 95Fe.
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium and sodium promote the water–gas shift reaction, leading to higher CO₂ selectivity. This effect is most pronounced in alkali-promoted samples.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity denotes the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity is influenced by promoter type. For CO₂ hydrogenation, CH₄ selectivity is 51.7% (100Fe), 42.7% (95Fe), 54.3% (95Fe-K), and 54.1% (95Fe-CuK).
- **Observations and Trends:** Copper promotion increases methane formation, while potassium’s presence can counteract this effect, reducing methane selectivity in doubly promoted catalysts.

#### 6.2.4 C₅–C₁₂ and C₁₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity to C₅–C₁₂ (gasoline range) and C₁₃⁺ (diesel range) hydrocarbons measures the fraction of long-chain products among total hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** For CO₂ hydrogenation, C₅–C₁₂ selectivity rises from 0.1% (100Fe) to 16.9% (95Fe-Na) and 21.3% (95Fe-CuK).
- **Observations and Trends:** Alkali and copper promotion significantly enhance long-chain hydrocarbon formation, with the highest selectivity observed in doubly promoted catalysts.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model, represents the probability of chain propagation:
\[
\alpha = \frac{\text{moles of C}_{n+1}}{\text{moles of C}_n}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** α values increase from 0.29–0.36 (unpromoted or Cu-promoted) to 0.52 (95Fe-K), 0.57 (95Fe-Na), and peak at 0.63 (95Fe-CuK).
- **Observations and Trends:** Alkali promotion, especially in combination with copper, markedly enhances chain growth probability, favoring longer hydrocarbon chains.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by resistance to deactivation, inferred from sustained CO conversion and surface area retention.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit deactivation rates are not provided, microemulsion-prepared and promoted catalysts maintain activity and resist excessive carbon deposition under reaction conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced dispersion and promoter effects contribute to improved stability, with potassium at high loadings being a potential exception due to carbon deposition risks.
