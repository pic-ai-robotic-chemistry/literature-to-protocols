## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines the synthesis procedure for the ε‑Fe₂C–xMn catalyst via the coprecipitation of Prussian blue analogues, followed by thermal treatments. A comparative analysis reveals that this method offers enhanced control over catalyst composition and dispersion of Mn when compared to other techniques. Although the coprecipitation–pyrolysis route involves more complex thermal processing, it provides cost effectiveness and reproducibility under controlled conditions.

#### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. • Precursor Mixing/Co‑precipitation  
   - Dissolve a calculated amount of polyvinylpyrrolidone K30 in 200 g deionized water (Solution A).  
   - Add (NH₄)₄Fe(CN)₆ to Solution A to form the Prussian blue precursor.  
   - Separately, dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Mn(NO₃)₂·4H₂O in 200 g deionized water (Solution B) adjusting the Fe/Mn molar ratio (e.g., 100/0, 100/1, 100/2, etc.).  
   - Under vigorous and continuous stirring, slowly add Solution B to Solution A, ensuring uniform mixing and complete coprecipitation.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   - With continuous stirring, allow the mixed solution to stand for a predetermined aging period to promote deposition of the precipitate and ensure a homogeneous distribution of Fe and Mn precursors.

3. • Drying  
   - Collect the precipitate and dry it at a controlled temperature (e.g., around 80–100 °C) for several hours, ensuring removal of residual water while maintaining the integrity of the structure.

4. • Calcination and Thermal Treatments  
   - Heat the dried precursor in an ammonia atmosphere at 550 °C to induce thermal decomposition and convert the Prussian blue precursor into activated Fe₂N.  
   - Follow with controlled carburization in a syngas (H₂/CO with a molar ratio of 2.5) at 300 °C for approximately 2 hours, transforming activated Fe₂N into the stable ε‑Fe₂C phase.

These steps, validated through in situ XRD along with complementary characterization techniques, ensure precise control over Mn doping levels and reproducibility of the final catalyst.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for the ε‑Fe₂C–xMn catalyst is performed via a dual-step protocol designed to transform the Prussian blue precursor into a highly active, stable phase suitable for Fischer‑Tropsch synthesis. This pre‐reaction treatment comprises an initial ammonia treatment followed by a controlled carburization step under a syngas atmosphere.

#### 5.1.1 Ammonia Treatment Activation
- **Definition:** This step converts the Prussian blue precursor into an activated Fe₂N phase. The ammonia atmosphere provides reactive nitrogen species that facilitate the removal of extraneous ligands and promote nitridation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The precursor undergoes thermal treatment in an ammonia atmosphere at approximately 550 °C. The transformation is rapid, completing within about 2 hours.
- **Observations:** In situ XRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy corroborate the successful formation of the activated Fe₂N phase. This precise thermal management enhances metal dispersion and mitigates risks of sintering, thereby establishing a robust intermediate for subsequent carburization.

#### 5.1.2 Carburization Activation
- **Definition:** Following ammonia treatment, the activated Fe₂N phase is transformed into the catalytically active ε‑Fe₂C phase via controlled exposure to a syngas atmosphere. This step incorporates carbon within the structure while preserving beneficial nitrogen dopants.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Carburization is performed under a syngas mixture (containing CO and H₂) at around 300 °C. Although the exact gas composition ratios are not further specified, the controlled environment is critical to inducing the phase transition effectively.
- **Observations:** The careful control of temperature and gas composition during carburization is found to yield a stable ε‑Fe₂C phase. This phase transition is crucial for achieving high olefin selectivity and suppressing C₁ byproducts, as confirmed in catalytic performance tests. The sequential activation steps also ensure that Mn is well incorporated, optimizing metal dispersion and enhancing the overall catalytic efficiency.

This structured activation protocol—first via ammonia treatment at 550 °C and then a carburization at 300 °C—forms the cornerstone for the catalyst's high performance in high‑temperature Fischer‑Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was performed at a temperature of 573 K. This parameter is critical for facilitating the activation of syngas, as the elevated temperature promotes the water–gas shift reaction and aids in the desorption of olefins from the catalyst surface. Under these conditions, the ε‑Fe₂C–xMn catalyst demonstrated reliable performance with effective CO activation and stable operation, supporting its applicability under industrially relevant temperatures.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The reaction pressure was maintained at 2.0 MPa throughout the catalytic evaluation. This relatively high pressure improves syngas concentration within the reactor, thereby enhancing CO conversion. It is observed that operating at this pressure level favors the formation of heavier hydrocarbons, as increased pressure is associated with improved product selectivity—specifically a notable reduction in C₁ byproduct formation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio was precisely set at 2.5. This ratio plays a vital role in determining the product distribution by optimizing CO conversion and suppressing excessive formation of undesired byproducts such as CO₂ and CH₄. By selecting a ratio of 2.5, the process emphasizes olefin production, as confirmed by an enhanced selectivity of up to 70.2% for the ε‑Fe₂C–0.4Mn catalyst, while also regulating the associated methane formation in line with the desired reaction pathway.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) was varied from 20 to 500 L·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹. This parameter defines the reactant contact time with the catalyst, thereby affecting the overall CO conversion. Higher GHSV values tend to shorten the residence time, leading to a decrease in CO conversion; however, this adjustment simultaneously contributes to an optimized distribution favoring C₂⁺ olefins over C₁ byproducts. The flexible range in GHSV also provided insights into the balance between catalyst stability and conversion efficiency during prolonged reaction tests, which extended up to 120 hours in continuous operation.

Collectively, these reaction conditions—temperature, pressure, H₂/CO ratio, and GHSV—are clearly defined to ensure reproducibility and offer a robust operational framework for high‑temperature Fischer–Tropsch synthesis targeting high olefin selectivity.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase evolution of the ε‑Fe₂C–xMn catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), including in situ measurements, and Mössbauer spectroscopy to distinguish closely related phases.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirmed the formation of the ε‑Fe₂C phase (JCPDS#36-1249) after carburization, with the (001) diffraction peak of ε‑Fe₂N shifting to lower angles as Mn content increased, indicating partial Mn incorporation and high dispersion at low loadings. At higher Mn levels (x ≥ 0.2), additional peaks corresponding to MnO (PDF#75-0257) appeared, evidencing MnO aggregation. Mössbauer spectroscopy differentiated ε‑Fe₂N (singlet) from ε‑Fe₂C (sextet), confirming phase purity and the successful transformation during synthesis.
- **Observations:** The Prussian blue precursor route yielded ε‑Fe₂C with enhanced thermal stability (up to 440 °C), attributed to residual N atoms, and enabled precise control over Mn dispersion and phase composition.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Valence States
- **Description:** Elemental composition and oxidation states were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES), and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES).
- **Findings:** XPS analysis revealed Fe 2p peaks at 706.3 eV (Fe⁰) and 710.5 eV (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺), with Mn addition reducing Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ content and stabilizing iron carbides. XANES spectra indicated that Mn existed predominantly as Mn²⁺, with the Mn K-edge matching MnO references. ICP-OES confirmed that the Fe/Mn molar ratios in the catalysts matched the intended synthesis values, and post-reaction analysis showed increased Mn/Fe ratios at the surface, suggesting Mn surface enrichment.
- **Observations:** Mn doping inhibited the oxidation of iron carbides, and the presence of Mn²⁺ was consistent across both fresh and spent catalysts.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and STEM-EDS mapping were used to assess particle morphology and elemental dispersion.
- **Findings:** At low Mn loadings (e.g., ε‑Fe₂C–0.06Mn), Mn was uniformly dispersed with minimal aggregation. At higher Mn contents (e.g., ε‑Fe₂C–0.4Mn), larger MnO clusters were observed, corroborated by the emergence of MnO peaks in XRD. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed uniformly dispersed particles with an average crystallite size of approximately 57.4 nm, and high-resolution TEM confirmed the ε‑Fe₂C (010) lattice spacing of 2.40 Å.
- **Observations:** The synthesis method enabled fine control over Mn dispersion, with higher Mn loadings leading to phase-separated MnO domains.

#### 6.1.4 Local Atomic Structure
- **Description:** XANES and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses provided insight into the local atomic environments of Fe and Mn.
- **Findings:** The Fe K-edge in XANES shifted toward lower energy upon Mn addition, indicating a reduction in Fe oxidation state. EXAFS revealed that Mn existed as (MnO)n clusters rather than isolated atoms, while the local structure around Fe remained largely unchanged with Mn doping.
- **Observations:** The interface between highly dispersed MnO clusters and ε‑Fe₂C was identified as critical for suppressing CO₂ formation and enhancing olefin selectivity.

#### 6.1.5 Bulk and Surface Composition
- **Description:** ICP-OES quantified bulk Fe/Mn ratios, while XPS provided surface composition data.
- **Findings:** The Fe/Mn ratios in the final catalysts corresponded closely to the synthesis targets, with surface analysis indicating Mn enrichment after reaction.
- **Observations:** Surface aggregation of Mn after reaction was linked to the observed catalytic performance trends.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 20 L·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹, CO conversion for ε‑Fe₂C catalysts decreased from 98.1% to 36.1% as Mn loading increased. For comparative studies at 573 K, 2.0 MPa, and H₂/CO = 2.5, CO conversion was maintained at 41.5 ± 1.5%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Mn content led to a systematic decrease in CO conversion, while higher H₂/CO ratios and reaction pressures promoted higher conversion. The trend highlights a trade-off between conversion and selectivity as Mn loading is optimized.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The ε‑Fe₂C–0.4Mn catalyst achieved a CO₂ selectivity of 11.9% under optimized conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity decreased markedly with increasing Mn loading, reaching its lowest at a Fe/Mn molar ratio of 0.2:1. This suppression of CO₂ formation is attributed to the MnO–ε‑Fe₂C interface, which inhibits the water–gas shift reaction.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, given by:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For ε‑Fe₂C–0.4Mn, CH₄ selectivity was 7.1%. Lower Mn loadings resulted in higher CH₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** As Mn content increased, CH₄ selectivity decreased, with the combined C₁ byproduct selectivity (CO₂ + CH₄) dropping from 66.7% to 19.0%. This demonstrates Mn’s role in suppressing methane formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbons:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At a Mn/Fe ratio of 0.2:1, C₂⁺ olefin selectivity reached 70.2%, with C₂–4 olefins at 7.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher Mn loading shifted product distribution toward longer-chain olefins, enhancing C₃⁺ selectivity and minimizing C₁ byproducts.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution:
  \[
  W_n = (1-\alpha)\alpha^{n-1}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The α factor increased with Mn loading, correlating with higher selectivity for C₂⁺ hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced α values with Mn promotion indicate improved chain growth, favoring olefin production over methane.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time; deactivation rate is the decline in conversion per hour:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 120 hours of continuous operation, ε‑Fe₂C–Mn catalysts maintained stable CO conversion with minimal deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low deactivation rate demonstrates the catalyst’s robustness, with the MnO–ε‑Fe₂C interface contributing to sustained activity and selectivity.

#### 6.2.7 Additional Performance Indicators
- **C₂⁺ Olefin Selectivity:** At optimal Mn loading, C₂⁺ olefins accounted for 70.2% of products.
- **C₃H₆/C₃H₈ Ratio:** Used as a probe for olefin/paraffin selectivity, confirming high olefin yields.
- **Carbon Balance:** Maintained within 100 ± 5%, validating the accuracy of conversion and selectivity data.

**Summary:** The ε‑Fe₂C–xMn catalysts, particularly ε‑Fe₂C–0.4Mn, exhibit high olefin selectivity, suppressed C₁ byproduct formation, and excellent stability, with performance metrics directly linked to Mn loading and the engineered catalyst interface.
