## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe/ZrO₂ reference and promoted iron catalysts is based on a single‐step coimpregnation method. An aqueous solution of 0.2 M Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, along with the required promoter salts (e.g., K, Ga, Mo, Cs, Cu, Ce), is prepared. For salts with low water solubility (e.g., Nb, Sn, Sb), ethanol is used as the solvent. In the case of Sb-containing catalysts, a sequential impregnation is performed by first adding Sb, followed by K.

#### • Co‑impregnation Method:

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Reagents: 0.2 M Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and selected promoter salts to achieve ~10 wt% iron and maintain an Fe-to-promoter molar ratio of 50:1 (nominally 1 wt% K).  
   - Equipment: Zirconia support, stirring apparatus, and dropwise addition setup.  
   - Conditions: The precursor solution is added dropwise to the ZrO₂ support, ensuring even distribution. An 8-hour stirring period at room temperature is employed to achieve thorough mixing.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - Some formulations include the precipitation–deposition step immediately after precursor mixing, wherein a support precursor is added dropwise and stirred for 1 hour. This step ensures enhanced homogeneity prior to drying.

3. **Drying**  
   - The impregnated support undergoes solvent evaporation at approximately 80 °C.  
   - Subsequently, the material is dried overnight at 120 °C in an oven to remove all residual solvents and moisture.

4. **Calcination**  
   - Conditions: The dried catalyst is calcined in air at 500 °C for 5 hours with a ramp rate of 1 °C/min.  
   - Equipment: A controlled furnace is used to ensure reproducibility and to promote the formation of the desired crystalline phases.

5. **Additional Post‑treatments**  
   - If required, further reduction or passivation steps are performed to develop the active iron carbide phases critical for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This method, characterized by its controlled dropwise addition and extended stirring, ensures a uniform dispersion of active metals on the ZrO₂ support. The detailed and sequential nature of the procedure allows for high reproducibility, essential for comparative catalytic studies and performance evaluations.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the preparation steps are organized by catalyst type, with reagent handling subdivided into weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing. The reproducibility is ensured by grouping operations for the Fe/ZrO₂ reference catalyst and one of the promoted systems (FeK/ZrO₂), as representative of the catalyst family.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe/ZrO₂ Reference Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed to achieve a target loading of approximately 10 wt% iron using an analytical balance (exact mass not specified).  
  (This precursor provides the active iron phase essential for subsequent transformation into iron carbides.)

##### 3.1.1.2 FeK/ZrO₂ Catalyst
- **K precursor (e.g., KNO₃)**: Weighed to establish a molar ratio of approximately 50:1 relative to iron.  
  (This step ensures the proper promotion of the catalyst with potassium, influencing dispersion and carbidization.)

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe/ZrO₂ Reference Catalyst
- **Deionized water**: Poured dropwise into the solid mixture using a graduated cylinder, ensuring controlled wetting of the support.  
  (An aqueous solution is typically used to dissolve the iron nitrate precursor.)

##### 3.1.2.2 FeK/ZrO₂ Catalyst
- **Ethanol**: Transferred dropwise using a syringe to facilitate dissolution of the potassium salt, which is less water‐soluble.  
  (The use of ethanol for certain promoters is critical to obtain a homogeneous impregnating solution.)

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe/ZrO₂ Reference Catalyst
- **Impregnation mixture**: Mixed by stirring at room temperature for 8 hours to ensure thorough distribution of the precursor over the ZrO₂ support, followed by ultrasonic treatment for 15 minutes to enhance homogenization.  
  (Extended stirring supports complete impregnation, and ultrasound aids in breaking agglomerates.)

##### 3.1.3.2 FeK/ZrO₂ Catalyst
- **Impregnation mixture**: Mixed by stirring at room temperature for 8 hours under ambient atmospheric conditions, promoting uniform contact between precursors and support.  
  (Consistent stirring conditions across catalysts help maintain reproducibility in catalyst formulation.)

This structured approach—with clear catalyst-specific grouping and detailed procedural steps—ensures that the reagent preparation process for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is both reproducible and precisely documented for subsequent catalytic evaluation.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/ZrO₂ Reference and Promoted Catalysts (e.g., FeK/ZrO₂, FeKGa/ZrO₂, FeKMo/ZrO₂, FeKCs/ZrO₂, FeKCu/ZrO₂, FeKCe/ZrO₂)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • 10 wt% targeted iron introduced via Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (prepared as a 0.2 M solution)  
  • Promoter salts (e.g., KNO₃, CsNO₃, Ga(NO₃)₃·xH₂O, etc.) added to maintain a Fe-to-promoter molar ratio of approximately 50:1  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Dissolution in distilled water or ethanol (for poorly water-soluble salts)  
  • No specific pH adjustment is detailed, although controlled reagent concentration is maintained  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve the required amounts of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and promoter salts in the selected solvent.  
  2. Stir the solution at room temperature for 8 hours to ensure complete homogenization.  
  3. Monitor the dropwise addition of the solution to the ZrO₂ support to secure uniform dispersion.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: Prepared precursor solution mixed with pre-treated zirconia support
- **Conditions**:  
  • Dropwise addition ensures a homogenous wetness  
- **Process**:  
  • Add the precursor solution slowly to the ZrO₂ support under continuous stirring.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:  
  • Post-impregnation, allow solvent evaporation at 80 °C.  
  • Optionally, conduct mild centrifugation or filtration if residual impurities are expected.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry the impregnated support overnight at 120 °C in an oven to remove residual solvents.
- **Calcination**:  
  • Calcine in air at 500 °C for 5 hours with a ramp rate of 1 °C/min to achieve the desired catalyst phase.

##### Step 5: Activation
- **Process**:  
  • Pre-treat the calcined catalyst by flowing CO at 350 °C for 10 hours.  
  • Cool to 180 °C, then expose to a H₂/CO₂ (3:1) gas mixture under 10 bar, subsequently raising the temperature to 350 °C prior to reaction initiation.

**Key Steps & Cautions**:  
• Ensure precise dropwise addition during precursor assembly to maintain concentration uniformity.  
• The stirring duration (8 hours) is critical for homogenization.  
• For Sb-containing catalysts, perform sequential impregnation (Sb before K) to secure optimal promoter interaction.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**:  
  XRD analysis was performed to determine the catalyst’s crystalline phases, including both zirconia (monoclinic and tetragonal) and iron carbide phases. The catalyst powder was dispersed in ethanol using ultrasounds and then a drop of the suspension was deposited on a holey carbon film attached to a 300 mesh TEM Cu grid. After drying, the sample was mounted on the diffractometer.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  Bruker AXS D8 diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Monochromatic Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1538 nm); measurements collected over a 2θ range of 10°–90°.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Step size of 0.02° and an acquisition time of 0.5 seconds per step.
- **Key Notes**:  
  Careful sample alignment is crucial for accurate phase identification, although distinguishing between similar iron carbide phases remains challenging due to overlapping peaks.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**:  
  BET analysis was conducted to measure the catalyst’s specific surface area and pore structure via nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  Micromeritics Tristar II PLUS analyzer.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Samples underwent degassing under vacuum at 120 °C for 3 hours prior to analysis; measurements were carried out at a cryogenic temperature of −196 °C.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Nitrogen adsorption and desorption isotherm data were collected for BET calculations.
- **Key Notes**:  
  Effective sample degassing is essential to remove surface moisture and ensure reliable surface area measurements.

### 4.3 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**:  
  H₂‑TPR was used to assess the reduction behavior of the catalyst by monitoring conversion steps from hematite to magnetite and further reductions.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  AutoChem II 2920 apparatus.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Approximately 0.05 g of catalyst was subjected to a flow of 5% H₂ in Ar at 50 mL/min.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Temperature ramped from ambient to 1000 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Key Notes**:  
  The pre-treatment and controlled heating rates are critical to accurately capture the reduction profiles and observe promoter-induced shifts.

### 4.4 STEM‑HAADF and STEM‑EDS
- **Operational Description**:  
  These microscopy techniques were employed for imaging the catalyst’s morphology and for elemental localization, determining nanoparticle sizes and dispersion.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  Transmission electron microscope operated at 200 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Samples were prepared by ultrasonic dispersion in ethanol and drop-casting onto a holey carbon TEM grid.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  STEM-EDS mapping used an accelerating voltage of 200 kV with a probe diameter of about 1 Å; imaging settings included a scanning speed of 20 μs/px and mapping at 0.05 μs/px at a resolution of 256 × 256 px.
- **Key Notes**:  
  Accurate sample dispersion is necessary to prevent agglomeration, ensuring representative nanoparticle size distribution.

### 4.5 CO₂ Temperature‑Programmed Desorption (CO₂‑TPD)
- **Operational Description**:  
  CO₂‑TPD was applied to evaluate the catalyst’s surface basicity and CO₂ adsorption properties.
- **Instruments and Models**:  
  Thermal conductivity detector (TCD) integrated within the TPD setup.
- **Characterization Conditions**:  
  Catalysts were pretreated at 500 °C in He for 1 hour, then cooled to 40 °C and exposed to CO₂ for 30 minutes.
- **Test Parameters**:  
  Subsequent heating was performed at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min up to 700 °C.
- **Key Notes**:  
  The sequential temperature and gas flow controls are critical to accurately quantify CO₂ desorption profiles and assess surface basicity.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is executed via a controlled pre‐reaction treatment designed to transition the precursor iron oxides into the active carbide phase. The following subsections detail each key parameter:

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for driving the reduction of iron oxides and promoting carbidization.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are pre-activated at exactly 350 °C in a fixed-bed reactor using controlled heating.  
- **Observations:** At 350 °C, the CO atmosphere effectively reduces the iron oxides while favoring the formation of iron Hägg carbide, ensuring the generation of an active catalytic phase without causing significant sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure governs gas–solid interactions and plays an essential role in maintaining uniform reduction kinetics.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is carried out under atmospheric pressure. A continuous CO flow at 10 mL/min is maintained to guarantee consistent exposure of the catalyst surface.  
- **Observations:** Operating at atmospheric pressure ensures effective gas contact, promoting thorough reduction while preventing catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition used during activation is decisive for initiating the reduction and subsequent carbidization processes.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A pure CO atmosphere is employed to drive the reduction of iron oxides and facilitate the formation of the requisite carbide phases.  
- **Observations:** This targeted use of CO ensures an optimal chemical environment with minimal oxygen interference, thus fostering the development of the active phase.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, reported as Weight Hourly Space Velocity (WHSV), measures the gas flow rate per catalyst mass and is important for controlling gas–solid contact time.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The WHSV is maintained at approximately 9.25 L/(gcat·h) (or 9,250 mL/(gcat·h)).  
- **Observations:** This condition is crucial for preventing over-reduction while ensuring effective activation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation ensures complete transformation of the catalyst precursor into its active form.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated for 10 hours at 350 °C under the aforementioned conditions.  
- **Observations:** This extended activation period supports thorough reduction and carbidization, leading to improved catalyst stability and sustained performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

This section details the reaction conditions adopted during the CO₂ hydrogenation process, which aligns with Fischer-Tropsch synthesis principles. The parameters outlined below ensure clear reproducibility and highlight their respective roles in influencing catalyst performance and product selectivity.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for syngas activation, influencing both the kinetics of CO₂ hydrogenation and the reduction behavior of the catalysts.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was conducted at 350 °C.
- **Observations:** At 350 °C, effective activation of syngas is achieved, which is essential for converting CO₂ into hydrocarbons. This temperature also supports a stable catalytic performance as evidenced by maintained conversion levels during testing.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure affects the concentration of reactants in the gas phase and plays a significant role in enhancing reaction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reactor pressure was strictly maintained at 10 bar.
- **Observations:** The use of a 10 bar pressure level ensures an increased syngas concentration, thereby accelerating CO₂ conversion. This high-pressure environment is key for optimizing reaction rates and achieving consistent product yields.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO₂ Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO₂ ratio governs the hydrogenation environment, influencing the balance between forming CO as an intermediate and the subsequent hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A H₂/CO₂ ratio of 3:1 was consistently applied during the catalytic evaluations.
- **Observations:** This ratio is pivotal in steering product selectivity. It fosters the reverse water-gas shift reaction, leading to efficient CO formation while maintaining an optimal balance between CO₂ conversion and hydrocarbon output.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, expressed as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), measures the volumetric flow rate of the reactant gas per unit mass of catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was varied in the range of 4.67 to 18.19 L g⁻¹ h⁻¹.
- **Observations:** Variations in GHSV were directly linked to changes in CO₂ conversion efficiency and catalyst stability. Lower space-time velocities favored higher conversions, while higher velocities helped in preserving the catalyst’s integrity during extended reaction periods.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases and understanding catalyst stability.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of calcined catalysts display characteristic peaks for monoclinic ZrO₂ at 28.2° and 31.5° (JCPDS 37-1484) and tetragonal ZrO₂ at 50.3° (JCPDS 17-0923). Broad, low-intensity peaks corresponding to hematite (Fe₂O₃) indicate the presence of small iron oxide nanoparticles (<5 nm). After CO₂ hydrogenation, new diffraction features at 2θ = 43.4° and 44.1° are observed, attributed to Hagg iron carbide (Fe₅C₂, JCPDS 20-0509), confirming the transformation of iron into active carbide phases under reaction conditions.
- **Observations:** The persistence of ZrO₂ phases after reaction demonstrates support stability. The formation of Fe₅C₂ is directly linked to enhanced Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were evaluated using N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms (BET analysis), which influence catalyst dispersion and reactant accessibility.
- **Findings:** The ZrO₂ support exhibits a surface area (SBET) of 95.6 m²/g and a total pore volume (Vtot) of 0.286 cm³/g. These mesoporous properties are retained in the supported catalysts, facilitating high dispersion of iron nanoparticles.
- **Observations:** High surface area and pore volume promote effective mass transport and increase the number of accessible active sites, supporting improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) profiles reveal the reducibility of iron oxides and the influence of promoters on reduction behavior.
- **Findings:** Two main reduction peaks are observed: the first at ~380 °C (Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄) and the second at ~500 °C (Fe₃O₄ → FeO → Fe) for reference and K-promoted catalysts. Addition of Cs, Ga, Mo, or Ce shifts the second peak to higher temperatures, indicating stronger iron–promoter interactions and hindered reduction. Cu as a co-promoter shifts both peaks to lower temperatures (~250 and 298 °C), facilitating reduction.
- **Observations:** Promoters generally increase total H₂ consumption, reflecting a higher amount of reducible iron and modified iron–support interactions.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM-HAADF) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) were used to assess nanoparticle size and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** The reference Fe/ZrO₂ catalyst contains iron nanoparticles with a mean diameter of 20 nm. Promoted catalysts (e.g., FeK/ZrO₂, FeKGa/ZrO₂, FeKMo/ZrO₂) exhibit smaller, homogeneously dispersed nanoparticles in the 11–14 nm range, with the overall size distribution spanning 6–27 nm. Elemental mapping confirms uniform dispersion of both iron and promoters.
- **Observations:** Dual promotion (K plus a second promoter) enhances iron dispersion and homogeneity, which correlates with improved catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.5 Iron Carbidization (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** In situ Mössbauer spectroscopy at –153 °C quantifies iron carbide species and assesses the extent of carbidization, a key factor in FT catalysis.
- **Findings:** After CO activation and CO₂ hydrogenation, the fraction of Hagg iron carbide (χ-Fe₅C₂) increases, especially in promoted catalysts. FeKMo/ZrO₂ shows the highest iron carbide content, while FeKGa/ZrO₂ exhibits improved carbide crystallinity (narrower line width) rather than increased quantity.
- **Observations:** Enhanced carbidization is associated with higher FT activity and selectivity to light olefins, particularly in dual-promoted systems.

#### 6.1.6 Basicity and CO₂ Adsorption (CO₂-TPD)
- **Description:** CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) evaluates the basicity and CO₂ adsorption capacity, which are critical for CO₂ activation.
- **Findings:** ZrO₂ displays broad desorption peaks at 100 and 220 °C (weak/medium basic sites). Iron addition reduces these peaks, while promotion with K and Cs increases both the amount and strength of basic sites, introducing new peaks at 120 °C (weak) and 300–420 °C (strong). The amount of CO₂ adsorbed is higher for promoted catalysts (e.g., ZrO₂: 197.5 μmol/g).
- **Observations:** Dual promotion tunes basicity, optimizing CO₂ activation and correlating with improved catalytic performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ in the feed that is transformed during reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO₂ at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (350 °C, 10 bar, H₂/CO₂ = 3), the Fe/ZrO₂ reference catalyst exhibited an initial CO₂ conversion of ~20%, which declined to ~8% after 48 h on stream. Promoted catalysts, particularly those containing potassium (K) and a second promoter (e.g., Ga, Mo, Cs, Cu, Ce), achieved up to 7 times higher activity, with potassium alone increasing activity up to 4-fold.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of K nearly doubled the iron time yield (FTY), and dual promotion (K plus a second promoter) further enhanced both conversion and stability. Promoted catalysts maintained higher conversion over time, indicating improved resistance to deactivation.

#### 6.2.2 CO Selectivity
- **Description:** CO selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO₂ forming CO, primarily via the reverse water–gas shift (RWGS) reaction:
\[
\text{CO Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At low CO₂ conversions, CO selectivity approached 100% for all catalysts, confirming RWGS as the dominant pathway. As conversion increased, CO selectivity decreased, indicating subsequent Fischer–Tropsch-type hydrocarbon formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoted catalysts, especially those with K, maintained higher CO selectivity at equivalent conversions compared to the reference, except for Nb-promoted samples.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO₂ yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity was high (~80%) at very low CO₂ conversions (<5%) for all catalysts, but decreased as conversion increased.
- **Observations and Trends:** Dual-promoted catalysts (K plus a second promoter) exhibited lower CH₄ selectivity than Fe/ZrO₂ or K-only catalysts, indicating suppression of methane formation and favoring higher hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₂⁺ and Light Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity to C₂⁺ hydrocarbons and light olefins (C₂–C₄) is defined as the fraction of converted CO₂ forming these products:
\[
\text{C}_n\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{\sum n \cdot F(\text{C}_n)}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
where \( n \geq 2 \).
- **Experimental Data:** Fe/ZrO₂ showed low and conversion-independent light olefin selectivity (5–15%). Potassium- or cesium-promoted catalysts achieved much higher light olefin selectivity, which decreased with increasing CO₂ conversion. Dual promotion further increased light olefin output, with FeKMo/ZrO₂ showing the most pronounced effect.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced iron dispersion and carbidization, promoted by K and second promoters, correlated with higher light olefin selectivity and reduced secondary hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, influencing the distribution of hydrocarbon products. It is derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model:
\[
W_n = (1 - \alpha) \cdot \alpha^{n-1}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the article reports that dual promotion (K plus a second promoter) increases selectivity toward longer-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺), especially at higher conversions, indicating a higher α factor. Catalysts without K showed α values varying between 5% and 20%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Improved iron dispersion and carbidization, along with optimized basicity, are linked to higher α values and greater selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by the rate of decline in CO₂ conversion over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time (h)}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe/ZrO₂ deactivated significantly, with conversion dropping from 20% to 8% over 48 h. Promoted catalysts maintained higher and more stable conversion, with minimal deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of K and a second promoter markedly improved catalyst stability, with light olefin selectivity remaining stable (except for FeKMo/ZrO₂, which showed a steady increase).
