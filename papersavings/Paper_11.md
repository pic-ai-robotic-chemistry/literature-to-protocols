## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fischer–Tropsch catalyst synthesis is performed by continuous precipitation of amorphous ferric oxyhydroxide, ensuring reproducibility by controlling key parameters such as pH, temperature, and reagent concentrations. The following detailed procedures describe the two methods developed for catalysts 100Fe/3.6Si/0.71K and 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K.

#### 1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation (Applicable to Both Methods)
1. Mix an aqueous solution of Iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 1.17 M) with hydrolyzed tetraethyl orthosilicate acting as the silica support precursor.
2. Introduce concentrated ammonium hydroxide (15.6 M) dropwise while constantly stirring, maintaining a pH of 9.5.  
   • Observation: A color change and precipitate formation signal the deposition of amorphous ferric oxyhydroxide.

#### 2. Precipitation–Deposition
• Immediately following precursor mixing, the continuous addition of ammonium hydroxide leads to the effective precipitation–deposition of the iron oxyhydroxide. This step is critical as it precedes drying and solid formation.

#### 3. Drying
• For the 100Fe/3.6Si/0.71K variant:
  - Wash the precipitate thoroughly to remove impurities.
  - Oven-dry at 393 K for a controlled duration to secure uniform drying.
• For the 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K variant:
  - Adjust the slurry by adding potassium nitrate before proceeding to the next step.

#### 4. Post‑processing and Calcination
• 100Fe/3.6Si/0.71K:
  - Crush the dried precipitate to −300 mesh.
  - Impregnate the crushed material with potassium tert‑butoxide under controlled conditions.
• 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K:
  - Subject the modified slurry to spray-drying.
  - Calcine the dried product in air at 623 K for 4–5 hours to stabilize the catalyst structure.

This sequential order—from precursor mixing, through precipitation–deposition, drying, to specific post‑processing—ensures the precise formation of active phases. Equipment such as continuous stirred tank reactors and rotary drum vacuum filters facilitate accurate reagent mixing and temperature control, thereby enabling reproducibility and scalability in catalyst synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation procedure is a critical pretreatment step designed to convert the precursor iron oxides into active iron carbide phases that enhance Fischer–Tropsch performance. The activation process is performed under carefully monitored parameters to ensure reproducibility and high catalytic activity.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
- **Definition:** The activation temperature controls the rate and extent of reduction and carburization of the catalyst.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Catalysts are activated at 543 K. A 24-hour pretreatment at this temperature, whether using pure carbon monoxide or a tailored syngas mixture, is implemented to ensure complete transformation to the desired active phase.  
- **Observations:** Maintaining this temperature over an extended period efficiently promotes the formation of active carbide phases while avoiding excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
- **Definition:** The activation pressure is essential for controlling the reaction kinetics during the gas–solid interaction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is carried out at 1.31 MPa.  
- **Observations:** This pressure facilitates effective gas contact with the catalyst, which is crucial for achieving the high CO conversion rates observed.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition  
- **Definition:** The gas composition in the activation environment determines whether the catalyst will form active iron carbides or less active Fe₃O₄.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Two activation strategies are employed. In the CO pretreatment, pure carbon monoxide is used, directly leading to carbide formation and reported CO conversion rates up to 92%. In contrast, syngas pretreatments are performed with controlled H₂/CO ratios (0.7 or 0.1); lower hydrogen partial pressures favor carbide formation by minimizing water production.  
- **Observations:** The choice between CO and syngas activation is critical, as a hydrogen-rich environment can promote the formation of inactive Fe₃O₄, whereas low hydrogen conditions foster the desired carbide species.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** This parameter reflects the gas flow rate per unit of catalyst and ensures uniform exposure across catalyst particles.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is conducted with synthesis gas flow rates of approximately 3.1 normal L h⁻¹ per gram of Fe.  
- **Observations:** Adequate flow maintains uniform activation and prevents local accumulation of reaction products.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
- **Definition:** The duration of activation is key to ensuring full phase transformation without overexposure that could lead to sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A steady 24-hour activation period is applied after reaching the set temperature.  
- **Observations:** This timeframe is optimized to achieve complete reduction while preserving catalyst stability.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for syngas activation, impacting the formation of active catalyst phases and overall Fischer-Tropsch performance.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic evaluation was conducted at a reaction temperature of 543 K.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 543 K promoted the formation of active iron carbide phases during activation. This temperature, carefully selected to avoid "supercarbonization," helped achieve high CO conversion rates—with initial conversions reaching up to 92% for CO-pretreated catalysts.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the concentration of syngas components in the reactor, affecting both selectivity and conversion efficiency.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was performed in a 1 L continuous stirred tank reactor (autoclave) with pressures ranging from 0.10 MPa to 1.31 MPa.  
- **Observations:** The higher end of the pressure range (1.31 MPa) was associated with enhanced activity and selectivity. Conversely, reactors operating at lower pressures exhibited different catalytic behavior, with pressure adjustments directly tailoring product distribution, particularly for C₅+ hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio in the feed gas plays a pivotal role in determining the product distribution by influencing hydrocarbon chain growth and the nature of the activated phases.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation pretreatments involved variable H₂/CO ratios of 0.7 and 0.1.  
- **Observations:** A higher H₂/CO ratio (0.7) was found to promote the formation of less active Fe₃O₄ species, while a lower ratio (0.1) favored the development of active iron carbide phases, thereby enhancing overall catalytic activity.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity (often related to gas hourly space velocity, GHSV) defines the contact time between the catalyst and the reacting gases, influencing CO conversion and catalyst stability.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction employed a space-time velocity of 3.1 normal L h⁻¹ per gram of Fe.  
- **Observations:** This parameter ensured sufficient reactant exposure to the catalyst, supporting reproducible performance during prolonged operation and aligning with established activation procedures for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Identification by X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Description:** The crystalline phases present in the Fischer-Tropsch catalysts were determined using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD). Analyses were performed on a Philips APD XRD spectrometer equipped with a copper (Cu) anode and nickel (Ni) filter, operated at 40 kV and 20 mA (Cu Kα, 1.5418 Å). Prior to analysis, accumulated reactor wax was removed from catalyst samples via Soxhlet extraction to ensure accurate phase identification. Phase assignments were made by comparing diffraction patterns to standard files from the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that the as-prepared catalysts, after appropriate activation, contained distinct iron phases depending on the activation conditions. Activation with carbon monoxide or synthesis gas at low hydrogen partial pressure (H₂/CO = 0.1) led to the formation of iron carbides, specifically *ø*-Fe₅C₂ and ′-Fe₂.₂C, along with Fe₃O₄. In contrast, activation with synthesis gas at high hydrogen partial pressure (H₂/CO = 0.7) and elevated pressure (1.31 MPa) resulted in the exclusive presence of Fe₃O₄, with no detectable iron carbides.
- **Observations:** The formation of iron carbides (*ø*-Fe₅C₂ and ′-Fe₂.₂C) is strongly correlated with high Fischer-Tropsch activity, while Fe₃O₄ is associated with low activity. The amount of bulk carbide detected by XRD does not directly correlate with catalytic performance, suggesting that surface species may play a more critical role. The phase distribution is primarily governed by activation conditions, particularly hydrogen partial pressure and water/CO₂ levels, rather than by potassium content or synthesis method.

#### 6.1.2 Influence of Synthesis and Activation Conditions on Crystallinity
- **Description:** The synthesis protocol, including continuous precipitation at pH 9.5, drying at 393 K, and, for some batches, spray-drying and calcination at 623 K, was designed to control the crystallinity and phase purity of the catalysts. These preparative steps influence the formation of amorphous or crystalline iron oxide precursors, which are subsequently transformed during activation.
- **Findings:** Calcination at 623 K (applied to the 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K catalyst) promoted the formation of well-crystallized Fe₃O₄, as confirmed by XRD. The oven-dried (393 K) 100Fe/3.6Si/0.71K catalyst exhibited similar phase behavior after activation, indicating that both synthesis routes yield comparable crystalline phases post-activation, provided the activation conditions are appropriately selected.
- **Observations:** The choice of activation gas and pressure is more decisive for the final crystalline phase composition than the specific synthesis route or potassium source. Maintaining a high pH during precipitation ensures homogeneous precursor formation, which, after thermal treatment, results in the desired crystalline structures detectable by XRD.

#### 6.1.3 Limitations of XRD and Phase Analysis
- **Description:** XRD provides information on bulk crystalline phases but does not detect surface species or amorphous components, which may be catalytically relevant.
- **Findings:** The study emphasizes that while XRD confirms the presence of Fe₃O₄ and iron carbides, it cannot resolve the nature or abundance of active surface carbides. No quantitative crystallite size or peak deconvolution data are reported.
- **Observations:** The catalytic activity is best interpreted in conjunction with XRD phase identification, but the true active sites may reside in surface layers not visible to XRD. Thus, XRD serves as a reliable tool for monitoring bulk phase transformations during activation and reaction.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions (543 K, 1.31 MPa, H₂/CO = 0.7), the 100Fe/3.6Si/0.71K catalyst achieved an initial CO conversion of 92% following CO pretreatment. The 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K catalyst exhibited an initial CO conversion of 85% under similar activation. When synthesis gas activation was performed at low hydrogen partial pressure (H₂/CO = 0.7, 0.10 MPa), initial CO conversions above 85% were also observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** High CO conversion is strongly correlated with activation using CO or synthesis gas at low hydrogen partial pressure, which promotes the formation of active iron carbide phases. In contrast, activation at high hydrogen partial pressure leads to lower conversions (<30%) due to the predominance of less active Fe₃O₄.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, defined as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Both catalysts exhibited CO₂ selectivity values in the range of 35–40% under optimal activation conditions (CO pretreatment or low H₂/CO ratio). These values were consistent across both 0.71K and 1.0K potassium loadings.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity remained relatively stable regardless of potassium content, but increased slightly when activation was performed at higher hydrogen partial pressures, reflecting a shift toward water–gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 100Fe/3.6Si/0.71K catalyst consistently showed lower CH₄ selectivity (typically 7–9%) compared to the 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K catalyst (10–12%) under identical conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower CH₄ selectivity is favored by CO pretreatment and lower hydrogen partial pressure during activation. Higher potassium content slightly increases methane formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** C₃⁺ selectivity for both catalysts was high, typically exceeding 70% under optimal activation, with the 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K catalyst reaching up to 75%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced C₃⁺ selectivity is associated with CO activation and higher potassium content, reflecting a greater tendency toward long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon synthesis, with values closer to 1 favoring longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** The α factor for both catalysts was reported in the range of 0.90–0.93 under CO pretreatment and low H₂/CO activation, with the 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K catalyst consistently at the higher end.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values correspond to increased C₃⁺ selectivity and are favored by CO activation and higher potassium loading.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\%\,\text{per week})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 100Fe/4.4Si/1.0K catalyst demonstrated exceptional stability, with a deactivation rate of only 0.59% per week over 2700 hours. The 100Fe/3.6Si/0.71K catalyst exhibited a slightly higher deactivation rate (~1% per week).
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower deactivation rates are achieved with higher potassium content and CO pretreatment, underscoring the importance of activation conditions and promoter levels for long-term catalyst performance.
