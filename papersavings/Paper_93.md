## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst was synthesized via a one‑step hydrothermal method combined with incipient wetness impregnation. This approach integrates precursor mixing, precipitation–deposition, drying, and calcination into a streamlined process while ensuring reproducibility and controlled dispersion of active species.

#### Hydrothermal Synthesis and Precipitation–Deposition
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: Dissolve (NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄•4H₂O in deionized water.  
   - Subsequent addition: Introduce CS(NH₂)₂ followed by Co(NO₃)₂•6H₂O under stirring to form a red transparent solution.  
   - Volume Adjustment: Adjust the solution to exactly 90 mL with deionized water.  
   - Equipment: Use a magnetic stirrer and a pH meter to ensure uniform mixing and accurate reagent addition.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Process: Transfer the homogeneous solution into a 150 mL Teflon‑lined autoclave.  
   - Reaction Conditions: Seal and heat at 473 K for 24 hours.  
   - Observations: Formation of a black precipitate from the initial red transparent solution indicates successful precipitation.

#### Post‑processing: Drying, Impregnation, and Calcination
3. • Drying  
   - Procedure: Wash the precipitate thoroughly with ethanol and deionized water.  
   - Drying Conditions: Dry at 323 K for 12 hours.  
   - Note: This step removes residual solvents and prepares the composite for further modification.

4. • Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
   - Procedure: Impregnate the dried Co–MoS₂ composite with an aqueous K₂CO₃ solution.  
   - Enhancement: Subject the mixture to ultrasonic dispersion for 20 minutes to ensure uniform distribution of the K promoter.  
   - Drying: Follow with drying at 338 K for 3 hours.

5. • Calcination  
   - Final Activation: Calcine the impregnated composite in flowing argon at 723 K for 90 minutes.  
   - Outcome: This calcination step stabilizes the active Co–Mo–S phase by promoting necessary structural rearrangements while inhibiting the formation of inactive species.

### Comparative Analysis
• Advantages: This integrated method produces well‐dispersed nanosheet‐structured catalysts with high reproducibility, controlled phase formation, and efficient utilization of equipment (e.g., Teflon‑lined autoclave).  
• Disadvantages: Precise control over reagent concentrations and reaction conditions is critical; scaling may require additional optimization to maintain uniform impregnation and phase stability.

This detailed sequential procedure, thoroughly documented with exact temperatures, durations, and observations, ensures that other researchers can replicate the process and achieve consistent catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation process for the Fischer–Tropsch catalyst is a critical pre‐reaction treatment step designed to prepare the fresh K–Co–MoS₂ catalyst for syngas conversion. In the reported study, two different activation atmospheres are evaluated: a pure hydrogen (H₂) environment and a diluted hydrogen environment (5% H₂/N₂). The choice of activation gas significantly influences the catalyst’s reduction behavior and subsequent performance.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
• Definition: The activation temperature is a key parameter that drives the reduction of precursor species and facilitates the hydrogenation of sulfur at the catalyst’s edge, thereby promoting SH group formation.  
• Experimental Conditions: The process is performed at an elevated temperature, exemplified by treatment at approximately 400 °C. Although the article does not provide additional temperature points, this condition is sufficient to ensure partial reduction without inducing sintering.  
• Observations: Activation at this temperature enables efficient hydrogenation, which is crucial for stabilizing the Co–Mo–S phase by inhibiting the segregation of Co into inactive Co₉S₈.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas Composition  
• Definition: The gas composition used during the activation step directly governs the reduction environment. A pure H₂ atmosphere offers a strongly reductive setting, while a 5% H₂/N₂ mixture provides a milder environment.  
• Experimental Conditions: The catalyst is exposed to either pure hydrogen or a 5% H₂/N₂ mixture.  
• Observations: Catalysts treated with pure H₂ exhibit a higher CO conversion (approximately 19.01%) and improved formation of coordinatively unsaturated sites compared to those activated in the diluted environment (around 13.87% CO conversion). The pure H₂ environment promotes the hydrogenation of edge sulfur, forming SH species that stabilize the active phase and enhance electron transfer from Co to Mo.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time  
• Definition: The duration of activation must be sufficient to complete the reduction and sulfur hydrogenation without promoting sintering or catalyst deactivation.  
• Experimental Conditions: Activation is carried out for several hours, ensuring an optimal balance between reduction efficiency and catalyst stability.  
• Observations: Extended activation in pure H₂ is correlated with higher turnover frequencies for both CO conversion and higher alcohol synthesis.

This activation strategy, distinct from characterization procedures, effectively primes the catalyst for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reactions by enhancing active site density and stability.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structural Evolution
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and their transformations were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), providing insight into the active and inactive species present in the catalyst before and after activation.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the fresh Nanosheet‐Structured K–Co–MoS₂ catalyst display weak diffraction peaks at 13.7°, 33.1°, 39.8°, and 58.9°, corresponding to MoS₂, and at 27.9°, 36.2°, 46.7°, and 55.0°, attributed to CoS₂. Additional peaks at 15.4° and 31.1° indicate the presence of the CoMo₂S₄ phase. No Co₉S₈ peaks are observed in the fresh catalyst. Upon activation with 5% H₂/N₂, new peaks at 29.8°, 47.5°, and 52.1° emerge, signifying the formation of the Co₉S₈ phase.
- **Observations:** The weak intensity of diffraction peaks in the fresh catalyst suggests a low degree of crystallinity and high dispersion of active phases. Activation under pure H₂ more effectively suppresses the formation of the inactive Co₉S₈ phase, maintaining the active Co–Mo–S structure.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Nanosheet Structure
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to examine the morphology, lateral dimensions, and dispersion of the catalyst’s nanosheet structure.
- **Findings:** TEM images reveal that the catalyst consists of thin, flexible, and interconnected 2D MoS₂ nanosheets with lateral sizes exceeding 100 nm. High-resolution TEM confirms an interlayer spacing of 0.63 nm for the (002) plane of MoS₂, characteristic of the 2H-MoS₂ structure. Lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.25 nm, assigned to the (210) plane of CoS₂, are observed at the nanosheet edges.
- **Observations:** The nanosheet morphology ensures abundant exposure of edge sites, which are catalytically active. The incorporation of Co and K does not induce aggregation, indicating high dispersion and structural integrity.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Surface Chemical States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to determine the surface elemental composition and oxidation states, providing information on the chemical environment of active sites.
- **Findings:** XPS spectra show Co 2p₃/₂ and 2p₁/₂ peaks at 779.3 eV and 794.2 eV, respectively, corresponding to CoS₂. The Mo⁴⁺ 3d₅/₂ and 3d₃/₂ peaks are at 229.5 eV and 232.8 eV. The binding energy difference between Co 2p₃/₂ and Mo 3d₅/₂ is 549.8 eV, consistent with the Co–Mo–S phase. After activation, catalysts treated with 5% H₂/N₂ show increased Co₉S₈ signals, while pure H₂ activation results in weaker Co₉S₈ peaks and a shift to lower binding energies for Mo and S, indicating enhanced electron density and SH group formation.
- **Observations:** Pure H₂ activation stabilizes the Co–Mo–S phase, suppresses Co₉S₈ formation, and increases the number of coordinatively unsaturated sites, which are beneficial for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Active Site Estimation
- **Description:** The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was used to measure the specific surface area, which, combined with XPS data, allows estimation of the number of surface Mo atoms for turnover frequency (TOF) calculations.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area of the catalyst is 0.9363 m²/g. This value, together with the surface atomic composition from XPS, is used to estimate the number of active surface Mo atoms, providing a basis for intrinsic activity evaluation.
- **Observations:** The measured surface area reflects the high dispersion and accessibility of active sites, supporting the catalyst’s high intrinsic activity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO entering and exiting the reactor, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (583 K, 5.0 MPa, GHSV 4800 h⁻¹, after 48 h induction), the Nanosheet‐Structured K–Co–MoS₂ catalyst exhibited a CO conversion of **19.01%** when activated in pure H₂. For comparison, activation in 5% H₂/N₂ resulted in a lower CO conversion of **13.87%**.
- **Observations and Trends:** Pure H₂ activation significantly enhances CO conversion, attributed to improved stabilization of the Co–Mo–S active phase and suppression of inactive Co₉S₈ formation.

#### 6.2.2 C₂⁺OH Selectivity
- **Description:** C₂⁺OH selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms higher alcohols (C₂ and above), a key indicator for alcohol synthesis catalysts. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{C}_{2}^{+}\text{OH Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_{2}^{+}\text{OH,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{C}_{2}^{+}\text{OH,out}} \) is the molar flow rate of higher alcohols in the product.
- **Experimental Data:** The C₂⁺OH selectivity increased from **0.76%** (5% H₂/N₂ activation) to **0.92%** (pure H₂ activation).
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced selectivity for higher alcohols is achieved with pure H₂ activation, correlating with improved active phase stability and electron transfer properties.

#### 6.2.3 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF measures the intrinsic catalytic activity, defined as the number of reactant molecules converted (or product molecules formed) per active site per second. For this system:
  \[
  \text{TOF}_{\text{CO}} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted s}^{-1} \times N_A}{\text{number of surface Mo atoms}}
  \]
  \[
  \text{TOF}_{\text{C}_{2}^{+}\text{OH}} = \frac{\text{mol C}_{2}^{+}\text{OH formed s}^{-1} \times N_A}{\text{number of surface Mo atoms}}
  \]
  where \( N_A \) is Avogadro’s constant.
- **Experimental Data:** For pure H₂-activated catalyst, TOF values were **0.33 s⁻¹** (CO conversion) and **0.044 s⁻¹** (C₂⁺OH formation). These values were lower for the catalyst activated in 5% H₂/N₂.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher TOF values under pure H₂ activation reflect increased intrinsic activity, consistent with improved CO conversion and alcohol selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor (Anderson–Schulz–Flory parameter) indicates the probability of chain propagation versus termination, influencing the distribution of alcohol products. It is derived from product distribution analysis.
- **Experimental Data:** Catalysts with stabilized Co–Mo–S phases (notably after pure H₂ activation) exhibited higher α factors, signifying a greater tendency for chain growth and higher C₂⁺OH selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor increases with improved active phase stability, directly correlating with enhanced selectivity for longer-chain alcohols.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended reaction times. A lower deactivation rate indicates better stability.
- **Experimental Data:** All performance data were collected after a 48 h induction period to ensure steady-state operation. Pure H₂ activation was found to better maintain the Co–Mo–S phase, resulting in more stable CO conversion and selectivity over time.
- **Observations and Trends:** The suppression of Co₉S₈ formation under pure H₂ activation is critical for maintaining catalyst activity and selectivity, leading to improved operational stability.
