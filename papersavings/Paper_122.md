## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe/CNS(1000) catalysts is based on a MOF-derived approach combined with incipient wetness impregnation. This method offers precise control over nitrogen doping and catalyst dispersion, albeit at the cost of increased procedural complexity and specialized equipment requirements.

#### MOF Precursor Synthesis via Co‑precipitation
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Reagents: 0.33 g Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and 0.99 g 2‑methylimidazole dissolved separately in 90 mL of deionized water.  
   • Process: Mix the two solutions and stir magnetically for 24 h at room temperature; formation of a white precipitate indicates proper reaction progress.  
   • Equipment: Magnetic stirrer and centrifuge (for separation after stirring).

2. Drying  
   • Conditions: The separated ZIF‑8 nanosheets are dried in air at 50 °C for 12 h, ensuring removal of moisture prior to pyrolysis.

3. Pyrolysis to Form N‑doped Carbon Support  
   • Conditions: Employ a controlled ramp rate of 2 °C/min, heating to a target temperature (900, 950, 1000, or 1100 °C) and maintaining for 2 h under a continuous argon flow.  
   • Outcome: Removal of zinc yields nitrogen-doped carbon nanosheets (CNS) with tailored graphitization and various N species (pyridinic, pyrrolic, graphitic, oxidized).

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation and Post‑processing
4. Precipitation–Deposition (Impregnation)  
   • Reagents: 0.214 g ammonium iron citrate dissolved in 1 mL deionized water.  
   • Process: Impregnate the solution into the pores of the CNS under vigorous stirring and ultrasonic treatment to promote a uniform distribution.  
   • Target Loading: Achieve a nominal Fe loading of 6.5 wt%.

5. Drying and Calcination  
   • Drying: Conducted overnight at 50 °C under vacuum to remove excess moisture.  
   • Calcination: Heat the impregnated sample under argon flow at 500 °C for 2 h, facilitating the formation and dispersion of iron species on the support.

6. Post‑treatment  
   • Passivation: Expose the calcined catalyst to 1 vol% O₂ in Ar for 1 h to stabilize the Fe species.

This structured procedure, integrating controlled MOF formation, precise impregnation, and tailored thermal treatments, ensures reproducibility and facilitates direct comparisons of synthesis complexity, scalability, and cost relative to traditional methods.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/CNS(1000)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 0.33 g Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O  
  • 0.99 g 2‑methylimidazole  
  • 90 mL deionized water (prepared separately for each reagent)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Mix the precursor solutions at room temperature  
  • Magnetically stir the combined solution for 24 hours  
- **Process**:  
  • Dissolve each reagent in its respective aqueous solution, then combine them to initiate ZIF-8 nanosheet formation.  
  • Stir continuously to ensure a homogeneous mixture and complete reaction of the building blocks.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Confirm complete dissolution and uniform mixing before proceeding.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Prepared ZIF-8 nanosheets  
  • 0.214 g ammonium iron citrate dissolved in 1 mL deionized water  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Incipient wetness impregnation under stirring; followed by ultrasonic treatment  
- **Process**:  
  • Evenly impregnate the CNS pores with the iron precursor solution.  
  • Use ultrasound briefly to enhance distribution.

##### Step 3: Purification  
- **Process**:  
  • Centrifuge and wash the precipitated material to remove impurities.  
  • Dry the washed samples at 50 °C for 12 hours under vacuum.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  • Maintain at 50 °C overnight to ensure complete removal of solvent.  
- **Calcination**:  
  • Perform in a tube furnace under argon flow at a ramp rate of 5 °C/min, reaching 500 °C and holding for 2 hours.

##### Step 5: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • Passivate by exposing the calcined catalyst to a 1 vol% O₂/Ar mixture for 1 hour.  
  • Optionally, a subsequent reduction in H₂ at 350 °C (15 mL/min for 4 hours) may be applied for final activation.

#### Catalyst Variations:  
For Fe/CNS(T), Fe/CP, and Fe/AC catalysts, the workflow order remains identical. Variations include adjustments in MOF precursor type (monoclinic ZIF-8 nanosheets versus cubic particles or commercial AC), carbonization temperatures (900, 950, 1000, or 1100 °C) to modulate nitrogen species, and subtle changes in impregnation or post-treatment conditions to achieve the target nominal 6.5 wt% Fe loading. Each step is precisely controlled to ensure reproducibility and optimal performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to identify crystalline phases (Fe, Fe₅C₂, Fe₃O₄) and to confirm the disappearance of MOF features after pyrolysis. Prior to measurement, catalyst samples were evacuated at 300 °C for 4 hours to remove adsorbed moisture and impurities.
- **Instruments and Models**: Rigaku D/max‑2500 X‑ray diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were taken using Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 200 mA, scanning the range of 2θ from 10° to 90°.
- **Test Parameters**: A scanning speed of 5°/min was used.
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment is critical to avoid errors during diffraction.

### 4.2 H₂‑Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR was conducted to assess the reducibility of the catalysts. Samples were pretreated in an Ar atmosphere at 200 °C for 1 hour, then cooled to room temperature.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micro fixed-bed reactor with a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: A 10% H₂/90% Ar mixture was passed through the catalyst as the temperature ramped from 50 °C to 600 °C.
- **Test Parameters**: The gradual temperature increase allowed continuous monitoring of H₂ consumption.
- **Key Notes**: Pre‐treatment ensures removal of residual contaminants for reproducible reduction profiles.

### 4.3 CO‑Temperature‑Programmed Desorption (CO‑TPD)
- **Operational Description**: The CO‑TPD experiment evaluated the catalyst’s CO adsorption capacity. Catalysts were first reduced at 350 °C in H₂ for 2 hours.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics Autochem II 2920 chemisorption apparatus.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Post‑reduction, samples were exposed to 10% CO in Ar at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by He purging for another hour. CO desorption was monitored from 25 °C to 900 °C.
- **Test Parameters**: Desorption profiles provide insights into surface adsorption energies.
- **Key Notes**: Temperature ramps and gas flow consistency are essential for accurate desorption analysis.

### 4.4 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: Nitrogen adsorption isotherms were measured at –196 °C to determine surface area and pore size distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2460.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were evacuated at 300 °C for 4 hours before analysis.
- **Test Parameters**: Surface areas were derived using the BET method, with pore volumes calculated at P/P₀ = 0.95 and micropore contributions assessed via the t‑plot and NLDFT methods.
- **Key Notes**: Consistent sample pre-treatment enhances reproducibility.

### 4.5 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)
- **Operational Description**: SEM analysis provided morphology and surface structure details.
- **Instruments and Models**: Hitachi S‑4800 field‑emission microscope.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV.
- **Key Notes**: Although specific sample coating was not detailed, electron beam settings were optimized for imaging catalyst morphology.

### 4.6 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)
- **Operational Description**: TEM, integrated with EDS mapping, was used to observe Fe nanoparticle dispersion and to analyze elemental distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: Philips TESNAI G2 F20 system at 100 kV.
- **Test Parameters**: Particle size distributions were calculated based on over 300 nanoparticles.
- **Key Notes**: EDS mapping in dark field confirmed uniform Fe dispersion within the carbon support.

### 4.7 X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: XPS assessed the chemical states of Fe and N species.
- **Instruments and Models**: ThermoFisher Scientific spectrometer with an Al Kα source.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Tests were conducted in a high‑vacuum environment (≈5×10⁻⁸ Pa) with samples pretreated at 300 °C for 4 hours.
- **Test Parameters**: Binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV.
- **Key Notes**: Accurate surface state analysis depends on stringent sample pre‑treatment protocols.

### 4.8 ⁵⁷Fe Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: This technique quantified Fe species distribution and hyperfine parameters.
- **Instruments and Models**: Topologic 500A spectrometer with a proportional counter.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed at room temperature.
- **Test Parameters**: Velocity calibration was achieved using a standard α‑Fe foil, and spectra fitting was performed with the MossWinn 3.0i software.
- **Key Notes**: Overlapping features in iron carbides may limit the detailed identification of all Fe phases.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for decomposing the iron precursor and facilitating the formation of active iron species on the carbon support.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is calcined at 500 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature ensures complete precursor decomposition and promotes strong metal-support interactions, thereby setting the stage for well-dispersed Fe(II)/Fe(III) species on the N-doped carbon framework.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Duration
- **Definition:** The duration of the calcination process is vital to achieve complete transformation of the precursor while avoiding overexposure that might induce sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The calcination is maintained for 2 hours.
- **Observations:** A 2-hour treatment provides sufficient time for uniform phase development, ensuring reproducible dispersion and stability without excessive particle growth.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Atmosphere and Gas Composition
- **Definition:** The choice of gas and atmospheric conditions during activation governs the reduction kinetics and preserves the integrity of both the iron species and the carbon support.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The calcination is performed under an inert argon atmosphere with a flow rate of 120 mL/min. Following calcination, the catalyst is passivated using a 1 vol% O₂/Ar mixture.
- **Observations:** The inert Ar atmosphere prevents unwanted oxidation during high-temperature treatment, while the subsequent passivation step forms a thin, controlled oxide layer on the catalyst surface. This protective layer stabilizes the active sites against rapid oxidation during storage and handling prior to the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Atmospheric pressure conditions are maintained to ensure consistency in gas-phase interactions during the activation process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Although not numerically specified, activation is conducted under ambient pressure.
- **Observations:** Maintaining atmospheric pressure allows for controlled gas dynamics during both calcination and passivation, supporting reproducible phase formation critical for catalytic performance.

Collectively, these activation parameters are tailored to optimize the catalyst’s active phase formation and dispersion, ensuring its readiness for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer-Tropsch reaction was performed in a tubular fixed-bed reactor with an inner diameter of 8.0 mm. The reaction conditions were carefully controlled to ensure reproducibility and to highlight the catalytic advantages of the Fe/CNS(1000) system relative to Fe/CP and Fe/AC.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is key for activating syngas and promoting Fe carburization, which enhances intrinsic catalytic activity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was conducted at 340 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature was found to be optimal, ensuring effective carburization of Fe and stable catalyst performance, as evidenced by a maintained carbon balance and uniform Fe dispersion.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reactor pressure affects the concentration of reactants and the formation of higher hydrocarbons in the Fischer-Tropsch process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were carried out at a pressure of 1 MPa.
- **Observations:** This pressure condition promoted enhanced reaction kinetics and contributed to the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons, consistent with the controlled environment needed for high catalytic conversion.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio determines the balance between hydrogenation and polymerization reactions, influencing product distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The feed gas comprised 45% CO, 45% H₂, and 10% Ar, corresponding to an H₂/CO ratio of 1.
- **Observations:** This balanced ratio supports efficient hydrocarbon synthesis while minimizing excess hydrogenation, thus optimizing the selectivity towards desired hydrocarbon products.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) represents the ratio of the syngas flow rate to the catalyst mass, controlling the contact time between the gas and the active sites.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was maintained between 9000 and 14,000 mL·g⁻¹·h⁻¹.
- **Observations:** This controlled flow allowed for sufficient reactant exposure, enabling consistent CO conversion and stability over extended time-on-stream, which was critical to evaluate the accelerator effect of N-doping in inhibiting Fe particle aggregation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD), providing insight into the active phases relevant for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS).
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of Fe/CNS(1000) reveal the disappearance of the original ZIF-8 MOF features after pyrolysis, with no residual zinc peaks, indicating complete framework collapse and Zn evaporation. The resulting carbon support exhibits broad peaks at 24–26° and 44°, corresponding to the (002) and (101) planes of graphitic carbon. After Fe loading, new peaks at 30.1°, 35.4°, 43.0°, 57.0°, and 62.5° are assigned to Fe₃O₄, while peaks at 44.7° and 82.3° correspond to metallic Fe. A broad feature at 41–43° indicates the presence of iron carbide (Fe₅C₂), which is further confirmed by Mössbauer spectroscopy.
- **Observations:** The formation of Fe₅C₂ is particularly significant, as this phase is recognized as the active component for FTS, directly correlating with enhanced CO conversion and catalytic activity. The N-doped carbon support promotes Fe carburization and the stabilization of active carbide phases.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Chemical State
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the surface elemental composition and the chemical states of Fe and N species.
- **Findings:** XPS analysis shows that Fe in Fe/CNS(1000) exists primarily as Fe(II) and Fe(III), with minor Fe₅C₂. The Fe 3p binding energies for Fe/CNS(1000) and Fe/CP are shifted to lower values compared to Fe/AC, indicating increased electron density on Fe due to N-doping and higher graphitization. N 1s spectra are deconvoluted into pyridinic (~398.6 eV), pyrrolic (~400.1 eV), graphitic (~401.1 eV), and oxidized N (~403.1 eV) species, with similar distributions for CNS(1000) and CP.
- **Observations:** The presence of pyrrolic N, which increases up to 1000 °C, is identified as the main electron donor, enhancing Fe–C interactions and facilitating Fe carburization. N-doping also strengthens Fe–CO back-donation, as evidenced by lower Fe 3p binding energies.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption–desorption (BET) measurements were conducted to assess surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which are critical for reactant diffusion and Fe dispersion.
- **Findings:** ZIF-8-derived carbon nanosheets (CNS(1000)) exhibit high surface area and abundant micro- and mesoporosity. After Fe loading, both surface area and pore volume decrease significantly, indicating that Fe species occupy the pores. The two-dimensional morphology of CNS(1000) is preserved, providing open channels for mass transport.
- **Observations:** The high porosity and nanosheet structure facilitate uniform Fe dispersion and improved reactant diffusion, contributing to superior catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM), along with EDX mapping, were used to examine catalyst morphology and Fe particle distribution.
- **Findings:** SEM images confirm the retention of thin, sheet-like morphology in CNS(1000) after carbonization and Fe loading. TEM reveals that Fe particles are uniformly dispersed on CNS(1000) and CP, with average sizes around 5.0 nm, smaller than those on Fe/AC (~6.7 nm). EDX mapping demonstrates homogeneous distribution of Fe and N in the nanosheet support.
- **Observations:** The combination of two-dimensional morphology and N-doping ensures fine and stable Fe dispersion, minimizing sintering and aggregation during reaction.

#### 6.1.5 Iron Species Quantification (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** ^57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy was utilized to quantify the distribution of Fe species in both fresh and spent catalysts.
- **Findings:** Fresh catalysts predominantly contain Fe(II)/Fe(III) and a small proportion of Fe₅C₂. After FTS, the content of Fe₅C₂ increases, especially in Fe/CNS(1000), correlating with higher catalytic activity.
- **Observations:** The ability of the N-doped nanosheet support to promote and stabilize active Fe₅C₂ phases is directly linked to the observed superior FTS performance.

#### 6.1.6 Reducibility and CO Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and CO temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD) were performed to evaluate catalyst reducibility and CO adsorption behavior.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR profiles show that reduction peaks shift to lower temperatures with increasing pyrolysis temperature, indicating enhanced reducibility due to N-doping. CO-TPD reveals higher CO adsorption capacity and distinct desorption profiles for Fe/CNS(1000) and Fe/CP compared to Fe/AC.
- **Observations:** Enhanced reducibility and CO adsorption, attributed to N-doping and higher graphitization, are critical for efficient FTS catalysis.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flows of CO into and out of the reactor, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (340 °C, 1 MPa, GHSV 9000 mL g⁻¹ h⁻¹, syngas: 45% CO, 45% H₂, 10% Ar), Fe/CNS(1000) achieves a CO conversion of 45.9%. For comparison, Fe/CP and Fe/AC yield 22.1% and 7.7%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** N-doped catalysts (Fe/CNS(1000), Fe/CP) exhibit significantly higher CO conversion than the N-free Fe/AC, attributed to enhanced Fe dispersion and electronic effects from nitrogen species. The CO conversion for Fe/CNS(T) follows a volcano trend with carbonization temperature, peaking at 1000 °C.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe/CNS(1000) displays a CO₂ selectivity of 29.4%. Across Fe/CNS(T) catalysts, CO₂ selectivity ranges from 28.6% to 33.0%, showing minimal dependence on carbonization temperature or N-doping.
- **Observations and Trends:** The relatively stable CO₂ selectivity suggests that N-doping and support morphology primarily influence CO conversion and hydrocarbon selectivity rather than water-gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe/CNS(1000) achieves a CH₄ selectivity of 22.4%. Fe/CP shows 23.6%, while Fe/AC is lower at 13.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** N-doped catalysts favor higher methane formation, attributed to increased electron density on Fe and stronger Fe–C interactions, as supported by XPS and product analysis.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe/CNS(1000) exhibits a C₅⁺ selectivity of 39.2%. Fe/CP and Fe/AC show 37.2% and 62.3%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** N-doping reduces C₅⁺ selectivity, shifting product distribution toward lighter hydrocarbons, consistent with enhanced Fe–C bonding and modified chain growth dynamics.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, with values closer to 1 favoring longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** The article correlates higher α values with increased C₅⁺ selectivity; Fe/AC, with the highest C₅⁺ selectivity, exhibits the highest α, while Fe/CNS(1000) and Fe/CP have lower α values, consistent with their product distributions.
- **Observations and Trends:** N-doping decreases the α factor, favoring methane and short-chain hydrocarbons over long-chain products.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time-on-stream (TOS). Deactivation is indicated by a decline in CO conversion.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe/CNS(1000) and Fe/CP maintain stable CO conversion over 100 h TOS, while Fe/AC deactivates rapidly, with significant loss of activity after 15 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior stability of N-doped catalysts is attributed to the anchoring effect of nitrogen, which inhibits Fe particle sintering and preserves active sites during prolonged operation.
