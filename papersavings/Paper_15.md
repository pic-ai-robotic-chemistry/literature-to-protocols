## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of FeK/AC catalysts with varying copper loadings is performed via an incipient‑wetness impregnation method. This method is specifically designed to ensure uniform dispersion of metal precursors on the activated carbon support, supporting reproducibility and scalability. The following step-by-step procedure details the experimental workflow:

#### 1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation
• An aqueous solution containing ferric nitrate and cupric nitrate is prepared to achieve a nominal loading of 15.7 wt % Fe along with Cu loadings of 0, 0.8, and 2.0 wt %.  
• The activated carbon support is impregnated with this solution, ensuring that the metal species are distributed evenly across the surface.

#### 2. Precipitation–Deposition
• Following the initial impregnation, a separate aqueous solution of potassium nitrate (nominally 0.9 wt % K) is introduced.  
• This step functions as a precipitation–deposition process, whereby the potassium species are deposited uniformly onto the already impregnated support, promoting better catalyst activity through enhanced metal interactions.

#### 3. Drying
• The impregnated materials are dried in air overnight at a temperature range of 90–100 °C.  
• This controlled drying step is essential to remove excess solvent and to prepare the catalyst precursor for thermal treatment without inducing inhomogeneities.

#### 4. Calcination
• The dried catalyst is calcined at 500 °C for 2 hours under a flowing nitrogen (N₂) atmosphere.  
• Calcination not only stabilizes the metal precursors on the support but also facilitates the formation of specific iron-containing phases necessary for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5. Additional Post‑treatments
• Prior to catalytic reaction, the catalyst undergoes in situ reduction using hydrogen (H₂) at 400 °C under a pressure of 0.5 MPa for 12 hours.  
• This reduction step is crucial to activate the iron and copper species, enhancing the subsequent catalytic performance.

This incipient‑wetness impregnation sequence—starting with metal precursor mixing, followed by controlled precipitation–deposition, and finalized through drying, calcination, and reduction—ensures uniform active phase dispersion and reproducible catalyst characteristics, aligning with the reproducibility requirements detailed in the article.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the preparation of FeK/AC catalysts with varying copper loadings (0Cu, 1Cu, and 2Cu) is detailed using an incipient‐wetness impregnation method. The following subsections document the operations for weighing solid reagents, transferring the corresponding aqueous solutions, and mixing to achieve a homogeneous impregnation on the activated carbon support.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

For reproducible preparation, the solid reagent amounts are calculated to meet the targeted loadings of 15.7 wt % Fe, 0.9 wt % K, and the nominal Cu levels (0, 0.8, and 2.0 wt %).

##### 3.1.1.1 0Cu Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed to provide 15.7 wt % Fe on the support using an analytical balance.
- **KNO₃**: Weighed to achieve 0.9 wt % K incorporation.

##### 3.1.1.2 1Cu Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed to secure the required 15.7 wt % Fe.
- **Cu(NO₃)₂·3H₂O**: Weighed to yield a copper loading of 0.8 wt %.
- **KNO₃**: Weighed to contribute 0.9 wt % K.

##### 3.1.1.3 2Cu Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed to deliver 15.7 wt % Fe.
- **Cu(NO₃)₂·3H₂O**: Weighed to attain a 2.0 wt % Cu level.
- **KNO₃**: Weighed to fulfill the 0.9 wt % K requirement.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

The catalyst precursors are prepared as aqueous solutions prior to impregnation.

##### 3.1.2.1 0Cu Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃ aqueous solution**: Transferred using a pipette into the vessel containing the activated carbon support.
- **KNO₃ aqueous solution**: Poured (50 mL typical volume) with a graduated cylinder.

##### 3.1.2.2 1Cu Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃ and Cu(NO₃)₂ aqueous solutions**: Sequentially transferred with pipettes to ensure even distribution over the support.
- **KNO₃ aqueous solution**: Transferred using a graduated cylinder.

##### 3.1.2.3 2Cu Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃ and Cu(NO₃)₂ aqueous solutions**: Delivered sequentially via pipette onto the support.
- **KNO₃ aqueous solution**: Poured using a graduated cylinder.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

To achieve a uniform distribution of reagents on the support, the mixed slurry is subjected to stirring and ultrasonic treatment.

##### 3.1.3.1 0Cu Catalyst
- **Impregnated AC**: Mixed at 600 rpm for 20 minutes at ambient temperature.

##### 3.1.3.2 1Cu Catalyst
- **Impregnated AC**: Stirred at 600 rpm for 20 minutes and ultrasonically treated for 10 minutes.

##### 3.1.3.3 2Cu Catalyst
- **Impregnated AC**: Mixed at 600 rpm for 20 minutes, followed by 10 minutes of ultrasonication under an inert atmosphere to ensure homogeneity.

This detailed preparation protocol, including catalyst‐specific grouping and clearly documented steps, supports full reproducibility for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: FeK/AC (0Cu)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 
  - Ferric nitrate (amount corresponding to 15.7 wt % Fe)
  - Deionized water (sufficient to fully dissolve the salt)
- **Conditions**: 
  - Room temperature with constant stirring
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the required ferric nitrate in deionized water to form a homogeneous solution ensuring complete dissolution.
  - Note: Since no copper is added for the 0Cu catalyst, no cupric nitrate is used.
- **Key Steps**: 
  - Ensure full dissolution for reproducibility in subsequent impregnation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**:
  - The prepared ferric nitrate solution
  - Aqueous solution of potassium nitrate (providing 0.9 wt % K)
  - Peat-derived activated carbon (previously washed in hot distilled water and calcined at 500 °C for 2 hours in flowing N₂)
- **Conditions**:
  - Incipient wetness impregnation performed at ambient conditions
- **Process**:
  - Impregnate the pre-treated activated carbon sequentially: first, evenly distribute the ferric nitrate solution, then introduce the potassium nitrate solution.
  - Allow the liquid to be completely adsorbed by the support.
- **Key Steps**:
  - Maintain uniform distribution of precursors to avoid local concentration gradients.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Process**:
  - After impregnation, let the catalyst dry in air overnight at 90–100 °C.
  - Calcine the dried precursor at a ramp rate (not specified) to reach a final temperature of 500 °C and maintain for 2 hours in a flowing N₂ atmosphere.
- **Key Steps**:
  - Consistent drying and calcination are critical for the formation of a stable active phase.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Conditions**:
  - In situ reduction under H₂ at 400 °C, 0.5 MPa for 12 hours (with an H₂ flow rate around 3 nL/(g·h))
- **Process**:
  - After calcination, reduce the catalyst in situ to generate the active phase for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: FeK/AC with Cu Promotion (1Cu and 2Cu)

The steps are analogous except for the incorporation of cupric nitrate in the precursor solution:
- **Reagents**:
  - In addition to ferric nitrate and potassium nitrate as above, add cupric nitrate to achieve 0.8 wt % for 1Cu or 2.0 wt % for 2Cu catalysts.
- **Process Adjustments**:
  - Dissolve both ferric and cupric nitrates concurrently ensuring complete dissolution and homogeneous mixing.
  - Proceed with impregnation, drying, calcination, and reduction, preserving the identical conditions outlined above.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Particle Size (XRD)
- **Description:** X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to identify the crystalline phases present in the catalysts after Fischer-Tropsch synthesis and to estimate particle sizes. The identification of iron oxide and carbide phases is critical, as these are directly linked to catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis of the spent 1Cu catalyst revealed the presence of Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) and Fe₃C₂ (iron carbide) phases, with particle sizes of 28 nm and 21 nm, respectively. The transformation from Fe oxides to iron carbides is indicative of the formation of active sites for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.
- **Observations:** The coexistence of Fe₃O₄ and Fe₃C₂ suggests incomplete reduction or possible re-oxidation during reaction, which may negatively impact catalyst activity. The formation of FeCu bimetallic clusters and increased carbon deposition at higher Cu loadings were also noted, potentially lowering overall FTS and WGS reaction rates despite improved reduction kinetics.

#### 6.1.2 Reduction Behavior (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was used to assess the reducibility of iron species and the promotional effect of copper. Shifts in reduction peak temperatures provide insight into the ease of iron oxide reduction and the interaction between Fe and Cu.
- **Findings:** The addition of Cu shifted the first four reduction peaks down by 20–80 °C compared to the Cu-free catalyst, indicating that Cu facilitates iron reduction. For the 2Cu catalyst, the first two reduction peaks merged into a single large peak, reflecting a much faster reduction process. The third reduction peak was larger in Cu-containing catalysts, and the final negative peak (associated with CO emission) was smaller, indicating more complete iron reduction.
- **Observations:** Enhanced reduction kinetics are attributed to hydrogen spillover from metallic Cu to iron oxides. However, excessive Cu loading can suppress iron carbonization and promote carbon deposition, which may decrease catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition (EDS)
- **Description:** Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) provided elemental analysis of the catalyst surfaces, confirming the presence of main and trace elements.
- **Findings:** EDS analysis identified carbon and oxygen as the primary elements, with trace amounts of silicon, calcium, and magnesium originating from the peat-based activated carbon support.
- **Observations:** The elemental composition confirms the purity and expected makeup of the support and catalysts, with no significant contamination detected.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Chemistry (XPS)
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to evaluate surface chemical states and adsorption properties, particularly regarding CO adsorption and spillover effects.
- **Findings:** XPS revealed that Cu-containing catalysts exhibited increased O(1s) signal intensity, indicating enhanced associative CO adsorption and supporting a CO-spillover mechanism. Graphitic carbon and carbonate-like species were detected on the Cu-containing catalyst after H₂/CO exposure, while the Cu-free catalyst showed more dissociative CO adsorption.
- **Observations:** The presence of Cu alters surface chemistry, promoting carbon deposition and modifying the nature of CO adsorption, which correlates with changes in product selectivity.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Area and Porosity (BET)
- **Description:** Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was conducted to determine the specific surface area and pore characteristics of the activated carbon support.
- **Findings:** The peat-based activated carbon support exhibited a high specific surface area of 606 m²/g, with 75% of this area attributed to micropores and an average pore diameter of 5.9 nm.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and favorable pore structure facilitate metal dispersion and provide abundant active sites, contributing to enhanced catalyst activity and stability.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (260–290 °C, 300 psig, H₂/CO = 0.9), CO conversion values at 280 °C were: 0Cu: 85.7%, 1Cu: 45.3%, 2Cu: 52.5%. At 290 °C, 1Cu achieved 55.5% and 2Cu reached 65.3%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The undoped catalyst (0Cu) exhibited the highest conversion at 280 °C. At 290 °C, increased Cu loading (2Cu) led to higher conversion than 1Cu, indicating that Cu promotion enhances iron reduction and active site formation at elevated temperatures.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicative of water–gas shift (WGS) activity:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 280 °C, CO₂ selectivity was: 0Cu: 47.5%, 1Cu: 45.2%, 2Cu: 44.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Cu addition consistently reduced CO₂ selectivity, demonstrating suppression of the WGS reaction across all tested temperatures.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 280 °C, CH₄ selectivity was: 0Cu: 8.6%, 1Cu: 7.5%, 2Cu: 7.0%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane selectivity decreased slightly with Cu addition. At early reaction times, Cu-containing catalysts showed marginally higher CH₄ selectivity, attributed to enhanced carbon deposition and surface carbon species formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C₅⁺ Selectivity} = \frac{\text{C₅⁺ hydrocarbons produced}}{\text{Total hydrocarbons produced}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 270 °C, C₅⁺ selectivity was: 0Cu: 59.0 wt%, 1Cu: 60.6 wt%, 2Cu: 60.5 wt%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Cu promotion slightly increased C₅⁺ selectivity, favoring heavier hydrocarbon production and altering olefin/paraffin ratios due to enhanced hydrogenation and isomerization.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory model, indicates the probability of chain growth:
\[
\text{ASF:} \quad \log(W_n/n) = n \log(\alpha) + \text{constant}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** Cu-containing catalysts (1Cu, 2Cu) exhibited slightly higher α values than 0Cu, reflecting increased chain growth probability.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced hydrogenation and H₂ spillover from Cu led to subtle shifts in product distribution, with a modest increase in long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time on stream}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** All catalysts (0Cu, 1Cu, 2Cu) displayed similar deactivation slopes, as determined from conversion vs. time-on-stream data.
- **Observations and Trends:** Cu addition did not significantly affect catalyst stability; deactivation rates remained comparable across all formulations.

#### 6.2.7 Oxygenate Selectivity
- **Description:** Oxygenate selectivity is the fraction of converted CO forming oxygenated products (e.g., methanol, C₃–C₅ alcohols).
- **Experimental Data:** Cu-containing catalysts showed 50–70% higher oxygenate selectivity than 0Cu, with maximum values below 3 wt% at ~270 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** Cu promoted molecular CO adsorption and inhibited CO dissociation, enhancing oxygenate formation via the CO insertion mechanism. However, excessive Cu (2Cu) slightly reduced oxygenate selectivity compared to 1Cu, likely due to FeCu phase formation or site blockage.
