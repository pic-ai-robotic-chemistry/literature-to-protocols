## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis employs a sequential impregnation procedure designed to achieve high dispersion of active cobalt and group VI metal oxide phases on the CeO₂ support. This method is chosen for its precise control over the metal loading and strong metal–support interactions, despite its complexity relative to co‑precipitation or hydrothermal alternatives.

#### Sequential Impregnation Procedure

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – An aqueous solution of the group VI metal precursor is prepared using one of the following: hexaammonium heptamolybdate 4‑hydrate ((NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄·4H₂O) for Mo, chromium nitrate nonahydrate (Cr(NO₃)₃·9H₂O) for Cr, or ammonium tungstate para pentahydrate ((NH₄)₁₀W₁₂O₄₁·5H₂O) for W.  
  – The solution is impregnated onto the CeO₂ support maintaining a constant metal/ceria molar ratio of 0.23 mol/mol.  

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – This step is integrated into the impregnation process. No separate deposition step is reported, as the wet‐impregnation achieves uniform distribution of the metal precursor.

3. • Drying  
  – The impregnated support is evaporated to dryness to remove the aqueous solvent, ensuring reproducibility in subsequent processing.

4. • Cobalt Nitrate Impregnation and Shaping  
  – A subsequent aqueous solution of cobalt nitrate hexahydrate is added to the dried group VI metal–CeO₂ precursor.  
  – The sample is then pressurized at 60 MPa for 5 minutes, a step which shapes the catalyst and enhances material integrity, followed by pulverization to a mesh size of 20–80.

5. • First Calcination  
  – The material is calcined in air at 450 °C for 12 hours, stabilizing the metal oxides and promoting strong metal–support interactions.

6. • Potassium Precursor Impregnation and Second Calcination  
  – An aqueous solution of potassium precursor (K₂CO₃ or KNO₃) is added to the calcined solid, followed by drying.  
  – A second calcination at 450 °C for 12 hours finalizes the catalyst synthesis, ensuring optimal formation of the desired active phases.

This method not only allows for precise control of catalyst composition but also facilitates the formation of mixed-metal sulfide phases during subsequent activation, a critical factor for achieving high CO hydrogenation performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: KxCoyzMCe

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: An aqueous solution is prepared by dissolving the group VI metal precursor (e.g., hexaammonium heptamolybdate for Mo, chromium nitrate nonahydrate for Cr, or ammonium tungstate for W) in deionized water. In addition, cobalt nitrate hexahydrate and a potassium precursor (KNO₃ or K₂CO₃) are used. The molar ratio of group VI metal to ceria is precisely maintained at 0.23. Specific quantities are determined based on targeted loadings (e.g., 10–20 wt.% with 15 wt.% observed as optimal).  
- **Conditions**: The metal precursor is dissolved at ambient temperature with stirring until a homogeneous solution is achieved. The pH adjustment is performed during reagent mixing, ensuring complete dissolution and proper precipitation where applicable.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Process**:  
  • First, impregnate the ceria support with the prepared aqueous solution of the group VI metal precursor and allow it to dry by evaporating excess water.  
  • Following drying, introduce an aqueous solution of cobalt nitrate hexahydrate under continuous stirring to ensure even distribution.  
  • Uniform mixing is assisted by pressurizing the impregnated sample at 60 MPa for 5 minutes prior to pulverizing it to a particle size between 20–80 mesh.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Purification**: While no washing or centrifugation is applied between impregnation and drying, the procedure relies on thorough reagent mixing to secure reagent binding.  
- **Drying & Calcination**: The dried sample undergoes calcination in air at 450 °C for 12 hours. After potassium addition, a second drying step is followed by a repeat calcination at identical conditions. Key steps include controlled temperature and even drying to prevent uneven precursor distribution.

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Process**: The final activation is achieved by in situ sulfidation using a 5 vol.% H₂S/95 vol.% H₂ gas mixture at 400 °C with a ramp rate of 5 °C/min for 3 hours. This step promotes the formation of the Co–Mo–S phase and the appearance of active thiol groups, as verified by XPS and H₂-TPR analyses.

Key conditions and reagent integration at every step ensure reproducibility and high catalyst dispersion for effective Fischer–Tropsch CO hydrogenation performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis involves a dedicated sulfidation treatment designed to convert precursor oxides into the active mixed-metal sulfide phase, particularly the Co–Mo–S phase. This activation is exclusively performed as part of the catalyst synthesis and pre-reaction treatment, ensuring that the catalyst surfaces are modified for optimal performance during CO hydrogenation.

#### Activation Temperature
Activation temperature is critical for promoting the desired sulfidation reactions. The catalyst is subjected to a sulfidation temperature of 400 °C. This temperature has been chosen to efficiently convert metal oxides into their sulfided forms, favoring the formation of active sulfide phases while avoiding excessive reduction that could compromise catalyst stability.

#### Activation Pressure
The sulfidation process is carried out under atmospheric pressure. Operating at atmospheric pressure ensures a controlled environment for the gas–solid interactions necessary for effective catalyst activation.

#### Activation Gas Composition and Flow
For the chemical conversion, a precise activation gas mixture composed of 5 vol.% H₂S and 95 vol.% H₂ is employed. This composition is essential for transforming the oxide precursors into sulfide phases and for generating surface thiol groups. Moreover, the activation gas is introduced at a controlled flow rate of 30 mL/min, which guarantees uniform exposure of catalyst particles to the reactive atmosphere.

#### Temperature Ramp Rate and Activation Time
A temperature ramp rate of 5 °C/min is utilized to gradually bring the catalyst to the target activation temperature. Once 400 °C is reached, the activation proceeds for a total duration of 3 hours. This period is sufficient to achieve full conversion of the surface oxides while preserving the dispersion of the active metals.

Overall, this sulfidation protocol at atmospheric pressure and 400 °C in a 5 vol.% H₂S/95 vol.% H₂ atmosphere has been reported to enhance CO conversion, improve selectivity toward C₂⁺ alcohols, and minimize undesired hydrocarbon formation. The controlled activation conditions thereby ensure reproducibility and optimal performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

- **Observations:** This parameter is optimized for a balance between sufficient contact time for effective conversion and the prevention of catalyst saturation, thereby ensuring reproducibility and consistency in the observed catalytic performance.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the KxCoyzMCe catalysts were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) to assess metal dispersion and the presence of crystalline phases. High dispersion of active metals is critical for maximizing catalytic activity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns, collected using a Rigaku RAD-IIC diffractometer (Cu Kα, 40 kV, 30 mA, 2θ = 5–80°, 1.5°/min), revealed only the characteristic peaks of the ceria support at 21.9°, 25.0°, 30.0°, 40.0°, 45.0°, 47.8°, 54.3°, and 57.0°. No peaks attributable to crystalline phases of the active metals (Mo, W, Cr, or Co) were detected.
- **Observations:** The absence of separate metal oxide or sulfide peaks confirms that the active metals are highly dispersed or present as amorphous phases, which is essential for high catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** The textural properties, including specific surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter, were determined by N₂ adsorption using the BET method. These parameters influence reactant accessibility and catalyst efficiency.
- **Findings:** For Mo-based catalysts, increasing Mo loading led to a decrease in BET surface area:  
  - 10 wt.% Mo: 71.6 m²/g  
  - 15 wt.% Mo: 63.6 m²/g  
  - 20 wt.% Mo: 48.3 m²/g  
  The corresponding pore volumes and average pore diameters for K005Co0620MoCe (20 wt.% Mo) were 0.13 cm³/g and 11 nm, respectively.
- **Observations:** Higher metal loadings reduce surface area and pore volume, indicating partial pore blockage or agglomeration. This trend is associated with decreased CO conversion at higher loadings, though alcohol selectivity remains stable above 15 wt.% Mo.

#### 6.1.3 Redox Properties and Sulfur Species
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction with hydrogen (H₂-TPR) was used to probe the reducibility and sulfur species present in the catalysts, which are linked to the formation of active phases for higher alcohol synthesis.
- **Findings:** Sulfided catalysts exhibited three main reduction peaks:  
  - 329 °C (reduction of thiol, –SH, groups)  
  - 831 °C (decomposition of CoMoS phase)  
  - 993 °C (reduction of MoS₂)  
  For Mo oxide catalysts, a dominant peak at 530–634 °C was observed, corresponding to partial reduction of Mo⁶⁺ to Mo⁴⁺, with a high-temperature peak at 864 °C for deep reduction.
- **Observations:** The presence of thiol groups, CoMoS, and MoS₂ phases is enhanced by sulfidation, correlating with improved C₂⁺ alcohol selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Chemical States (XPS Analysis)
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the chemical states and surface composition of Mo, Co, and S species, which are critical for catalytic function.
- **Findings:**  
  - **Mo 3d:** Mo⁴⁺ (228.6, 231.9 eV, MoS₂), Mo⁶⁺ (232.6, 235.7 eV), Mo⁵⁺ (230.0 eV, MoOₓSᵧ), S 2s (225.8 eV)  
  - **Co 2p:** Co₉S₈ (777.8 eV), Co–Mo–S (778.9 eV), Co²⁺ (781.4 eV)  
  - **S 2p:** S²⁻ (161.5 eV), S₂²⁻ (162.8 eV)
- **Observations:** The formation of MoS₂ and Co–Mo–S phases, along with surface thiol groups, is directly linked to higher C₂⁺ alcohol selectivity. Sulfidation increases the abundance of these active species.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition and Dopant Control
- **Description:** Elemental analysis and compositional control were verified by XPS and supported by the synthesis protocol, ensuring a constant group VI metal/ceria molar ratio of 0.23.
- **Findings:** XPS confirmed the presence and chemical states of all intended elements (Mo, Co, S, K, Ce), with compositional ratios matching the targeted values.
- **Observations:** Consistent elemental composition across samples supports the reproducibility of the synthesis method and the uniform dispersion of active phases.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (260–300 °C, 5.0 MPa, GHSV 5000 h⁻¹, H₂/CO = 1.0–2.0), ceria-supported Mo-based catalysts (e.g., K02Co0610MoCe) achieved a CO conversion of 3.2% at 260 °C and H₂/CO = 1.0. At 280 °C and H₂/CO = 2.0, both Cr- and Mo-based ceria-supported catalysts reached approximately 8.9% CO conversion, with W-based catalysts showing lower values. Increasing Mo loading to 15 wt.% (K005Co0615MoCe) raised CO conversion to 11% at 260 °C (H₂/CO = 2.0), but further increase to 20 wt.% reduced conversion to 7.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Ceria-supported catalysts consistently outperform silica-supported analogues (e.g., 3.2% vs. 0.9% CO conversion at 260 °C). Optimal Mo loading (15 wt.%) maximizes conversion, while excessive loading leads to performance decline due to reduced dispersion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Across all group VI metal-based ceria catalysts, CO₂ selectivity is approximately 10%. Ceria support reduces CO₂ selectivity compared to silica.
- **Observations and Trends:** Sulfidation activation slightly increases CO₂ selectivity, but differences among activation methods are minor.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ and Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
  Hydrocarbon selectivity encompasses all hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Data:** Hydrocarbon selectivity is highest for Cr-based catalysts (78%), followed by W, and lowest for Mo-based catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** As the catalyst composition shifts from Cr to Mo, hydrocarbon (including CH₄) selectivity decreases, while alcohol selectivity increases.

#### 6.2.4 C₂⁺ Alcohol Selectivity and Space–Time Yield
- **Description:** C₂⁺ alcohol selectivity is the carbon-based fraction of converted CO forming alcohols with two or more carbons. Space–time yield (STY) quantifies alcohol production per catalyst mass per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** For K(C)005Co0615MoCe, C₂⁺ alcohol selectivity reaches 37–38% at 15–20 wt.% Mo, with a STY of 0.61 mol/h/kg-cat. W- and Cr-based catalysts show lower selectivity and STY (W: 0.28, Cr: 0.074 mol/h/kg-cat.).
- **Observations and Trends:** Mo-based catalysts exhibit the highest C₂⁺ alcohol selectivity and STY, with the order Mo > W > Cr. Optimal performance is linked to the formation of Co–Mo–S phases.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth in product formation, typically derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not detailed, product distributions indicate higher chain growth probability for Mo-based, ceria-supported, and sulfided catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced C₂⁺ alcohol formation on these catalysts suggests a higher α, correlating with improved selectivity for longer-chain products.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring product yields over time at constant conditions.
- **Experimental Data:** For sulfided K005Co0620MoCe, space–time yields of C₂⁺ alcohols remain nearly constant (0.08 to 0.07 mol/h/kg-cat.) over 10.5 h at 260 °C, indicating negligible deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst demonstrates excellent stability, with no significant loss in activity or selectivity during extended operation.
