## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis is performed via a well‐defined three‐step process designed to achieve a phase‑pure (′)-carbide catalyst from a reducible iron oxide precursor. This method has been compared with more conventional routes and is favored for its simplicity, scalability, and avoidance of expensive or toxic chemicals.

#### Three‑Step Synthesis Protocol for (′)-Carbide Catalyst

1. **Precursor Reduction**
   - • Reagents: Iron oxide precursor.
   - • Conditions: Reduced in a H₂ flow at 430°C for 1 hour.
   - • Equipment: Stainless steel tubular fixed-bed reactor, with in situ X‑ray diffraction for monitoring the complete conversion to metallic iron.
   - • Observations: The conversion is confirmed by the disappearance of iron oxide peaks and the appearance of metallic iron signals.

2. **Pretreatment**
   - • Gas Composition: H₂/CO/N₂ = 2/1/10.
   - • Conditions: Exposure at 170°C for 40 minutes.
   - • Equipment: Fixed-bed reactor setup optimized for low‑temperature gas treatments.
   - • Role: Deposits a thin layer of surface carbon on the freshly reduced metallic iron, preparing it for subsequent carburization.

3. **Carburization**
   - • Procedure: Controlled temperature ramping from 170°C to 250°C at 0.5°C/min using a synthesis gas mixture (H₂/CO/N₂ = 3/2/2).
   - • Duration: 6 hours.
   - • Equipment: In situ reactors equipped for high‑precision temperature control.
   - • Outcome: Sufficient carbon diffusion into the iron matrix, resulting in the formation of phase‑pure (′)-Fe₂.₂C and a minor –Fe₂C component.

4. **Post‑treatment**
   - • Additional Steps: Controlled cooling to 235°C and pressurization to 23 bar to initiate the Fischer–Tropsch reaction, followed by passivation in a 1% O₂/He flow for 20 hours.
   - • Purpose: Ensures stabilization of the carbide phase while preventing unwanted metallic or mixed oxide phases.

This stepwise approach, by precisely controlling temperature, gas compositions, and durations, guarantees reproducibility and minimizes the formation of unwanted phases. The comparative advantages include operational simplicity, effective reduction/carburization, and enhanced catalyst performance under industrial Fischer–Tropsch conditions.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is executed through a sequential three-step procedure that prepares the catalyst for optimal Fischer–Tropsch performance by precisely controlling the chemical state and surface properties.

#### 5.1.1 Reduction Step
• **Definition:** The reduction step is crucial for converting the iron oxide precursor completely into metallic iron, which is necessary for subsequent carbide formation.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst precursor is reduced in an H₂ atmosphere at 430 °C for 1 hour.  
• **Observations:** This high-temperature treatment ensures complete removal of oxygen from the precursor, preventing residual iron oxide that could otherwise compromise the formation of the pure (′)-carbide phase.

#### 5.1.2 Pretreatment Step
• **Definition:** In the pretreatment phase, a controlled, low-temperature exposure to synthesis gas deposits a thin layer of surface carbon on the reduced metallic iron. This seeding is essential for guiding controlled carburization.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained at 170 °C for 40 minutes in a dilute syngas mixture with a H₂/CO/N₂ ratio of 2/1/10.  
• **Observations:** The mild conditions allow carbon deposition on the surface while preserving the metallic state. This step primes the catalyst by avoiding premature or uncontrolled bulk carburization.

#### 5.1.3 Carburization Step
• **Definition:** The carburization step facilitates the gradual diffusion of the predeposited carbon into the bulk, leading to the formation of a phase-pure (′)-carbide catalyst that exhibits low CO₂ selectivity and stable performance.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The temperature is ramped slowly from 170 °C to 250 °C at 0.5 °C/min, and then held at 250 °C for 6 hours using a synthesis gas mixture with a H₂/CO/N₂ ratio of 3/2/2.  
• **Observations:** The controlled temperature increase minimizes the risk of forming mixed phases and ensures uniform carbon uptake, resulting in a robust and phase-pure (′)-carbide structure.

This stepwise activation, from complete reduction to gentle pretreatment and controlled carburization, is fundamental for achieving the desired catalyst phase. The precision in temperature control, gas composition, and activation duration collectively contributes to maintaining high metal dispersion and minimizing sintering, thereby enabling reproducible low CO₂ selectivity and enhanced catalytic performance under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structural Purity
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and structural purity of the catalysts were determined using in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) and operando Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are critical for confirming the formation and stability of the desired (′)-carbide phase, which is directly linked to catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** In situ XRD patterns collected after each synthesis step revealed that the metallic iron precursor was fully converted to a phase-pure (′)-carbide following controlled carburization. No peaks corresponding to iron oxide or metallic iron were detected post-carburization, indicating complete transformation. Operando Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the phase composition under reaction conditions (235°C, 23–35 bar), showing the catalyst consisted of 88% (′)-Fe₂.₂C and 12% –Fe₂C after 12 hours on stream. This phase composition remained unchanged even after temperature excursions to 250°C and subsequent return to 235°C.
- **Observations:** The absence of iron oxide and metallic iron phases is directly correlated with the catalyst’s low CO₂ selectivity, as only the (′)-carbide phase is present to facilitate selective CO conversion to hydrocarbons and H₂O.

#### 6.1.2 Carbon Species and Carburization Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed hydrogenation (TPH) was employed to analyze carbon species present on the catalyst during synthesis, providing insight into the carburization process and the incorporation of carbon into the bulk structure.
- **Findings:** TPH profiles of the pretreated sample indicated surface carbon deposition on metallic iron, which was hydrogenated to methane at 350°C. After carburization, the TPH profile exhibited distinct peaks characteristic of carbon species in (′)-carbide, confirming successful bulk incorporation of carbon.
- **Observations:** The transition from surface carbon to bulk carbide is essential for achieving phase-pure (′)-carbide, and the controlled temperature ramping during carburization was critical in preventing the formation of mixed or unwanted iron phases.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Epitaxial Growth
- **Description:** High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) with fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis was used to investigate catalyst morphology, particle size, and the epitaxial relationship between Fe and –Fe₂C.
- **Findings:** HRTEM images revealed that –Fe₂C grows epitaxially on Fe, with FFT analysis showing lattice spacings of 2.1 Å (Fe(110)) and 2.0 Å (–Fe₂C(101)), and a mismatch angle of 4.7°. Inverse FFT images displayed Fe at the core and –Fe₂C at the surface, with dislocations at the interface accommodating lattice mismatch. The average particle sizes were determined to be 27 nm for R-Fe and 19 nm for Fe/SiO₂ catalysts.
- **Observations:** Maintaining particle sizes below 30 nm was found to be crucial for synthesizing phase-pure (′)-carbide, as larger particles led to mixed carbide phases. The observed epitaxial growth and uniform morphology are likely beneficial for catalytic stability and activity.

#### 6.1.4 Phase Stability Under Reaction Conditions
- **Description:** The stability of the (′)-carbide phase during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was monitored using operando Mössbauer spectroscopy and in situ XRD.
- **Findings:** Both techniques confirmed that the (′)-carbide phase remained dominant and stable throughout extended reaction times (up to 150 hours) and during temperature cycling. No significant phase transformation or reappearance of iron oxide or metallic iron was observed.
- **Observations:** The robust phase stability under industrially relevant conditions underpins the catalyst’s sustained low CO₂ selectivity and consistent performance.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO entering and exiting the reactor, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions (235°C, 23 bar, H₂/CO = 1.5, GHSV = 18,000 h⁻¹), the phase-pure (′)-carbide catalyst (primarily (′)-Fe₂.₂C with 12% –Fe₂C) achieved a stable CO conversion of approximately 15%. This value was consistently maintained over a 150-hour continuous operation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst demonstrated remarkable stability, with no significant decline in CO conversion throughout the long-term test. Transient temperature increases to 250°C temporarily elevated CO conversion, which reverted to the original value upon returning to 235°C, indicating robust thermal stability.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The (′)-carbide catalyst exhibited a CO₂ selectivity as low as 5% under the standard FT conditions described above.
- **Observations and Trends:** This low CO₂ selectivity is attributed to the absence of iron oxide phases, resulting in minimal primary CO₂ formation. The oxygen from CO dissociation is removed almost exclusively as H₂O, enhancing carbon efficiency and reducing energy requirements for downstream processing.

#### 6.2.3 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The catalyst maintained high and stable C₃⁺ selectivity, with product distributions favoring long-chain hydrocarbons under all tested conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high C₃⁺ selectivity, coupled with low methane and CO₂ formation, indicates efficient chain growth and suitability for liquid fuel production.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of hydrocarbon chain propagation versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution. Values closer to 1 indicate a higher likelihood of long-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Data:** The (′)-carbide catalyst exhibited high α values, consistent with the observed product distribution and high C₃⁺ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The elevated α factor reflects the catalyst’s propensity for producing longer-chain hydrocarbons, a key advantage for synthetic fuel applications.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring the change in CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 150 hours of continuous operation, the (′)-carbide catalyst showed negligible deactivation, with CO conversion remaining at ~15%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst’s phase composition and performance were fully reversible after thermal cycling, confirming exceptional operational stability under industrially relevant FT conditions.
