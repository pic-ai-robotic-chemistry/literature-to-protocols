## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis for the 20 wt.% Co/c‑Al₂O₃ system is accomplished using a slurry impregnation method. This technique offers the advantage of uniform cobalt distribution on the commercial alumina support and is scalable for industrial applications; however, care must be taken to avoid uneven metal dispersion during the drying phase.

#### Catalyst Preparation via Slurry Impregnation

1. • Precursor Mixing  
   - Reagents: Aqueous solution of Co nitrate (Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) is prepared to achieve a target of 20 wt.% cobalt.  
   - Equipment: Mixing is performed using standard laboratory reactors with precise stirring to ensure homogeneous contact with the c‑Al₂O₃ support.

2. • (Precipitation–Deposition)  
   - Not applicable: The procedure does not report a precipitation–deposition step.

3. • Drying  
   - Initial drying is carried out in a rotary evaporator (Buchi R-205 with a temperature-controlled bath) at 80 °C for 3 hours under reduced pressure (80 mbar).  
   - Observations: During drying, a change in color is noted as the solvent is efficiently removed and nitrate precursors begin decomposing.

4. • Calcination  
   - Conducted in an electric furnace (Carbolite RWF 1200) with flowing air at 400 °C for 2 hours to transform the precursor into cobalt oxide.
   - The controlled heating rate (1 °C/min up to 400 °C) helps maintain uniform catalyst properties.

5. • Additional Post‑processing  
   - A second impregnation step introduces an aqueous boric acid (H₃BO₃) solution to achieve promoter loadings of 0.5 wt.% and 2.0 wt.% along with an additional platinum precursor.  
   - These modifications are designed to enhance the catalyst’s reducibility and improve its stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
   - Reduction is subsequently performed in flowing H₂ (50 Nml/min) at 500 °C for 12 hours to activate the cobalt phase.

### Comparative Analysis

The slurry impregnation method is straightforward and conducive to scale‑up. Its strength lies in achieving even metal dispersion, proven by the consistent performance of the catalysts. However, as noted during the experimental procedure, slight deviations in drying conditions can result in uneven dispersion, highlighting the importance of precise control over parameters such as temperature, pressure, and drying duration.

## 4. Characterization Methods

Approximately 0.2 g of the calcined catalyst is loaded into a U‑shaped cell, dried at 120 °C for 1 hour, and preheated in flowing air to 400 °C at 5 °C/min. After cooling to 25 °C, the diffraction profile is recorded.
- **Instruments and Models**: Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements involve a Cu Kα radiation source, with the Co₃O₄ peak at 36.8° used as a reference.
- **Test Parameters**: Although exact step size is not specified, the scan is performed up to 850 °C at a scanning rate of 10 °C/min.
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment is critical to determine particle size via the Scherrer equation reliably.

### 4.2 H₂ Chemisorption and Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Approximately 0.2 g of catalyst is placed in a quartz cell and dried at 120 °C for 1 hour. After preheating in air to 400 °C at 5 °C/min, the catalyst is reduced in a flow of 50 Nml/min 5% H₂/Ar, with temperature ramped to 850 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Instruments and Models**: Quantachrome Autosorb 1C.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Controlled flow rates and calibrated reduction conditions ensure accurate measurement.
- **Test Parameters**: Calibration assumes a H:Co stoichiometry of 1.
- **Key Notes**: The procedure minimizes errors by ensuring complete precursor decomposition and uniform catalyst loading.

### 4.3 In‑situ DRIFTS
- **Operational Description**: About 50 mg of catalyst is placed in a DRIFTS cell, dried in He at 150 °C for 1 hour, and reduced in 50 Nml/min H₂ for 2 hours at 400 °C. The reduced catalyst is subsequently exposed to a 50 Nml/min flow of 2% CO/Ar at room temperature.
- **Instruments and Models**: Perkin-Elmer FTIR 2000.
- **Test Parameters**: Infrared spectra are collected with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.
- **Key Notes**: This method elucidates CO adsorption behavior and surface site characteristics.

### 4.4 X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: Post reduction (heating to 120 °C under Ar followed by 500 °C under H₂), samples are transferred in an inert atmosphere to avoid air exposure.
- **Instruments and Models**: Thermo ESCA-LAB 250.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Analysis performed with a 20-eV pass energy, 0.1-eV step, and 0.1-s dwell time, with the Al 2p peak (74.3 eV) used for calibration.
- **Key Notes**: Enables identification of boron species and carbon deposits with precision.

### 4.5 Temperature‑Programmed Hydrogenation (TPH)
- **Operational Description**: Approximately 20 mg of catalyst is loaded into a quartz tube and pretreated at 200 °C under 50 Nml/min Ar for 1 hour. After cooling, a flow of 50 Nml/min H₂ is introduced while temperature is increased to 600 °C at 5 °C/min; the CH₄ signal is monitored.
- **Instruments and Models**: Hiden HPR 20 mass spectrometer.
- **Key Notes**: TPH is crucial for assessing resilient carbon deposits on the catalyst surface.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction is achieved through a high-temperature hydrogen reduction step designed to convert cobalt oxide precursors to their active metallic form. This activation is critical for ensuring optimum cobalt reducibility and effective formation of the active phase prior to reaction testing.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential to drive the complete reduction of cobalt oxides, enabling the formation of metallic cobalt and controlling surface modifications such as limited cobalt boride formation when boron is present.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 500 °C.
- **Observations:** Maintaining 500 °C ensures a high reduction efficiency. The process effectively converts the Co₃O₄ precursor to metallic cobalt with minimal evidence of sintering, thus preserving catalyst dispersion.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The hydrogen atmosphere is employed during activation to facilitate a clean and complete reduction process by preventing undesired oxidation and partial carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A continuous flow of hydrogen is applied at a rate of 50 Nml/min.
- **Observations:** This pure H₂ environment ensures efficient cobalt reduction even in the presence of promoters. The steady hydrogen flow supports high metal dispersion while any minor changes in hydrogen uptake with boron doping do not compromise the activation outcome.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step ensures sufficient contact time between the catalyst and the reducing gas, effecting complete transformation to the active metallic phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Catalysts are typically subjected to a reduction time of up to 12 hours during in-reactor activation, although ex situ treatments have also been performed for 2 hours at 500 °C.
- **Observations:** Extended reduction times (up to 12 hours) have been correlated with complete reduction and robust catalytic performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The prolonged treatment ensures that even catalysts with modest boron promotion achieve the desired active state without inducing significant sintering.

Overall, the controlled activation conditions—specifically the use of 500 °C, a 50 Nml/min hydrogen flow, and an appropriately extended reduction time—are critical for preparing cobalt-based catalysts that exhibit high initial activity and sustained performance under Fischer–Tropsch operating conditions.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluations were conducted under carefully controlled reaction conditions to ensure reproducibility and accurate assessment of catalyst performance over long-term testing. The following subsections detail these reaction parameters as extracted directly from the article.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature, a critical parameter for syngas activation and hydrocarbon chain growth, was maintained at 240 °C throughout the catalytic tests. This temperature was chosen to facilitate effective CO conversion while slightly enhancing the deactivation rate, thereby allowing clear observation of catalyst stability and promoter effects. For instance, while the unpromoted catalyst displayed a decrease in CO conversion from 96% to 67% over 200 hours, the boron-promoted variant preserved a higher conversion rate (88%) under these conditions.

Operating at a system pressure of 20 bar in a fixed-bed micro-reactor, the elevated pressure ensured an increased concentration of synthesis gas, thereby enhancing the reaction rate and steering product selectivity toward long-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺ products). The controlled 20 bar pressure setting is instrumental in achieving the desired product distribution and consistent reactor performance during the prolonged 200-hour testing regimen.

An H₂/CO ratio of 2 was maintained for all catalytic evaluations. This ratio is pivotal in balancing the hydrocarbon product distribution and overall CO conversion. Notably, the article reports that the selected ratio leads to a methane selectivity of approximately 23%, while favoring the formation of higher hydrocarbons. ## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Particle Size (XRD)
- **Description:** The crystal structure and average particle size of cobalt phases were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer. The Co₃O₄ peak at 36.8° was used for phase identification, and Scherrer’s equation was applied to estimate crystallite size. The reduced Co particle size was calculated from the Co₃O₄ size using the ratio of molar volumes: d(Co) = 0.75 d(Co₃O₄).
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirmed the presence of Co₃O₄ in calcined samples and metallic Co after reduction. The introduction of boron at both 0.5 wt.% and 2.0 wt.% did not significantly alter the average Co particle size compared to the unpromoted catalyst.
- **Observations:** The stability of the Co particle size across different boron loadings suggests that boron promotion does not induce sintering or significant restructuring of the cobalt phase under the applied synthesis and reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Reducibility and Dispersion (TPR and H₂ Chemisorption)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) assessed the reducibility of cobalt species, while H₂ chemisorption quantified the dispersion and surface accessibility of metallic Co. TPR was performed by heating the calcined catalyst in 5% H₂/Ar up to 850 °C, and H₂ chemisorption was conducted at 25 °C after reduction at 500 °C.
- **Findings:** The addition of 0.5 wt.% boron had only a modest effect on cobalt reducibility and dispersion, as indicated by similar H₂ uptake and particle size to the unpromoted catalyst. In contrast, 2.0 wt.% boron significantly decreased both reducibility (higher reduction temperatures, lower H₂ uptake) and dispersion, resulting in fewer accessible metallic Co sites.
- **Observations:** The data indicate that low-level boron promotion preserves the number of active sites, while higher boron loading impedes reduction and diminishes catalytic surface area, correlating with reduced Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Composition and Chemical States (XPS)
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze surface elemental composition and oxidation states. B 1s and C 1s spectra provided insights into boron speciation and carbon deposits, respectively.
- **Findings:** For boron, B 1s peaks at 191.0 eV (boron oxide) and 188.1 eV (reduced cobalt boride, Co₂B) were observed. C 1s spectra after 200 h of reaction showed peaks at 284.5–284.6 eV (graphene overlayer) and 283.0–283.4 eV (p4g surface carbide). The absence of a Si 2s peak confirmed the exclusion of SiC from the analyzed samples.
- **Observations:** The presence of surface cobalt boride and reduced carbon deposition with 0.5 wt.% boron supports the role of boron in stabilizing the catalyst and mitigating deactivation by resilient carbon formation.

#### 6.1.4 CO Adsorption Properties (DRIFTS)
- **Description:** In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was employed to probe CO adsorption on metallic Co sites. Spectra were collected after reduction and CO exposure at room temperature.
- **Findings:** The CO stretching band near 2045 cm⁻¹, characteristic of CO adsorbed on metallic Co, exhibited only a minor shift (5 cm⁻¹) upon boron promotion. However, higher boron loading led to a marked decrease in band intensity, reflecting a lower number of available metallic Co sites.
- **Observations:** These results are consistent with TPR and H₂ chemisorption data, confirming that excessive boron reduces the population of active sites for CO adsorption.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Deposition and Reactivity (TPH)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed hydrogenation (TPH) quantified and characterized carbon species deposited after Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The evolution of CH₄ during hydrogenation distinguished between different carbon types.
- **Findings:** Catalysts promoted with 0.5 wt.% boron exhibited significantly less resilient carbon deposition compared to unpromoted and 2.0 wt.% B samples. The TPH profiles indicated that boron preferentially suppresses the formation of inert, graphitic carbon species.
- **Observations:** Reduced carbon accumulation with optimal boron loading correlates with improved catalyst stability and slower deactivation during prolonged FTS operation.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) conditions (240 °C, 20 bar, H₂/CO = 2), the unpromoted 20 wt.% Co/c‑Al₂O₃ catalyst achieved an initial CO conversion of approximately 96%. After 200 hours on stream, conversion declined to 67%. The 0.5 wt.% boron-promoted catalyst exhibited a similar initial conversion (92–96%) and maintained 88% conversion after 200 hours. In contrast, the 2.0 wt.% B-promoted catalyst showed a lower steady-state conversion of 54%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Low-level boron promotion (0.5 wt.%) preserves high initial activity and significantly improves long-term conversion stability. Excessive boron (2.0 wt.%) impairs CO conversion.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO yielding methane, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 24 h at standard conditions, CH₄ selectivity was 24% for the unpromoted catalyst and 23% for the 0.5 wt.% B-promoted catalyst. At lower space time (25 h), values were 27% (unpromoted) and 26% (0.5 wt.% B).
- **Observations and Trends:** Boron promotion at 0.5 wt.% does not significantly alter methane selectivity, indicating product distribution remains largely unaffected.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of long-chain hydrocarbon production.
- **Experimental Data:** Both unpromoted and 0.5 wt.% B-promoted catalysts exhibited C₅⁺ selectivity of 60–61%. For the 2.0 wt.% B-promoted catalyst, C₅⁺ selectivity dropped to 32%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal boron loading (0.5 wt.%) maintains high C₅⁺ selectivity, while overpromotion (2.0 wt.%) shifts selectivity toward lighter products.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth during hydrocarbon formation, with values closer to 1 favoring longer chains. It is typically derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** Both unpromoted and 0.5 wt.% B-promoted catalysts exhibited α values of 0.7 after 25 h on stream.
- **Observations and Trends:** The identical α values confirm that boron promotion at 0.5 wt.% does not compromise the catalyst’s ability to produce long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time, modeled as a first-order process:
  \[
  \frac{dX}{dt} = -k \cdot X
  \]
  where \( X \) is CO conversion and \( k \) is the deactivation rate coefficient (h⁻¹).
- **Experimental Data:** The unpromoted catalyst exhibited a deactivation rate coefficient of \( 1.7 \times 10^{-3} \) h⁻¹. The 0.5 wt.% B-promoted catalyst showed a much lower rate of \( 2.7 \times 10^{-4} \) h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** Boron promotion at 0.5 wt.% reduces the deactivation rate by more than six-fold, attributed to suppression of resilient carbon deposition, as confirmed by XPS and TPH analyses.

**Summary:**  
The 0.5 wt.% boron-promoted 20 wt.% Co/c‑Al₂O₃ catalyst demonstrates high initial CO conversion, stable long-term activity, and preserved selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons, with a substantial reduction in deactivation rate. Overpromotion with 2.0 wt.% boron, however, leads to diminished activity and selectivity, underscoring the importance of optimal promoter loading for maximizing catalyst performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
