## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Rh-based catalysts, with and without Fe promotion, utilizes an incipient wetness impregnation technique. This method ensures reproducible metal dispersion through controlled addition of precursors and subsequent thermal treatments.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation Method
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**
   - • Prepare a solution by dissolving Rh(NO₃)₃·2H₂O in distilled deionized water. When Fe promotion is desired, include Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in the same solution.
   - • The supports used are silica (Cabot Cab‑O‑Sil M‑5, pre‑washed with HNO₃ to remove impurities and improve surface properties) and titania (Degussa P‑25).
   - • The precursor solution is added dropwise to the support material with controlled kneading until the incipient wetness point is reached. This ensures that the support is uniformly moistened without any excess liquid.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition (if reported)**
   - • In cases where support precursors are employed, they are added dropwise following the initial mixing to promote deposition, with continued stirring for 1 hour.
   - • This step aids in achieving enhanced interaction between metal precursors and the support before thermal treatments.

3. **Drying**
   - • The resultant paste is dried overnight in air at 413 K. This step removes the solvent and begins the fixation of metal species onto the support.

4. **Calcination**
   - • Following drying, the catalyst is calcined in air at 723 K for 4 hours. This calcination step decomposes the metal nitrates and aids in the formation of a well-dispersed oxide phase.

5. **Post‑Treatment: Reduction**
   - • The calcined catalyst is then reduced in flowing dihydrogen (20 cm³(STP) min⁻¹) at 573 K for 2 hours. This reduction is critical for converting metal oxides to their active metallic form, ensuring the catalyst is ready for Fischer–Tropsch reaction testing.

### Comparative Analysis
- **Advantages:**  
  • Precise control over metal loadings (e.g., formulations of 2 wt% Rh with varying Fe loadings) is achieved.  
  • Uniform distribution is enhanced by the controlled dropwise addition and kneading process.  
- **Disadvantages:**  
  • Requires careful handling during impregnation to avoid uneven metal dispersion and potential agglomeration.

This detailed, stepwise procedure facilitates reproducibility by clearly defining reagent concentrations, reaction conditions, and equipment requirements as reported in the article "Fe-promotion of supported Rh catalysts for direct conversion of syngas to ethanol."

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the step-by-step reagent preparation process for the Fischer–Tropsch catalysts: Fe-promoted Rh/TiO₂ and Rh/SiO₂. The procedure is divided into weighing of solid reagents, liquid transfer, and mixing. Each step is performed with precision to ensure reproducibility and uniform catalyst formation.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe-promoted Rh/TiO₂ Catalyst
- **Rh(NO₃)₃·2H₂O**: Weighed 0.315g.
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 0.36g.

##### 3.1.1.2 Rh/SiO₂ Catalyst
- **Rh(NO₃)₃·2H₂O**: Weighed 0.129g.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe-promoted Rh/TiO₂ Catalyst
- **Distilled deionized water**: Added dropwise 7.5mL to the titania support while performing controlled kneading to ensure gradual absorption and attainment of incipient wetness.

##### 3.1.2.2 Rh/SiO₂ Catalyst
- **Distilled deionized water**: Added dropwise 6mL to the pre-washed silica support with HNO₃, facilitating uniform precursor distribution through controlled kneading.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe-promoted Rh/TiO₂ Catalyst
- **Metal nitrate solution**: Mixed via dropwise addition combined with systematic kneading to guarantee a homogeneous distribution of the Rh and Fe precursors over the titania support. This controlled approach secures reproducibility of the incipient wetness impregnation.

##### 3.1.3.2 Rh/SiO₂ Catalyst
- **Metal nitrate solution**: Mixed by dropwise addition and thorough kneading to ensure even dispersion on the silica support. The meticulous mixing technique prevents oversaturation and enhances distribution uniformity.

These procedures, incorporating precise mass measurements and controlled liquid addition with careful kneading, ensure that both catalyst formulations attain the required metal loadings and homogeneity. The dropwise addition method, fundamental to incipient wetness impregnation, coupled with controlled mixing, affords reproducible and consistently performing catalysts for the direct conversion of syngas to ethanol.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe-promoted Rh/TiO₂ Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 0.315 g Rh(NO₃)₃·2H₂O  
  • 0.36 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (for a formulation with 1 wt% Fe)  
  • Appropriate amount of distilled deionized water (typically 6 mL per batch)  
  • TiO₂ support (Degussa P-25)  
- **Conditions**: Ambient temperature stirring; use controlled kneading during solution addition to achieve incipient wetness.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve the metal nitrate salts completely in distilled deionized water under stirring until a homogeneous solution forms.  
  2. Prepare the TiO₂ support and, if not pre-treated, ensure it is ready for impregnation.  
  3. Add the nitrate solution dropwise to the support while continuously kneading to guarantee uniform adsorption, ensuring the support reaches the incipient wetness state.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution of precursors and avoid over-wetting by monitoring the mixing consistency.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification  
- **Process**:  
  1. Once the support is uniformly wetted, allow the mixture to stand briefly for precursor–support interaction.  
  2. Employ gentle centrifugation or filtration, if needed, to remove any loosely bound reagents.  
  3. Rinse lightly if any impurity is suspected, noting that for silica-based catalysts (Cabot Cab-O-Sil M-5), a prior HNO₃ pre-wash aids in purification.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry the impregnated support overnight in air at 413 K to remove all residual moisture.  
- **Calcination**:  
  • Heat the dried precursor in air at 723 K for 4 hours with a controlled ramp rate to prevent particle sintering.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain temperature stability, ensuring complete drying and controlled calcination for optimal metal-support interactions.

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  1. Reduce the calcined catalyst in flowing dihydrogen (20 cm³(STP)/min) at 573 K for 2 hours.  
- **Key Considerations**: Consistent reduction conditions are crucial for forming well-dispersed Rh–Fe nanoparticles (~2 nm), as confirmed by electron microscopy and H₂ chemisorption.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 H₂ Chemisorption

- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2020 automated adsorption system.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Prior to analysis, catalysts were reduced in flowing H₂ at 473 K for 2.5 hours, then evacuated at the same temperature for 2 hours. The adsorption measurements were conducted at 308 K using H₂ as the probe gas.  
- **Test Parameters**: Key parameters include the use of a controlled H₂ flow and a stable temperature during the test to ensure reliability of the chemisorption data.  
- **Key Notes**: Attention to uniform catalyst loading and precise temperature control is critical. Although calibration details were not provided, reliable determination of exposed sites underpins the dispersion assessment.

### 4.2 Electron Microscopy  
- **Operational Description**: Electron microscopy was performed to examine the catalyst morphology and dispersion. Combined FEI TITAN TEM imaging and EDAX Genesis EDS analysis confirmed the presence of ∼2 nm bimetallic Rh–Fe nanoparticles, elucidating structural and compositional aspects.  
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI TITAN Field Emission Transmission Electron Microscope paired with an EDAX Genesis EDS system.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were prepared by sprinkling powdered catalyst onto a copper grid with a lacy carbon support film. The microscope was operated at 200 kV, with the electron-beam focused on the central region of target particles.  
- **Test Parameters**: The beam diameter was controlled to match particle size, ensuring optimal resolution of approximately 2 nm.  
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample mounting and careful beam tuning are essential for reproducible high-resolution measurements.

### 4.3 DRIFTS Analysis

- **Instruments and Models**: Bio-Rad FTS-60A FTIR spectrometer equipped with an MCT detector and a Harrick DRIFTS cell.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: The sample, mixed with KBr, was pre-treated by heating under flowing Ar and subsequently reduced in H₂ at 573 K for 2 hours. CO was introduced at 50 cm³/min for 10 minutes, followed by purging with Ar before collecting the spectrum at a resolution of 2 cm⁻¹.  
- **Test Parameters**: Spectral acquisition covered the necessary range, with a background spectrum taken prior to sample analysis.  
- **Key Notes**: Ensuring complete removal of gas-phase CO post-exposure is vital for accurate spectral interpretation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pre-reaction treatment designed to convert the calcined Rh precursors into their active metallic form. In this work, the activation is carried out exclusively as part of the catalyst synthesis for Fischer–Tropsch performance and not as part of any characterization protocols.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Activation temperature is key for effectively reducing Rh species while avoiding excessive sintering. In the experiments, the catalyst is activated at 573 K. This temperature is carefully selected to ensure complete reduction of the Rh and to expose active sites necessary for efficient CO hydrogenation without inducing unwanted structural changes such as support overlayer formation.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time  
The duration of the activation process is equally important for ensuring uniform metal reduction over the catalyst bed. The catalyst is maintained at 573 K in flowing dihydrogen for 2 hours. This period is optimized to allow complete transformation of the metal oxide precursors to their metallic state while limiting any adverse effects like particle agglomeration, thus ensuring a high dispersion of Rh with effective Fe promotion.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas and Flow Rate  
Pure dihydrogen (H₂) is used as the activation gas. The use of H₂ serves as an effective reducing agent that rapidly removes oxygen from the catalyst precursors. In the present study, the catalyst undergoes reduction under a controlled H₂ flow of 20 cm³(STP)/min. This steady flow rate is critical in maintaining a uniform reducing environment and preventing local concentration gradients, which contributes to the formation of well-dispersed, bimetallic Rh–Fe nanoparticles.

By adhering to these precisely defined activation conditions—573 K temperature, 2 hours duration, and a H₂ flow rate of 20 cm³(STP)/min—the activation process reliably converts the supported Rh catalysts into their active metallic form, thereby setting the stage for optimal catalytic performance in direct syngas conversion to ethanol.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** Reaction temperature is crucial for activating syngas and driving the catalytic conversion in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was conducted at a temperature of 543 K in a fixed-bed micro-reactor.
- **Observations:** Operating at 543 K enhances syngas activation and catalyst activity. However, this higher temperature also tends to increase methane production, thereby reducing the selectivity towards ethanol and other oxygenates.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the concentration of syngas, impacting both conversion and product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were performed at a pressure of 20 atm.
- **Observations:** Although a pressure of 20 atm ensures sufficient syngas concentration, its influence on altering product selectivity was moderate. The fixed-bed reactor design helps maintain a uniform pressure environment, contributing to the reproducibility of the reaction outcomes.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is a key parameter that determines the balance between hydrogenation and carbon–carbon coupling reactions, critically affecting the hydrocarbon distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A H₂/CO ratio of 1:1 was applied throughout the catalytic tests.
- **Observations:** This balanced ratio provides the optimal conditions for syngas conversion. Adjustments in the H₂ concentration can enhance CO conversion but often lead to increased methane formation, thereby affecting the desired ethanol production.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The weight hourly space velocity (WHSV) is defined as the volumetric flow rate of the feed per unit mass of catalyst, which governs the contact time between reactants and the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The WHSV was maintained at 8000 cm³/(g_cat·h).
- **Observations:** This standardized flow condition ensures consistent reactant exposure to the catalyst, aiding in reliable CO conversion and product selectivity measurements. Reproducibility across experiments is enhanced by accurately controlling the flow rate through the fixed-bed reactor system.

Overall, these reaction parameters (543 K, 20 atm, 1:1 H₂/CO ratio, and 8000 cm³/(g_cat·h) WHSV) have been finely tuned to balance syngas activation with the controlled formation of ethanol and other products during the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Metal Dispersion and Surface Accessibility (H₂ Chemisorption)
- **Description:** H₂ chemisorption measurements, performed using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 at 308 K, were used to quantify the dispersion of Rh and the number of exposed active sites on the catalyst surface. This parameter is critical for understanding the availability of catalytically active Rh sites, which directly influences Fischer–Tropsch performance.
- **Findings:** The addition of Fe to Rh/TiO₂ catalysts led to a systematic decrease in H₂ chemisorption, indicating reduced Rh surface accessibility. As Fe loading increased, the exposed Rh surface area decreased, attributed to Fe covering or interacting with Rh sites. This trend was consistently observed across Fe loadings from 1 to 10 wt%.
- **Observations:** The suppression of H₂ chemisorption with increasing Fe content suggests that Fe promotion modifies the Rh surface, potentially enhancing interfacial sites while reducing the number of isolated Rh atoms.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size (TEM/EDS)
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was employed to examine catalyst morphology, particle size, and elemental distribution. These characteristics are essential for correlating structural features with catalytic behavior.
- **Findings:** TEM analysis of the 2 wt% Rh–2.5 wt% Fe/TiO₂ catalyst revealed uniformly dispersed bimetallic Rh–Fe nanoparticles with an average diameter of approximately 2 nm. EDS confirmed the presence of both Rh and Fe within individual particles, demonstrating intimate mixing at the nanoscale.
- **Observations:** The uniform particle size and homogeneous distribution across the titania support indicate effective synthesis and strong metal-support interaction, which are favorable for catalytic performance and reproducibility.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition Verification (ICP Analysis)
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis was conducted to verify the actual metal loadings of Rh and Fe in the catalysts. Accurate elemental composition ensures that observed catalytic properties can be reliably attributed to the intended formulations.
- **Findings:** ICP results confirmed that the synthesized catalysts closely matched their nominal metal loadings (e.g., 0.44% Rh for 0.5% Rh/TiO₂, 0.80% Rh for 1% Rh/TiO₂, etc.), validating the precision of the incipient wetness impregnation method.
- **Observations:** The close agreement between targeted and measured metal contents supports the reliability of subsequent catalytic and characterization data.

#### 6.1.4 CO Adsorption Properties (DRIFTS)
- **Description:** Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was used to probe CO adsorption on the catalyst surface, providing insights into the nature and stability of surface species relevant to syngas conversion.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS spectra showed that gem-dicarbonyl species (band at ~2094–2098 cm⁻¹) formed on Rh⁺ sites were more thermally stable on Fe-promoted Rh/TiO₂ catalysts, persisting at temperatures up to 448 K. In the presence of hydrogen, a band at 2039 cm⁻¹ (linear CO) was observed at 548 K, indicating stabilization of both charged and neutral Rh–CO species by Fe promotion.
- **Observations:** Enhanced thermal stability of CO adsorption on Fe-promoted catalysts suggests improved retention and activation of CO, which is critical for ethanol selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed into carbon-containing products. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{\sum (n_i \cdot M_i)}{M_{\text{CO}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( n_i \) is the number of carbon atoms in product \( i \), \( M_i \) is the mole percent of product \( i \), and \( M_{\text{CO}} \) is the mole percent of CO in the feed.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (543 K, 20 atm, H₂:CO = 1:1, WHSV = 8000 cm³ g⁻¹_cat h⁻¹), 2 wt% Rh/TiO₂ exhibited higher CO conversion than Rh/SiO₂. Fe promotion (1–5 wt%) on Rh/TiO₂ further increased CO conversion, with 2.5 wt% Fe yielding optimal results. However, at 10 wt% Fe, CO conversion decreased.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion is maximized at moderate Fe loadings (2.5–5 wt%) and with titania support. Excessive Fe (>5 wt%) suppresses conversion, likely due to over-coverage of Rh active sites.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ was not detected as a product under any tested conditions, indicating negligible water–gas shift activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The absence of CO₂ across all catalysts and conditions demonstrates high selectivity for hydrocarbon and oxygenate formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the proportion of converted CO yielding methane, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{n_{\text{CH}_4} \cdot M_{\text{CH}_4}}{\sum (n_j \cdot M_j)} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane was the dominant product for Rh/SiO₂, with selectivity decreasing upon Fe promotion and when using TiO₂ support. For 2 wt% Rh/TiO₂ with 5 wt% Fe, methane selectivity was significantly reduced compared to unpromoted catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe addition and titania support both suppress methane formation, favoring oxygenate production.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\sum (n_i \cdot M_i)_{\text{C}_3^+}}{\sum (n_j \cdot M_j)} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** C₃⁺ hydrocarbon selectivity was low for all catalysts, consistent with the product distribution dominated by methane and C₂ oxygenates.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low C₃⁺ selectivity reflects limited chain growth, characteristic of supported Rh catalysts under these conditions.

#### 6.2.5 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF measures the number of CO molecules converted per surface Rh atom per second, providing insight into intrinsic catalytic activity.
- **Experimental Data:** For 2 wt% Rh/TiO₂, TOF was 0.02 s⁻¹. Fe promotion increased TOF to 0.0472 s⁻¹ (1 wt% Fe) and 0.0515 s⁻¹ (2.5 wt% Fe), despite a decrease in exposed Rh sites.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe enhances the activity of Rh–Fe interfacial sites, resulting in higher TOF even as overall Rh dispersion decreases.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion over time. It is typically expressed as the percentage loss of initial activity per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** Both unpromoted and Fe-promoted Rh/TiO₂ catalysts exhibited a 30–50% decrease in CO conversion within the first 30 hours of operation, after which activity stabilized. Product selectivities remained largely unchanged during deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The initial deactivation is attributed to surface restructuring or site blocking, but the catalysts maintain stable selectivity profiles over extended operation.

#### 6.2.7 Ethanol Selectivity
- **Description:** Ethanol selectivity is the fraction of converted CO forming ethanol, calculated analogously to other selectivities.
- **Experimental Data:** Ethanol selectivity increased from 13.4% (2 wt% Rh/TiO₂) to 31.0% (2.5 wt% Fe) and peaked at 37.2% (5 wt% Fe). On silica, maximum ethanol selectivity was 22% (1 wt% Fe).
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate Fe promotion (2.5–5 wt%) on titania support yields the highest ethanol selectivity, with further Fe addition reducing overall activity.

---
**Summary:**  
Catalyst performance is strongly influenced by support type and Fe promotion. Titania-supported Rh catalysts, especially with moderate Fe loading, exhibit superior CO conversion, high ethanol selectivity, suppressed methane formation, and stable operation after initial deactivation. CO₂ formation is negligible, and chain growth beyond C₂ is limited, reflecting the unique product distribution of these systems.
