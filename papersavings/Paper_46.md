## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the step‐by‐step synthesis routes employed for Fe‐based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts. Each method follows a systematic sequence: precursor mixing, an intervening Precipitation–Deposition step (when applicable), drying, calcination, and any additional post‑treatments to ensure reproducibility and phase control.

#### • Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles via Co‑precipitation
1. Precursor Mixing:
   - Dissolve 5.0 g of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in 20.0 mL of distilled water.
2. Drying:
   - Dry the solution at 60°C for 6 hours and subsequently at 120°C overnight to isolate a reddish brown precursor.
3. Calcination:
   - Heat under air flow with a ramp rate of 5°C·min⁻¹ to 300°C and maintain for 5 hours.

#### • Polymeric Route for Carbonized Ash Formation
1. Precursor Mixing:
   - Mix iron(II) acetate with gelatin acting as a biopolymer matrix.
2. (Precipitation–Deposition is inherently integrated during film formation.)
3. Calcination:
   - Calcine the polymeric film under a nitrogen atmosphere at a controlled ramp rate of 2°C/min to 650°C; the treatment yields a carbonized ash with desired properties.

#### • Fe₅C₂ Nanoparticles via Thermal Decomposition
1. Precursor Mixing:
   - Combine 14.5 g of octadecylamine with 0.113 g of CTAB and degas the mixture under argon.
2. Precipitation–Deposition:
   - Although not a distinct addition, the dropwise injection of 0.5 mL Fe(CO)₅ is performed after achieving the reaction medium.
3. Thermal Treatment:
   - Heat to 120°C prior to injection, then increase to 180°C for 10 minutes, followed by further heating to 350°C for an additional 10 minutes.
4. Post‑processing:
   - Wash the product with ethanol and hexane to remove excess reagents.

#### • Fe⁰ Nanoparticles under Reducing Conditions
1. Precursor Mixing:
   - Dissolve 4.0 g of FeCl₃·6H₂O in 20.0 mL of ethanol, then add 5.0 g of NaOH and 10.0 mL of N₂H₄·H₂O.
2. Reactor Setup:
   - Transfer the mixture into an 80 mL stainless steel autoclave.
3. Reaction:
   - Seal and heat at 80°C for 10 hours under reducing conditions before cooling to ambient temperature.

A comparative analysis of these methods reveals trade-offs: while the Fe₂O₃ route is straightforward and cost‑effective, the polymeric and thermal decomposition methods offer unique particle morphologies and phase distribution. Equipment such as drying ovens, autoclaves, and controlled-atmosphere furnaces are critical to ensuring reproducibility and scalability of these processes.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [Fe₂O₃-Based Catalyst]
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 5.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O dissolved in 20.0 mL distilled water.
- **Conditions**: Vigorous stirring at room temperature until full dissolution; solution is then cast into Petri dishes.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the specified Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in distilled water, ensuring complete and homogeneous mixing.
  - Pour the solution into Petri dishes and dry at 50°C in air.
  - Continue drying at 60°C for 6 hours followed by drying at 120°C overnight.  
- **Key Steps**: Achieve uniform drying to prevent phase inconsistencies before calcination.

##### Step 2: Drying & Calcination
- **Conditions**: Calcination at a ramp rate of 2°C/min up to 300°C; maintain at 300°C for 5 hours under air flow.
- **Process**:
  - Transfer the dried precursor into an oven.
  - Heat gradually to ensure uniform thermal treatment, forming nanosized Fe₂O₃.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor the temperature closely to control particle crystallinity.

#### Catalyst Preparation [Polymeric Route for Iron Carbide]
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation and Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: 1.0 g Iron(II) acetate, 6 mL Iron(III) acetate solution (prepared by adding a few drops of H₂O₂), and 2 g gelatin dissolved in deionized water at 90°C.
- **Conditions**: Vigorous stirring during mixing; dropwise addition of the Iron(III) acetate solution into the gelatin solution to control pH.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve Iron(II) acetate in deionized water.
  - Prepare the gelatin solution by heating to 90°C until clear.
  - Add the Iron(III) acetate solution dropwise under vigorous stirring to ensure proper mixing and pH adjustment.
  - Cast the resultant viscous solution into Petri dishes.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain constant mixing and controlled reagent addition for reproducible precursor formation.

##### Step 2: Drying & Calcination
- **Conditions**: Dry at 60°C for 6 hours; calcine with a 2°C/min ramp to 650°C under an inert (N₂) atmosphere.
- **Process**:
  - After drying, grind the film into a fine powder.
  - Calcine the powder under N₂ to yield a carbonized ash containing χ‐Fe₅C₂.
- **Key Steps**: The inert atmosphere is critical to prevent oxidation and to favor carbide formation.

#### Activation (General for All Catalysts)
- **Process**: Conduct activation by reducing the calcined materials under H₂. In an autoclave maintained at 80°C, ensure controlled gas flow for effective reduction.
- **Key Steps**: Verify that all thermal and atmospheric conditions are strictly maintained for reproducible catalyst performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Activation temperature is pivotal for transforming precursor phases into the active iron carbide and oxide species. In the described procedures, catalysts pretreated under Fischer–Tropsch conditions are activated at 340 °C. This temperature is chosen to guarantee effective phase conversion while minimizing sintering; notably, particle sizes remain above the critical 7 nm threshold, ensuring that metal dispersion is maintained without compromising catalyst integrity.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
The activation pressure governs gas–catalyst interactions during pretreatment. The experimental workflow distinguishes two sets of conditions: Series 2 catalysts undergo activation at the full Fischer–Tropsch reaction pressure of 20 bar, whereas Series 1 catalysts are initially exposed to lower pressures with controlled H₂/CO ratios before transitioning to standard operating conditions. This variation in pressure during pre-reaction treatment is designed to influence the phase distributions, resulting in distinct core–shell structures that directly affect catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time  
The activation duration is critical for achieving complete conversion of the iron precursors into the desired active phases. A 60-hour activation period is employed as part of the catalyst pretreatment. This prolonged treatment ensures that the transformation processes are uniform, thereby promoting reproducibility in both structure and activity during subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Reactor and Workflow Considerations  
The Flowrence high‐throughput unit is used to carry out the activation procedure, delivering precise control over temperature, pressure, and gas flow. Although no explicit values for the activation gas composition or space–time velocity are provided separately from the reaction conditions (which involve a weight hourly space velocity of 30,000 mL/(g_cat·h) during FTS testing), the activation process is carefully separated from subsequent characterization. This rigorous approach ensures that the pre‐reaction treatment parameters are clearly defined and reproducible, contributing to the overall stability and performance of the Fe‐based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction conditions were carefully controlled to ensure that the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis proceeds reproducibly and with high catalytic performance. The reaction was carried out using a Flowrence high-throughput unit equipped with 48 parallel fixed-bed microreactors, enabling multiple evaluations under identical conditions.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature, maintained at 340 °C, plays a critical role in the activation of syngas and the interconversion of iron phases. At this temperature, the formation and stability of key carbide phases, such as χ‐Fe₅C₂, are enhanced, which in turn influence the catalyst’s performance. The high temperature ensures that the catalyst remains active, with a noted correlation between higher oxidation states and increased iron time yield (FTY).

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Operated at a pressure of 20 bar, the system benefits from an increased concentration of syngas, which favors C–C coupling reactions essential for hydrocarbon chain growth. This pressure setting is integral to achieving the appropriate chemical environment where iron-based catalysts can effectively convert syngas into higher-carbon products. The controlled pressure also supports stable catalyst performance by minimizing fluctuations that could lead to deactivation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
An effective H₂/CO ratio of 1 is used during the experiments, derived from a syngas composition in which hydrogen and carbon monoxide are present in equal parts. This balanced ratio is key for managing the trade-off between methane formation and olefin production, ensuring that the catalytic process aligns with the targeted product distribution.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space–Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (WHSV) is set to 30,000 mL/(gcat·h). This parameter is critical as it defines the exposure time of the reactants to the catalyst. Utilizing a WHSV of 30,000 mL/(gcat·h) helps optimize both the conversion rate of CO and the overall stability of the catalyst during extended reaction periods.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were elucidated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). These techniques are critical for identifying active phases and understanding phase transformations under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirm that catalysts derived from different iron precursors (nanosized Fe₂O₃, χ‐Fe₅C₂, θ‐Fe₃C, and Fe⁰) evolve into mixed-phase materials after pretreatment and reaction. Under Condition 1, all samples display the characteristic diffraction pattern of χ‐Fe₅C₂, indicating successful carburization. HRTEM reveals lattice fringes with d-spacings of 2.0 Å (Fe₅C₂ (510) plane) and 2.6 Å (Fe₃O₄ (311) plane), confirming the coexistence of iron carbides and oxides.
- **Observations:** The formation of core–shell structures is observed, with the spatial arrangement of Fe₃O₄ and Fe₅C₂ dependent on the precursor. The degree of crystallinity and phase distribution is strongly influenced by synthesis and pretreatment conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Oxidation State
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) analyses were employed to determine the surface and bulk oxidation states of iron, as well as the distribution of Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺, and FeCₓ species. These parameters are essential for correlating electronic structure with catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XPS spectra show Fe 2p₃/₂ peaks at ~711.3 eV (Fe³⁺), ~709.8 eV (Fe²⁺), and ~707.0 eV (FeCₓ). XANES L₃-edge features are observed at 709.3 eV (Fe³⁺), 707.9 eV (Fe²⁺), and 706.8 eV (Fe⁰/FexCy). Linear combination fitting quantifies the relative amounts of these species. The average surface iron oxidation state remains within +2.40 to +2.70 across samples, while the bulk oxidation state varies with pretreatment and correlates linearly with iron time yield (FTY).
- **Observations:** The presence of FeCₓ is indicative of effective carburization, and the bulk oxidation state is a key descriptor for catalytic activity, surpassing the influence of surface oxidation state.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphological features and particle size distributions were assessed using TEM, HRTEM, and STEM–EELS. These analyses provide insight into the physical structure and phase distribution at the nanoscale.
- **Findings:** All catalysts exhibit core–shell-like morphologies, with the arrangement of Fe₃O₄ and iron carbides varying by precursor. For example, in A-1, Fe₃O₄ is encased in a carbide shell, while in C-1, Fe₃O₄ is predominantly in the shell. Particle sizes, estimated by the Scherrer equation and TEM, range as follows (Condition 1, TEM): A = 22.2±3.5 nm, B = 19.0±8.5 nm, C = 25.0±7.6 nm, D = 24.2±5.5 nm.
- **Observations:** The core–shell architecture and particle size are strongly influenced by the synthesis route and pretreatment, impacting phase accessibility and catalytic behavior.

#### 6.1.4 Electronic Properties and Surface Adsorption
- **Description:** FTIR spectroscopy of CO adsorption was used to probe the electronic environment of surface Fe sites, particularly the d-band center and π-back-donation capability.
- **Findings:** FTIR spectra reveal a red-shift in the CO stretching frequency, attributed to π-back-donation from Fe to CO. Higher iron oxidation states result in more electron-deficient Fe centers, leading to weakened π-back-donation and decreased Fe–CO binding strength.
- **Observations:** These electronic effects are directly linked to catalytic activity, with higher oxidation states correlating with increased FTY, underscoring the importance of electronic structure in catalyst design.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Distribution and Core–Shell Confirmation
- **Description:** STEM–EELS mapping was conducted to visualize elemental distribution and confirm the presence of core–shell structures.
- **Findings:** EELS profiles demonstrate the coexistence of iron oxides and carbides, as well as sp² hybridized carbide species and amorphous carbon. The spatial separation of phases and the presence of interlayers are evident, with the arrangement dependent on the initial precursor.
- **Observations:** The confirmed core–shell structures and phase distributions are critical for understanding the relationship between catalyst architecture and performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet molar flow rates of CO, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under FTS conditions (340 °C, 20 bar, WHSV 30,000 mL/(g·h)), the CO conversion values for the Fe-based catalyst series were:
  - A-1: 23.9%
  - B-1: 66.5%
  - C-1: 32.1%
  - D-1: 8.1%
  - A-2: 44.1%
  - B-2: 24.3%
  - C-2: 49.2%
  - D-2: 48.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest CO conversion was observed for B-1 (66.5%), while D-1 exhibited the lowest (8.1%). Catalysts pretreated under FTS conditions (series 2) generally showed increased conversion, except for B-2, which decreased relative to B-1. These trends correlate with the bulk iron oxidation state and phase composition, with higher oxidation states and crystalline carbide phases favoring higher activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity varied across catalysts, with values ranging from 5.3% (D-1) to 36.6% (D-2).
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher CO₂ selectivity was generally associated with catalysts possessing higher iron oxidation states and those subjected to direct FTS pretreatment (series 2). This suggests that both the chemical environment and pretreatment history of the Fe species significantly influence CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity increased with the Fe–C coordination number, particularly in catalysts dominated by χ-Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations and Trends:** Synthesis and pretreatment conditions that promoted the formation of crystalline χ-Fe₅C₂ led to higher CH₄ selectivity, indicating enhanced C–H activation and hydrogenation ability.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** C₃⁺ selectivity was closely linked to the catalyst’s phase composition and bulk properties.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts with higher bulk iron oxidation states and crystalline carbide phases favored greater C₃⁺ hydrocarbon formation, demonstrating that phase engineering can tune hydrocarbon distribution.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of hydrocarbon chain propagation versus termination, with values closer to 1 indicating a preference for longer-chain products.
- **Experimental Data:** While specific α values were not numerically detailed, the study established that higher α factors correlated with catalysts exhibiting higher iron oxidation states and favorable carbide phases.
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor increased with the presence of electron-rich iron sites and crystalline carbides, supporting the formation of longer hydrocarbon chains.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability was assessed by monitoring CO conversion and product selectivity over time, with deactivation rate typically expressed as the decline in activity per hour (\( \text{h}^{-1} \)).
- **Experimental Data:** Time-resolved measurements indicated that catalysts with a higher proportion of stable carbide phases (χ-Fe₅C₂) maintained activity more effectively, while those with more amorphous or mixed phases showed greater deactivation.
