## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis was systematically achieved via an incipient wetness impregnation method. This technique was selected for its ability to uniformly distribute the metal precursors across the silica gel support, thereby ensuring high catalytic performance. The detailed experimental procedure is as follows:

#### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**
   - The synthesis commences by dissolving cobalt and rare‑earth precursors—Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (to provide approximately 18 wt% Co) along with La(NO₃)₃·6H₂O and Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O—into deionized water. The solution volume is calibrated at 1.2 mL per gram of silica gel support. This precise liquid-to-support ratio is critical for achieving incipient wetness, thereby ensuring homogeneous distribution without over-saturation.
   - Although a “Precipitation–Deposition” step is mentioned in general protocols, it is not explicitly applied here. The focus remains on thorough impregnation under controlled conditions.

2. **Drying**
   - Post impregnation, the catalyst mixture is subjected to an overnight shaking period to facilitate uniform dispersion of metal salts on the support.
   - The wet samples are then transferred to a vacuum oven. Initial drying is executed at 75°C for 2 hours, followed by further drying at 100°C for 4 hours. These carefully controlled conditions are crucial to remove residual moisture while preventing premature agglomeration of the active components.

3. **Calcination**
   - The dried catalysts are calcined in a temperature‐controlled furnace. The temperature is increased at a ramp rate of 2°C per minute until reaching 500°C, where the samples are maintained for 6 hours. This calcination step ensures the transition of the metal nitrates to their respective oxides while stabilizing the catalyst structure.

4. **Activation**
   - Finally, the catalysts are activated by reduction under a hydrogen atmosphere. This activation is carried out at 400°C using a ramp rate of 1°C per minute and maintained for 6 hours at a space velocity of 1000 mL/g-cat/h. This controlled reduction is essential for transforming cobalt oxides into the active metal phase.

This procedural outline, with strictly defined reagent quantities, time durations, and temperature profiles, guarantees reproducibility and lays the groundwork for achieving the enhanced catalytic performance reported by the study.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Co/S.G

**Step 1: Precursor Preparation**  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 10.0 g Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O  
  • Deionized water (using ~1.2 mL/g of silica support)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Room temperature, constant stirring  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O in the required volume of water to ensure a homogeneous solution.  
  2. Use incipient wetness impregnation by adding the solution slowly to the SiO₂ support.  
  3. Shake the impregnated mixture overnight on a shaking bed to ensure uniform precursor distribution.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain the water-to-support ratio precisely to avoid excess moisture.

**Step 2: Catalyst Assembly**  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Prepared cobalt precursor  
- **Process**:  
  1. Ensure the support is fully saturated with the precursor solution before proceeding to drying.  
  2. For catalysts with promoters (e.g., Co-1La/S.G, Co-1Ce/S.G, etc.), incorporate La(NO₃)₃·6H₂O and/or Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O in quantities defined by an n(RE):n(Co) ratio of 0.1 with specified La:Ce molar ratios.  

**Step 3: Purification**  
- No distinct purification (e.g., washing or centrifugation) is required post-impregnation; the focus remains on ensuring homogeneity during impregnation.

**Step 4: Drying & Calcination**  
- **Drying Conditions**:  
  • Vacuum oven drying at 75°C for 2 hours followed by 100°C for 4 hours.  
- **Calcination Conditions**:  
  • Ramp at 2°C/min up to 500°C, hold for 6 hours in air.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Transfer impregnated material to a vacuum oven immediately after impregnation.  
  2. After drying, subject the samples to controlled calcination to achieve the desired oxide phases.

**Step 5: Activation**  
- **Conditions**:  
  • H₂ reduction at a ramp rate of 1°C/min to 400°C, held for 6 hours with a space velocity of 1000 mL/g-cat/h.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Place the calcined catalyst in the fixed-bed reactor.  
  2. Activate under flowing H₂ to reduce cobalt oxides to the metallic state.

**Notes and Caution**:  
- Ensure that each catalyst’s workflow starts with the complete synthesis of its primary precursors.  
- Monitor the drying and calcination temperatures closely to avoid catalyst structural degradation.  
- Similar protocols are applied for Co-1La/S.G, Co-1Ce/S.G, Co-1La1Ce/S.G, Co-2La1Ce/S.G, Co-1La2Ce/S.G, Co-5La1Ce/S.G, and Co-1La5Ce/S.G, with the only variation being the relative amounts of rare-earth precursors.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: The crystalline structure of the catalysts is determined by XRD analysis. Catalyst samples are first reduced at 400 °C for 6 hours in a self‑assembled quartz reactor under hydrogen, then cooled under nitrogen. A thin layer of the sample is mounted on a zero background quartz plate for analysis.
- **Instruments and Models**: Rigaku Smartlab X‑ray diffraction system.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements are conducted using Cu Kα radiation (1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA. The scan range is 10 to 90 degrees with a scanning rate of 4°/min and a chopper increment of 0.02°.
- **Test Parameters**: Particle size is estimated via the Scherrer equation, using the correlation d(Co°) = 0.75 d(Co₃O₄).
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment on a zero background plate minimizes diffraction errors and improves detection, though low crystallinity phases may be challenging to observe.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: Textural properties, including surface area, pore volume, and pore diameter, are measured by N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms.
- **Instruments and Models**: Quantachrome Autosorb‑iQ.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Catalyst samples are degassed at 200 °C under high vacuum for 4 hours (with a ramping rate of 5 °C/min) before measurement at 77 K using nitrogen.
- **Test Parameters**: The total pore volume is calculated at a relative pressure of approximately 0.99.
- **Key Notes**: The presence of rare‑earth oxides can block pore paths, potentially lowering the measured surface area and pore volume.

### 4.3 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Reduction profiles are obtained by heating catalyst samples in a quartz tube reactor. Initially, helium is used for pretreatment followed by exposure to a H₂/He gas mixture.
- **Instruments and Models**: Self‑assembled quartz reactor fitted with the Hiden HPR‑20 QIC online mass spectrometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: The sample is treated with He (20 mL/min) from room temperature to 200 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min, held for 1 hour, then cooled to 50 °C before H₂ (2 mL/min) mixed with He (20 mL/min) is introduced.
- **Test Parameters**: Real‑time gas monitoring facilitates detailed reduction profile analysis.
- **Key Notes**: Proper pretreatment removes moisture and volatiles, ensuring accurate reduction behavior assessment.

### 4.4 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)
- **Operational Description**: Catalyst morphology is examined to distinguish between Co₃O₄ clusters and promoter oxide distributions.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Quanta FEG MK2 equipped with Oxford Instruments EDS.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Although specific imaging conditions are not detailed, the SEM analysis provides average size information (e.g., Co₃O₄ clusters averaging ~1.75 µm).
- **Test Parameters**: EDS mapping is utilized for elemental distribution.
- **Key Notes**: Fine dispersion of La₂O₃ may limit its clear identification, necessitating complementary techniques for precise characterization.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation step is an essential component of the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis catalyst preparation. This process is carefully embedded within the synthesis protocol, immediately following calcination, to convert cobalt oxides into their active metallic state. The following subsections detail the specific activation parameters extracted directly from the study.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for inducing the reduction of cobalt oxides, thereby exposing the active metallic cobalt sites required for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 400 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 400 °C ensures that sufficient thermal energy is provided to reduce cobalt oxides without causing excessive sintering, thereby preserving metal dispersion.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Ramp Rate
- **Definition:** The ramp rate controls the speed at which the temperature increases during activation. A slow ramp prevents thermal shock and ensures a uniform reduction process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A ramp rate of 1 °C/min is employed.
- **Observations:** This controlled heating minimizes the risk of agglomeration and ensures a gradual, complete reduction of the catalyst’s active phase.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition used during the activation process governs the reduction kinetics. Hydrogen, being a strong reducing agent, is used to achieve complete conversion.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen is used as the activation gas.
- **Observations:** The exclusive use of hydrogen guarantees an uncontaminated environment, ensuring efficient reduction of cobalt oxides to metallic cobalt.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity represents the flow rate of the hydrogen gas relative to the catalyst mass, which is crucial for exposing every catalyst particle uniformly to the reducing environment.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A space velocity of 1000 mL/(gcat·h) is maintained during activation.
- **Observations:** This high space-time velocity ensures a constant and sufficient supply of hydrogen, facilitating uniform and effective activation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step allows for complete reduction while avoiding overexposure that might lead to unwanted sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained at 400 °C in the hydrogen atmosphere for 6 hours.
- **Observations:** A 6-hour activation period is sufficient to fully reduce cobalt oxides, setting an optimal state for subsequent catalytic testing in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This activation protocol, integrated seamlessly into the catalyst preparation process, ensures reproducibility and sets the stage for enhanced catalytic performance in subsequent reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch experiments were conducted at two specific reaction temperatures: 220 °C and 240 °C. These temperatures are critical for optimizing syngas conversion and controlling the selectivity of hydrocarbon products. At 220 °C, the presence of Ce in the composited rare-earth (CRE) promoted catalysts resulted in a reduction of C₁–C₄ hydrocarbons, while favoring an increase in the C₅⁺ fraction. When the reaction temperature was increased to 240 °C, enhanced CO conversion and higher productivity of long-chain hydrocarbons were observed. The temperature variation provides insights into catalyst performance, balancing increased activity with potential trends toward deactivation or changes in methane selectivity.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The reaction was performed under a controlled pressure of 2.0 MPa. This pressure condition plays a vital role in increasing the solubility of syngas components, which in turn enhances the formation of liquid hydrocarbons and extends the hydrocarbon chain length. Operating at this elevated pressure also supports improved reaction kinetics and facilitates a more favorable equilibrium for the production of longer-chain products.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
A H₂/CO molar ratio of 2:1 was consistently maintained during the catalytic tests. This ratio is essential as it directly influences the distribution of hydrocarbon products; a balanced ratio promotes olefin and long-chain hydrocarbon formation while suppressing excessive methane production. The established ratio ensures that the catalyst performs optimally under the given reaction conditions.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The experimental setup featured a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 800 mL/(g-cat·h). This parameter, defined as the volumetric flow of syngas relative to the catalyst mass, is crucial for determining the exposure time of reactants to the catalytic surface. A controlled GHSV allows for reproducible reaction conditions, ensuring that the observed CO conversion and product distributions can be reliably attributed to the catalyst’s performance. Additionally, the reactions were carried out in a stainless fixed-bed reactor with an inner volume of 38 mL, ensuring consistency in the reactor environment throughout the experiments.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Crystallite Size
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and average crystallite sizes of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku Smartlab instrument with Cu Kα radiation. The Scherrer equation was applied to estimate crystallite sizes, and phase identification was used to assess the effects of rare-earth promoters.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of calcined catalysts revealed Co₃O₄ as the main cobalt phase, with average crystallite sizes ranging from 12.54 nm (Co/S.G) to 14.70 nm (Co-1La2Ce/S.G). After H₂ reduction at 400 °C, Co₃O₄ peaks disappeared, and new peaks corresponding to cubic metallic Co (Co⁰) appeared at 44.42° and 76.18°, indicating nearly complete reduction. CoO peaks (36.5°, 42.5°) were also detected, suggesting partial reduction. CeO₂ peaks remained, while La₂O₃ was not detected, likely due to its high dispersion. The Co⁰ particle size was estimated as 0.75 × d(Co₃O₄), yielding values from 9.41 nm to 11.03 nm.
- **Observations:** The addition of La and Ce increased the average crystallite size of both Co₃O₄ and Co⁰. Ce enhanced the intensity of Co⁰ peaks, indicating improved reduction, while La led to weaker Co peaks, possibly due to lower crystallinity. The absence of La₂O₃ peaks suggests excellent dispersion.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were measured by N₂ adsorption–desorption (BET) using a Quantachrome Autosorb-iQ after degassing at 200 °C under high vacuum. Surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter were quantified to assess the impact of rare-earth modification.
- **Findings:** The unpromoted Co/S.G catalyst exhibited the highest surface area and pore volume. Both individual rare-earth (IRE) and composited rare-earth (CRE) promoted catalysts showed decreased surface area (e.g., from 245 m²/g for Co/S.G to 192 m²/g for Co-1La2Ce/S.G), reduced pore volume (from 0.78 cm³/g to 0.62 cm³/g), and smaller average pore diameter (from 13.2 nm to 11.5 nm). These changes are attributed to partial pore blockage and aggregation of metal oxides within the silica support.
- **Observations:** Despite lower surface area and porosity, CRE-promoted catalysts demonstrated superior Fischer–Tropsch performance, indicating that optimal textural properties, rather than maximum surface area, are critical for high activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used to examine particle morphology, cluster size, and the distribution of cobalt and rare-earth oxides.
- **Findings:** SEM images showed Co₃O₄ as large, well-dispersed clusters (~1.75 μm) on the silica surface, composed of ~13 nm crystallites. With rare-earth addition, cluster size increased slightly (up to 4.31 μm for Co-2La1Ce/S.G). CeO₂ appeared as uniformly distributed white dots (200–500 nm), while La₂O₃ was not visible, indicating high dispersion. EDS mapping confirmed homogeneous distribution of Co, La, and Ce.
- **Observations:** The fine dispersion of La₂O₃ and uniform CeO₂ distribution are associated with improved reducibility and catalytic performance. The morphological uniformity supports enhanced CO conversion and selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was performed using a self-assembled quartz reactor and an online mass spectrometer to monitor reduction profiles and assess the influence of rare-earth promoters.
- **Findings:** Pure Co₃O₄ exhibited two main reduction peaks at 347 °C and 438 °C. When supported on silica, these peaks shifted to lower temperatures. Rare-earth modification introduced a new broad peak at ~190 °C (surface species reduction) and shifted the high-temperature peak (hardly reducible Co silicate) from ~870 °C to 770–750 °C, indicating facilitated reduction.
- **Observations:** The presence of La and Ce, especially in composite form, enhances the reducibility of cobalt species, which correlates with improved catalytic activity and selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{M_{\text{CO,in}} - M_{\text{CO,out}}}{M_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( M_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( M_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the moles of CO in the inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2:1, and space velocity of 800 mL/g-cat/h, CO conversion at 220 °C for Co/S.G (unpromoted) was 65.08%. CRE-promoted catalysts exhibited significantly higher conversions: Co-2La1Ce/S.G reached 78.16%, Co-1La2Ce/S.G 76.50%, and Co-1La1Ce/S.G 81.51%. At 240 °C, CO conversion further increased, with Co-2La1Ce/S.G achieving 99.36%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing La content generally enhanced CO conversion, with optimal performance at specific La:Ce ratios (notably 2:1 and 1:2). CRE catalysts outperformed both unpromoted and individually promoted variants, demonstrating a synergistic effect.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{M_{\text{fg}} \times P_{\text{CO}_2,\text{tg}}}{M_{\text{CO}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( M_{\text{fg}} \) is the moles of feed gas, \( P_{\text{CO}_2,\text{tg}} \) is the mole ratio of CO₂ in the tail gas, and \( M_{\text{CO}} \) is the moles of converted CO.
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity for all catalysts ranged from 2.0% to 4.6%, with a slight increase at higher temperatures.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity remained low and relatively constant across all catalyst formulations, indicating efficient hydrocarbon synthesis with minimal CO oxidation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{M_{\text{fg}} \times P_{\text{CH}_4,\text{tg}}}{M_{\text{CO}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 220 °C, CH₄ selectivity for Co/S.G was 19.82%. CRE-promoted catalysts showed marked reductions: Co-1La1Ce/S.G at 13.57% and Co-1La2Ce/S.G at 11.31%. At 240 °C, CH₄ selectivity increased for all catalysts but remained lower for CRE variants.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of La and Ce, especially in combination, significantly suppressed methane formation, attributed to improved cobalt reducibility and larger particle size.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 1 - S_{\text{CH}_4} - S_{\text{C}_2} - S_{\text{C}_3} - S_{\text{C}_4}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 220 °C, C₅⁺ selectivity increased from 61.26% (Co/S.G) to 80.18% (Co-1La2Ce/S.G). At 240 °C, Co/S.G yielded 54.84%, while Co-1La2Ce/S.G achieved 76.69%.
- **Observations and Trends:** CRE catalysts, particularly with a La:Ce ratio of 1:2, maximized C₅⁺ selectivity, supporting enhanced diesel-range hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.5 Diesel Fraction (C₁₂–C₁₈) Selectivity
- **Description:** The selectivity for C₁₂–C₁₈ hydrocarbons indicates the catalyst’s ability to produce diesel-range products.
- **Experimental Data:** The highest C₁₂–C₁₈ selectivity was observed for Co-1La2Ce/S.G, reaching 26.77% at 240 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** CRE-promoted catalysts with optimal La:Ce ratios favor diesel-range hydrocarbon formation, aligning with industrial targets.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability was assessed by maintaining steady-state operation for 48 h before data collection.
- **Experimental Data:** No significant deactivation was observed during the steady-state period for any catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** The promoted catalysts demonstrated excellent stability under reaction conditions, supporting their suitability for long-term operation.

---
**Summary:** The composited rare-earth (CRE) promoted cobalt catalysts, especially with optimized La:Ce ratios, exhibited superior CO conversion, suppressed methane formation, and enhanced selectivity for long-chain and diesel-range hydrocarbons. These improvements are attributed to synergistic effects between La and Ce, which enhance cobalt reducibility and dispersion, ultimately leading to more efficient and stable Fischer–Tropsch synthesis performance.
