## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

For the preparation of the silica-supported catalyst (nominal composition 100Fe/5Cu/6K/139SiO₂), the incipient wetness impregnation method is employed. This method has been compared with precipitation techniques in the article, and its advantages include precise control over metal loading and uniform distribution on the support, although it requires multiple impregnation and drying steps.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation for Silica-Supported Catalyst

1. **Precursor Preparation**  
   • The commercial silica support (Davison Grade 952) is first sieved to a particle size range of 140–325 mesh (45–106 µm).  
   • The sieved silica is then calcined in air at 500 °C for 5 h to remove impurities and enhance surface area.

2. **Co‑impregnation Process**  
   • The pre‑calcined silica is impregnated with aqueous solutions containing ferric nitrate, copper nitrate, and potassium bicarbonate.  
   • The catalyst is synthesized by maintaining the nominal mass ratio of 100Fe/5Cu/6K, ensuring uniform distribution of Fe, Cu, and K on the support.  
   • Multiple impregnation steps are implemented—in this case, three successive steps—to build up the desired metal loading.

3. **Vacuum Drying (Post‑Impregnation Treatment)**  
   • After each impregnation step, the sample is vacuum dried at 100 °C for approximately 2 h.  
   • A final drying step, also under vacuum at 100 °C, is carried out for 12 h to ensure complete moisture removal.

4. **Calcination**  
   • Following the final drying, the catalyst is calcined in air at 300 °C for 5 h, which stabilizes the active phases and finalizes the catalyst structure.

#### Comparative Analysis and Additional Notes

• The incipient wetness impregnation method facilitates better control over active component distribution compared to the precipitation method, which is also discussed in the article for catalysts like 100Fe/3Cu/4K/16SiO₂ and 100Fe/5Cu/6K/24SiO₂.  
• For alumina-supported catalysts (e.g., 100Fe/5Cu/9K/139Al₂O₃), a similar procedure is followed but requires seven impregnation steps due to the lower pore volume of boehmite alumina.  
• Consistent numerical parameters (temperatures, durations, and drying conditions) ensure reproducibility, allowing other researchers to replicate these procedures accurately.

## 4. Characterization Methods

- **Operational Description**: The BET analysis was conducted to quantify the surface area and pore volume of the catalysts. Approximately 1–2 g of catalyst was first degassed to remove moisture and weakly bound adsorbates before nitrogen physisorption measurements.
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements were performed using a Micromeritics Digisorb 2600 system.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were degassed at 150 °C for 12 hours. Nitrogen physisorption was carried out at 77 K using both single‐point and multi‐point methods.
- **Test Parameters**: The analysis compared surface area values before and after impregnation, documenting decreases (e.g., a reduction from 308 m²/g to 252 m²/g for the silica support).
- **Key Notes**: The observable decrease in surface area is attributed to pore filling and possible pore blocking during the impregnation process, which is critical when interpreting catalyst activity.

### 4.2 Temperature-Programmed Reduction (TPR)
- **Operational Description**: TPR was used to monitor the reduction behavior of the iron catalyst. Initially, the catalyst was pretreated in a helium flow to remove physically adsorbed moisture, then subjected to a reducing atmosphere.
- **Instruments and Models**: A micro fixed-bed reactor equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) was employed.
- **Characterization Conditions**: The pretreatment involved purging with helium at 40 cm³/min while heating from room temperature to 280 °C at 20 °C/min for 30 minutes. Following pretreatment, a 5% H₂/95% N₂ atmosphere was introduced, and the sample was heated from room temperature to 800–900 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: The flow rate was maintained at 40 cm³/min, and distinct reduction peaks corresponding to Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ and Fe₃O₄ to metallic iron were observed.
- **Key Notes**: Accurate temperature control is essential for reproducibility. Calibration using a CuO standard ensured reliable quantification of reduction peaks.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation of the 100Fe/5Cu/6K/139SiO₂ catalyst is conducted as an in situ pretreatment step to generate the active phases necessary for effective Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This process is carried out in a stirred tank slurry reactor with precise control over critical parameters.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential for reducing the iron oxide precursor to its metallic state, thereby exposing the active sites required for the reaction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 280 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at this moderately elevated temperature ensures effective reduction while avoiding the risk of overheating that could lead to undesirable sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences gas–solid interactions and affects the reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is maintained at 0.8 MPa.
- **Observations:** This pressure level provides a consistent environment that promotes uniform reduction across the catalyst particles without contributing to increased agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas determines the efficiency of the reduction process. It acts as the reducing agent to convert the iron oxides.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated with either pure CO or CO diluted with helium. Reported mixing ratios include CO/He = 1/8, 1/5, and 1/10.
- **Observations:** Both pure CO and the specified CO/He mixtures facilitate the exposure of active sites, with similar reduction behavior observed under these conditions.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** This parameter, defined as the gas flow per catalyst weight, ensures adequate and uniform exposure to the reducing gas.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A gas flow of 3 L (STP) per gram of catalyst per hour is maintained.
- **Observations:** This flow rate is critical to avoid incomplete activation due to insufficient gas contact.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation is set to guarantee thorough reduction while mitigating risks of minor deactivation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation step lasts for 8 hours.
- **Observations:** An 8-hour activation ensures an approximate 80% degree of reduction without excessive exposure that might induce catalyst deactivation.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for promoting the reduction of iron oxides and forming active iron species, thereby enhancing syngas activation and overall catalyst performance.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch catalytic tests were conducted at 260 °C.
- **Observations:** At this temperature, the silica-supported catalyst exhibited effective syngas conversion with enhanced catalytic activity, as active phases were maintained during prolonged testing periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** The reaction pressure determines the partial pressures of reactant gases in the system, directly affecting syngas concentration and product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The slurry reactor tests were performed at a reaction pressure of 1.5 MPa.
- **Observations:** This pressure condition contributed to optimized syngas conversion, balancing the formation of shorter-chain hydrocarbons and longer-chain products, and ensuring reproducible catalyst performance.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO molar ratio governs the distribution of hydrocarbon products by influencing the chain growth probability during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 0.67 was maintained for the supported catalyst tests.
- **Observations:** The lower H₂/CO ratio was associated with higher methane and C₂–C₄ hydrocarbon yields. It played a crucial role in steering selectivity by reducing the chain growth probability factor, unlike the higher ratios observed for precipitated catalysts.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, presented as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) or gas space velocity (GSV), quantifies the ratio of synthesis gas flow to the catalyst mass, thereby influencing residence time.
- **Experimental Conditions:** For the silica-supported catalyst (run SB-3007), the gas space velocity was approximately 4.0 L (STP)/g_Fe·h, while the precipitated catalyst (run SB-2537) exhibited a velocity of about 3.3 L (STP)/g_Fe·h.
- **Observations:** These values indicate that higher space-space velocity conditions correlate with improved catalyst stability and controlled product distributions, ensuring reproducibility across multiple tests.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** The textural properties of the catalysts were evaluated using nitrogen physisorption at 77 K, with BET surface area and pore volume measurements performed on a Micromeritics Digisorb 2600 system. Samples were degassed at 150 °C for 12 h prior to analysis. These parameters are critical for understanding the dispersion of active phases and the accessibility of reactants during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** The pre-calcined silica support (Davison Grade 952) exhibited a BET surface area of 308 m²/g. After impregnation with iron, copper, and potassium, followed by vacuum drying and calcination, the surface area of the resulting 100Fe/5Cu/6K/139SiO₂ catalyst decreased markedly to 100 m²/g. Pore volume also decreased, attributed to pore filling and/or blocking by the impregnated metals. Precipitated catalysts retained higher surface areas (260–310 m²/g).
- **Observations:** The significant reduction in surface area and pore volume for supported catalysts suggests limited dispersion of active phases, potentially impacting catalytic activity. In contrast, higher surface area in precipitated catalysts is linked to improved reactant diffusion and higher catalytic efficiency.

#### 6.1.2 Reduction Behavior and Phase Composition
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) with 5% H₂/95% N₂ (40 cm³/min) and isothermal reduction studies (TGA/DTA) were used to assess the reducibility and phase evolution of the catalysts. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy provided direct identification of crystalline phases after reduction.
- **Findings:** TPR profiles revealed two distinct reduction peaks: the first (∼250–400 °C) corresponds to Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄, and the second, broader peak to Fe₃O₄ → metallic Fe. Precipitated catalysts exhibited sharper, more facile reduction peaks, while supported catalysts showed broader peaks and slower reduction. Isothermal reduction at 280 °C confirmed that silica-supported catalysts achieved a higher degree of reduction than alumina-supported ones. XRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy identified Fe₃O₄ and metallic Fe as the main phases after hydrogen reduction, with iron carbides (e.g., χ-Fe₅C₂) and Fe₃O₄ present after CO reduction, indicating partial carburization.
- **Observations:** The reduction behavior is strongly influenced by catalyst preparation and support. Potassium promotion was less effective on silica due to strong support interaction, affecting both reduction and selectivity. The presence of iron carbides after CO reduction confirms carburization phenomena, which are relevant for Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Support Loading
- **Description:** The effective loading of iron, copper, and potassium on the silica support was inferred from changes in textural properties post-impregnation and synthesis procedures.
- **Findings:** The marked decrease in BET surface area and pore volume after impregnation and calcination is consistent with successful incorporation of the active metals onto the support. The nominal composition of the catalyst was maintained as 100Fe/5Cu/6K/139SiO₂, with an overall iron content of approximately 33.8 wt%.
- **Observations:** The synthesis approach ensured reproducible loading and distribution of active components, as reflected in the consistent changes in physical properties and catalytic performance.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For the silica-supported 100Fe/5Cu/6K/139SiO₂ catalyst under slurry reactor conditions (260 °C, 1.5 MPa, H₂/CO = 0.67), the CO conversion was approximately 60–65%. The alumina-supported catalyst exhibited a lower CO conversion of about 35–40%, while the precipitated catalysts showed 20–40% lower activity than the silica-supported variant.
- **Observations and Trends:** The silica-supported catalyst demonstrated the highest initial activity, attributed to a higher degree of iron reduction. The alumina-supported catalyst’s lower activity is linked to inhibited iron reduction by the support.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit numerical values are not provided, the silica-supported catalyst generally exhibited higher CO₂ selectivity compared to the alumina-supported and precipitated catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increased CO₂ selectivity in supported catalysts is attributed to diminished potassium promotion on silica, which favors the water–gas shift reaction.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The silica-supported catalyst produced 6–7 mol% methane, higher than the alumina-supported and precipitated catalysts (≤5 mol%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Elevated methane selectivity for the silica-supported catalyst is linked to reduced potassium promotion effectiveness due to strong support interactions.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The silica-supported catalyst exhibited lower C₃⁺ selectivity, producing more light hydrocarbons (C₁–C₄), while the alumina-supported and precipitated catalysts favored higher C₃⁺ selectivity and greater yields of long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The trend is attributed to the effectiveness of potassium promotion, which is suppressed on silica but retained on alumina.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The Anderson–Schulz–Flory α factor indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination, with values closer to 1 favoring longer hydrocarbon chains.
- **Experimental Data:** Precipitated Fe/Cu/K/SiO₂ catalysts achieved α values of 0.92–0.95, corresponding to 80–89% C₅⁺ hydrocarbon yield at ~80% syngas conversion. The silica-supported catalyst, with higher methane selectivity, exhibited a lower α.
- **Observations and Trends:** High α values in precipitated and alumina-supported catalysts reflect strong chain growth, while the silica-supported catalyst’s lower α is consistent with its higher methane and light hydrocarbon selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The alumina-supported catalyst exhibited the highest deactivation rate, with activity dropping to about 50% of the silica-supported catalyst after 100 hours. The silica-supported catalyst maintained higher activity over the same period compared to one of the precipitated catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior stability of the silica-supported catalyst is attributed to its higher degree of reduction and support properties, though further improvements are needed for long-term operation.
