## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis was executed using a stepwise incipient wetness impregnation method that ensures precise control of metal loading and uniform dispersion on high‑surface‑area activated carbon supports (AC1: Norit D10, ~600 m²/g; AC2: Norit SAE Super, ~1200 m²/g). This method is particularly advantageous due to its simplicity, scalability, and lower equipment requirements compared to co‑precipitation techniques.

#### Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation
1. • An aqueous solution of (NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄·4H₂O is prepared at the required concentration.
2. • The activated carbon support is impregnated with the Mo precursor solution.
3. • A noticeable color change accompanies the impregnation, indicating proper absorption.
4. • The material is then dried at 110 °C for 10 hours.

#### Precipitation–Deposition
• After the Mo impregnation and drying, the Ni precursor (e.g., Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) is sequentially added via a precipitation–deposition step.  
• This step, inserted before the secondary drying, ensures that the deposited nickel is evenly distributed over the Mo-loaded support.

#### Drying
• Following the Ni precursor addition, the catalyst is dried again at 110 °C for 6 hours, ensuring that both metal precursors are well-adhered to the support.

#### Calcination
• The impregnated material is calcined at 575 °C for 4 hours under a nitrogen (N₂) atmosphere to stabilize and activate the metal phases, preventing unwanted oxidation.

#### Post‑Treatment and K Promoter Addition
• The K promoter is introduced by dry impregnation using an aqueous K₂CO₃ solution.
• The catalyst is dried at 110 °C for 6 hours and then calcined at 400 °C for 4 hours under N₂.
• Additionally, an acid pre‑treatment with 50 vol% HNO₃ at room temperature overnight is applied to AC1 and AC2 supports to remove ash and generate surface carbon–oxygen complexes, enhancing metal dispersion.

This systematic sequence, with detailed control over temperatures, durations, and reagent concentrations, ensures reproducibility and optimal catalytic performance as confirmed by subsequent ICP‑AES, XRD, BET, and TPR analyses.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the precise steps followed during the catalyst synthesis to ensure full reproducibility, with catalyst‑specific grouping under each procedural stage.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

Catalyst preparation begins by accurately weighing the solid metal precursors using an analytical balance (model details not specified). For both K–NiMo/AC1a and K–NiMo/AC2a catalysts, the target molar ratio is maintained at K/Ni/Mo = 0.05/1/1.

##### 3.1.1.1 K–NiMo/AC1a Catalyst
- **(NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄·4H₂O**: Weighed to achieve a Mo content corresponding to 21.6 wt% on the support.
- **Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed to provide 13.4 wt% of Ni as per the formulation requirements.

##### 3.1.1.2 K–NiMo/AC2a Catalyst
- **(NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄·4H₂O**: Weighed to yield a Mo loading of 21.6 wt% on the support.
- **Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed to deliver 13.4 wt% of Ni content.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

The liquid transfer step involves preparing precursor solutions by dissolving the weighed solids in deionized water, then applying the solutions via incipient wetness impregnation to ensure uniform distribution over the activated carbon supports. Additionally, a K₂CO₃ aqueous solution is employed for dry impregnation to introduce the promoter.

##### 3.1.2.1 K–NiMo/AC1a Catalyst
- **Deionized water**: Dissolved the precursors and was transferred onto the AC1 support by incipient wetness impregnation.
- **K₂CO₃ aqueous solution**: Applied by dry impregnation to achieve the desired K loading.

##### 3.1.2.2 K–NiMo/AC2a Catalyst
- **Deionized water**: Used similarly to dissolve precursors and impregnate the AC2 support.
- **K₂CO₃ aqueous solution**: Transferred by dry impregnation to incorporate the K promoter.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

After liquid transfer, the impregnated supports are mixed to form homogeneous precursor distributions, a critical step to ensure optimal metal dispersion prior to drying and calcination.

##### 3.1.3.1 K–NiMo/AC1a Catalyst
- **Precursor solution on AC1**: Mixed thoroughly under ambient conditions until uniform distribution was achieved.

##### 3.1.3.2 K–NiMo/AC2a Catalyst
- **Precursor solution on AC2**: Mixed under similar conditions to facilitate homogeneous impregnation.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: K–NiMo/AC Catalyst

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Mo precursor: (NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄·4H₂O dissolved in deionized water at the stoichiometric amount required to maintain a K/Ni/Mo molar ratio of 0.05/1/1.  
  • Ni precursor: Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O prepared similarly, with quantities based on the desired catalyst loading.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Heat the aqueous Mo solution to 70 °C under continuous stirring for 1 hour to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneous mixing.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve the calculated mass of (NH₄)₆Mo₇O₂₄·4H₂O in deionized water.  
  2. Stir at 70 °C for 1 hour.  
  3. Separately, prepare the Ni precursor by dissolving Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O following the same rigorous mixing protocol.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Precise molar calculations are essential for maintaining the constant catalyst composition.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly via Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Acid pre-treated activated carbon supports (AC1 or AC2) that have been washed with deionized water following an overnight treatment with 50 vol% HNO₃ at room temperature.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Sequential impregnation is performed; first with the Mo precursor and then with the Ni precursor.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Impregnate the support with the Mo precursor solution.  
  2. Dry at 110 °C for 6–10 hours to ensure complete solvent removal.  
  3. Carry out a subsequent impregnation with the Ni precursor solution.  
  4. Repeat the drying step at 110 °C.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Achieve uniform distribution via controlled addition and drying.

##### Step [3]: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  1. Wash the impregnated support thoroughly with deionized water to purge residual impurities.  
  2. Dry the material at 110 °C.  
  3. Calcine in a nitrogen atmosphere at 575 °C for 4 hours to convert the precursors into their active oxide forms.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Maintain controlled temperature and atmosphere to prevent metal agglomeration.

##### Step [4]: K Promoter Addition and Activation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Aqueous K₂CO₃ solution for dry impregnation.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Dry at 110 °C for 6 hours following K addition, then final calcination at 400 °C for 4 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Introduce the K promoter via dry impregnation.  
  2. Dry and calcine to achieve proper promoter dispersion and catalyst activation.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensuring the K/Ni/Mo ratio remains constant is critical for reproducible catalyst performance.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 ICP-AES  
- **Operational Description**: This technique confirms the elemental composition of the catalyst by quantifying metal loadings. It ensures that the Ni, Mo, and K ratio is maintained at the desired 1/1/0.05 molar ratio.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Plasma 400 (Perkin Elmer) spectrometer equipped with a Cetac6000AT+ ultrasonic nebulizer.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Standard ICP sample preparation is used, although explicit calibration procedures are not detailed.  
- **Test Parameters**: Basic operating parameters inherent to the Plasma 400 system are employed to achieve reliable quantification.  
- **Key Notes**: This method provides a crucial chemical composition check to support structure–performance correlations in catalyst design.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis  
- **Operational Description**: N₂ adsorption/desorption experiments are conducted to determine surface area, total pore volume, and micropore volume using t-plot analysis.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Autosorb-1MP (Quantachrome).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Experiments are performed at −196 °C. Samples are dehydrated in vacuum at 250 °C overnight, with total pore volume measured at P/P⁰ = 0.99.  
- **Test Parameters**: Detailed adsorption isotherms are recorded to ensure accurate porosity and surface area evaluation.  
- **Key Notes**: This analysis is essential to validate that higher-surface-area supports improve metal dispersion.

### 4.3 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)  
- **Operational Description**: XRD is used to identify crystalline phases and differentiate between −NiMoO₄ and α‑NiMoO₄ phases.  
- **Instruments and Models**: SIEMENS D‑500 diffractometer operating at 40 kV and 30 mA with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15405 nm).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Diffraction patterns are collected over a 2θ range of 5–75° with a step size of 0.02° and a counting time of 2 seconds per step.  
- **Test Parameters**: Proper sample mounting and prior calcination/acid treatment are employed to optimize phase detection.  
- **Key Notes**: Consistent instrument settings ensure reproducibility and accurate phase identification.

### 4.4 H₂‑Temperature Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)  
- **Operational Description**: This method evaluates the reduction behavior of the catalyst, distinguishing reduction peaks corresponding to different species such as −NiMoO₄ and Mo.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements are performed in a quartz fixed-bed reactor with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: A 200 mg catalyst sample is pretreated in flowing He at 350 °C for 0.5 hours, then cooled to room temperature. The temperature is ramped from room temperature to 800 °C at 10 °C/min under a 5% H₂/He gas flow (50 cm³/min).  
- **Test Parameters**: Controlled heating rate and gas composition ensure clear resolution of reduction events.  
- **Key Notes**: Pre-treatment conditions are critical for stabilizing the catalyst surface to achieve reproducible reduction profiles.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch reactions is integrated as an in situ pre-treatment step focused purely on optimizing the catalyst surface for subsequent CO hydrogenation. This activation process exclusively targets catalyst synthesis and pre-reaction stabilization, deliberately excluding any procedures related to characterization methods such as TPR or XPS.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical in reducing metal oxide precursors and ensuring that active Ni–Mo sites are efficiently exposed without promoting sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 350 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at this moderate temperature effectively stabilizes the catalyst surface by reducing the metal oxides, leading to enhanced dispersion of the Ni and Mo species.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences gas-solid interactions, affecting reduction kinetics and overall catalyst performance.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The pre-treatment is conducted at atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** This pressure condition supports sufficient hydrogen-catalyst contact while maintaining optimal reduction without catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The choice of activation gas is pivotal since a strongly reducing environment is necessary for the efficient reduction of precursor species to active metallic states.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen is used, providing a reductive atmosphere that ensures complete reduction of metal species.
- **Observations:** The use of pure hydrogen has been confirmed to maximize the formation of stable Ni–O–Mo sites, thereby improving catalyst performance during CO hydrogenation.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation ensures that the reduction process is complete and the catalyst surface is properly stabilized.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst undergoes a 12-hour pre-treatment under the specified conditions.
- **Observations:** This extended activation time allows full reduction and stabilization of metal species, leading to enhanced CO conversion and favorable selectivity towards higher alcohols during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

This well-defined activation protocol, performed in a fixed bed reactor with proper dilution using SiC to ensure isothermal conditions, has been proven essential for achieving consistent catalyst performance across different support types (e.g., K–NiMo/AC1a and K–NiMo/AC2a).

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction conditions in this evaluation of Fischer-Tropsch synthesis for higher alcohol production were meticulously defined to ensure reproducibility and clear performance comparisons. The following subsections detail the specific parameters extracted from the study.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The operational temperature controls the activation of syngas and the reaction kinetics that govern product distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst tests were conducted within a temperature range of 250–280 °C.
- **Observations:** Within this range, optimal syngas activation was achieved. Notably, the upper limit near 280 °C was associated with a high CO conversion and a significant space-time yield of oxygenates, highlighting improved catalyst performance.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reactor pressure influences the concentration of the reactant gases and thereby affects both conversion and selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A constant reaction pressure of 60 bar was maintained during tests using a stainless steel fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure contributed to increased reactant density, supporting a robust interaction with the catalyst surface and favoring the formation of higher alcohols over undesired side products.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The hydrogen to carbon monoxide ratio is crucial in controlling the extent of hydrogenation and thus the product distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 2 was used throughout the experiments.
- **Observations:** This ratio facilitated optimal hydrogen availability that encouraged selective formation of oxygenates while minimizing excessive hydrogenation into methane.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The weight-to-flow (W/F) ratio provides a measure of the contact time between the reactants and the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A W/F of 0.63 g·s/cm³ was employed, ensuring a controlled residence time.
- **Observations:** This condition helped balance the conversion rate and catalyst stability, contributing to consistent performance over prolonged reaction intervals.

#### 5.2.5 Catalyst Activation
- **Definition:** Pre-treatment of the catalyst is essential to render the active sites fully accessible and to ensure stability during the reaction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Prior to testing, the catalyst underwent an in-situ activation under pure hydrogen at 350 °C for 12 hours.
- **Observations:** This reductive pre-treatment was critical for stabilizing the catalyst surface, thereby improving activity and mitigating deactivation during the CO hydrogenation reaction.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural order of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases relevant to Fischer–Tropsch-type reactions.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis revealed that the unsupported K–NiMo catalyst exhibits only the well-crystallized α-NiMoO₄ phase, characterized by sharp, intense peaks. In contrast, when supported on activated carbon (AC1 or AC2), the bimetallic catalysts display predominantly the β-NiMoO₄ phase, along with minor amounts of α-NiMoO₄, Ni, and MoO₂. The supported catalysts are notably more amorphous, as indicated by broad, low-intensity peaks, reflecting smaller crystallite sizes and higher disorder. The calcined, acid-treated AC1 support itself is largely amorphous, with only a minor peak at 26.6° (graphitic carbon).
- **Observations:** The stabilization of the β-NiMoO₄ phase on the support, as opposed to the α-phase in unsupported catalysts, is associated with enhanced catalytic activity for higher alcohol synthesis. The increased amorphous character and reduced crystallite size on high-surface-area supports (especially AC2) suggest improved dispersion of active phases, which is beneficial for Fischer–Tropsch performance.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** BET surface area and porosity measurements assess the textural properties of the supports and catalysts, which influence metal dispersion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** The AC1 support has a surface area of 600 m²/g, while AC2 offers a higher surface area of 1150–1200 m²/g. After metal impregnation, a significant reduction in surface area and pore volume is observed (up to 50% for K–Mo/AC1a and K–NiMo/AC1a), indicating partial pore blockage by metal oxides. The higher surface area and pore volume of AC2 facilitate superior dispersion of the active metal phases.
- **Observations:** Enhanced dispersion on AC2 is corroborated by less crystalline XRD patterns and is linked to higher catalytic activity. The textural properties of the support, particularly after acid pre-treatment, are critical for maximizing the exposure and uniformity of active sites.

#### 6.1.3 Chemical Composition and Metal Dispersion
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) was used to verify the chemical composition and metal loadings, ensuring the intended stoichiometry and uniformity.
- **Findings:** ICP-AES confirmed that the catalysts achieved the target equimolar Ni and Mo composition, with a K/Ni/Mo molar ratio of 0.05/1/1 across all samples. Acid pre-treatment effectively reduced ash content, as evidenced by TGA and ICP-AES, and promoted the formation of surface carbon–oxygen complexes, enhancing metal dispersion.
- **Observations:** The accurate metal loading and improved dispersion, particularly on acid-treated supports, are essential for the observed synergistic effects and high catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility and Metal–Support Interaction
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) profiles provide insight into the reducibility of metal oxides and the strength of metal–support interactions, which are crucial for catalyst activation.
- **Findings:** Supported bimetallic K–NiMo catalysts (β-NiMoO₄ phase) exhibit a main reduction peak at approximately 440 °C, attributed to the reduction of β-NiMoO₄, and a supplementary peak at 610 °C for partial Mo(IV) reduction. In contrast, the unsupported K–NiMo catalyst (α-NiMoO₄) shows two main peaks at 550 °C and ~650 °C, indicating more difficult reduction of the well-crystallized phase.
- **Observations:** The lower reduction temperature and broader peaks for supported catalysts reflect easier and more gradual reduction, consistent with better dispersion and smaller crystallite size. This enhanced reducibility is directly correlated with improved catalytic activity in CO hydrogenation.

#### 6.1.5 Support Acidity and Surface Functionalization
- **Description:** The acidity and surface chemistry of the supports, modified by acid pre-treatment, were characterized by ammonia-TPD and elemental analysis.
- **Findings:** Acid pre-treatment with HNO₃ removes inorganic ash and generates surface carbon–oxygen complexes (e.g., COOH, OH groups), increasing the concentration of weak acid sites. This modification does not significantly alter the overall texture but enhances the formation of thermally stable acidic functionalities.
- **Observations:** Increased surface acidity and functionalization promote better metal anchoring and dispersion, which are reflected in higher CO conversion and selectivity toward higher alcohols.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is typically calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (280 °C, 60 bar, H₂/CO = 2, W/F = 0.63 g·s/cm³), the K–NiMo/AC2a catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 25%. The K–NiMo/AC1a catalyst, supported on a lower surface area carbon, exhibited a 45% lower conversion under identical conditions. Monometallic K–Ni and K–Mo catalysts showed negligible CO conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced CO conversion is directly correlated with higher support surface area and acid pre-treatment, which improve metal dispersion. The bimetallic NiMo system demonstrates a pronounced synergistic effect, as monometallic analogues are largely inactive.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, a key indicator of the water–gas shift side reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{C in CO}_2}{\text{Total C converted}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ is consistently reported as a major product for bimetallic K–NiMo catalysts. While explicit numerical values are not tabulated, the data indicate that CO₂ selectivity is significant for these catalysts, whereas monometallic catalysts produce negligible CO₂ due to their low activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The formation of CO₂ is more pronounced in active, well-dispersed bimetallic systems, reflecting the concurrent water–gas shift reaction.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{C in CH}_4}{\text{Total C converted}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane is included within the total hydrocarbon (HC) fraction. The bimetallic K–NiMo catalysts generate more hydrocarbons, including CH₄, than monometallic counterparts, though specific CH₄ selectivity values are not isolated.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher hydrocarbon (including CH₄) formation is associated with increased catalyst activity and improved metal dispersion.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, represents the probability of chain growth during product formation. It is calculated from the distribution of alcohols with varying carbon numbers, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher likelihood of forming longer-chain products.
- **Experimental Data:** For K–NiMo/AC catalysts, α values are reported in the range of 0.40–0.45.
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor increases with improved metal dispersion, particularly on high-surface-area, acid-treated supports, favoring the formation of higher alcohols.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time, expressed as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\%\,\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The K–NiMo/AC catalysts exhibit a deactivation rate of approximately 0.2% per hour over a 24-hour period.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low deactivation rate reflects the stabilizing effect of in-situ hydrogen pre-treatment and the beneficial role of acid-treated, high-surface-area supports in maintaining catalyst activity.

#### 6.2.6 Space Time Yield to Oxygenates
- **Description:** Space time yield (STY) to oxygenates measures the mass of oxygenated products produced per gram of catalyst per hour:
  \[
  \text{STY}_{\text{oxygenates}} = \frac{\text{mg oxygenates}}{\text{g}_{\text{cat}} \cdot \text{h}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The K–NiMo/AC2a catalyst achieves an STY to oxygenates of 141.5 mg/g_cat/h at 280 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** Superior STY is attributed to enhanced metal dispersion and support acidity, with the bimetallic system outperforming both monometallic and unsupported references.

---
**Summary:**  
The K–NiMo/AC2a catalyst, featuring a high-surface-area, acid-treated support, demonstrates superior CO conversion, high selectivity toward oxygenates, favorable chain growth (α factor), and excellent stability. These performance metrics underscore the critical influence of support properties and bimetallic synergy in optimizing CO hydrogenation to higher alcohols.
