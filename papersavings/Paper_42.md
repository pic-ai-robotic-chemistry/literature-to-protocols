## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fischer–Tropsch catalyst (Fe/Mn/K) is synthesized using a combined co‑precipitation and impregnation approach. This method ensures reproducibility, precise control over the catalyst composition, and optimal performance through sequential processing steps.

#### • Co‑precipitation Method
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: Aqueous solutions of Fe(NO₃)₃ and Mn(NO₃)₂ are mixed in a continuously stirred tank reactor.  
   - pH Adjustment: NH₄OH is added to maintain a pH between 8.8 and 9.0 throughout the process.  
   - Temperature: The reaction is conducted at 351–353 K, ensuring proper precipitate formation of the Fe–Mn oxyhydroxide precursor.
   
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - As the metal nitrates mix with ammonium hydroxide, a definitive precipitation–deposition step occurs immediately, forming a uniform precipitate.  
   - Observations: Formation of a solid precipitate indicates successful deposition before any drying process.

3. Drying  
   - Filtration and Washing: Following precipitation, the solid is filtered and thoroughly washed with de‐ionized water to remove residual ions.  
   - Air‑Drying: The wet precipitate is then air‑dried at approximately 393 K for 36 hours to remove moisture effectively.

#### • Impregnation and Final Processing
4. Potassium Impregnation  
   - Reagent: K₂CO₃ solution is added for potassium doping, with target loadings of 0, 0.2, 0.7, 1.5, and 3.0 wt.%.  
   - Process: After impregnation, the sample is dried an additional 24 hours at ~393 K, ensuring the potassium is incorporated uniformly.

5. Calcination and Shaping  
   - Calcination: The impregnated precursor is calcined at 773 K for 5 hours, facilitating the transformation of Fe–Mn species into stable iron oxide phases and eventual formation of iron carbides under reaction conditions.  
   - Post‑treatment: The calcined catalyst is compressed into pellets at 60 MPa and sieved to retain particles in the 20–40 mesh range, optimizing flow characteristics for catalytic evaluation.

### Comparative Analysis of Synthesis Techniques
• Advantages: The integrated co‑precipitation and impregnation method permits precise control of metal ratios, pH, and thermal conditions, ensuring reproducibility and scalability.  
• Disadvantages: The need for continuous pH monitoring and multiple drying steps increases procedural complexity and requires specialized equipment, such as a pH meter and controlled heating systems, to maintain consistency.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the step‐by‐step preparation of the Fe/Mn/K catalyst precursor with emphasis on complete reproducibility. The procedures below are organized into three parts: weighing of solid reagents, liquid transfer operations, and mixing of the resulting solutions.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe/Mn/K Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃**: Weighed using an analytical balance; the exact mass is determined based on the desired metal loading.
- **Mn(NO₃)₂**: Weighed using an analytical balance; the quantity is adjusted to achieve the targeted catalyst composition.

Note: Although specific masses are not provided in the text, these reagents are measured precisely to ensure the correct stoichiometry for the co‐precipitation process.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe/Mn/K Catalyst
- **Metal Nitrate Solution**: Prepared by dissolving the weighed Fe(NO₃)₃ and Mn(NO₃)₂ in deionized water and transferred dropwise into a preheated (351–353 K) reactor containing a 200 cm³ ammonium hydroxide solution. The dropwise addition is performed under continuous stirring to maintain a pH between 8.8 and 9.0.
- **K₂CO₃ Solution**: Transferred by controlled pouring into the precipitated precursor. The volume of the K₂CO₃ solution is adjusted to achieve potassium loadings at 0, 0.2, 0.7, 1.5, and 3.0 wt.%, with the optimum performance typically observed around 0.7 wt.%.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe/Mn/K Catalyst
- **Precursor Suspension**: Mixed continuously at 351–353 K during the precipitation step. The metal nitrate solution, upon addition into the ammonium hydroxide, is stirred at constant speed to ensure uniform pH control and homogeneity.
- **Post-Impregnation Mixture**: The impregnated mixture is stirred under similar thermal conditions to ensure complete dispersion of the potassium dopant. No ultrasonic treatment is applied; continuous stirring remains critical for reproducible catalyst precursor formation.

This structured protocol—comprising precise solid reagent weighing, controlled liquid transfers, and regulated mixing—ensures that each experimental step is clearly documented and reproducible, aligning with the detailed protocol necessary for synthesizing the Fe/Mn/K catalyst used in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/Mn/K Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Prepare an aqueous solution containing Fe(NO₃)₃ and Mn(NO₃)₂ in deionized water (exact quantities as per experimental design). Concurrently, prepare an NH₄OH solution for pH adjustment.
- **Conditions**: Preheat both solutions; maintain the reaction temperature between 351–353 K. Use a continuously stirred tank reactor with real‐time pH monitoring to keep the pH within 8.8–9.0.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the metal nitrates in deionized water to form a homogeneous mixture.
  - Gradually add the Fe/Mn solution into the NH₄OH solution at a controlled flow rate while continuously stirring.
  - Adjust the pH by titrating the NH₄OH to ensure consistent precipitation of the Fe–Mn oxyhydroxide precursor.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure rigorous pH control and temperature stability to guarantee a uniform precursor formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly (Potassium Impregnation)
- **Reagents**: Utilize the previously prepared Fe/Mn precipitate along with an aqueous K₂CO₃ solution (doping levels set at 0, 0.2, 0.7, 1.5, and 3.0 wt.%).
- **Conditions**: Following filtration and initial drying, expose the precursor to the K₂CO₃ solution at room temperature under gentle stirring.
- **Process**:
  - Filter and wash the precipitate with deionized water.
  - Air-dry the washed material at approximately 393 K for 36 hours.
  - Impregnate the dried precursor with the K₂CO₃ solution ensuring even distribution of the promoter.
  - Remove excess solvent by gentle evaporation at 353 K.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Purification**: Post-impregnation, re-filter and thoroughly wash the material.
- **Drying**: Dry in air again at 393 K for an additional 24 hours.
- **Calcination**: Heat the dried catalyst at 773 K for 5 hours in an air atmosphere. Monitor the ramping rate to ensure complete conversion of the precursor.

##### Step 4: Final Activation and Shaping
- **Process**: Compress the calcined catalyst into pellets at 60 MPa, then crush and sieve to a particle size of 20–40 mesh.
- **Key Steps**: Adhere to strict temperature, pH, and timing protocols to ensure reproducibility of catalyst properties and performance.

This comprehensive workflow—from raw precursor synthesis to potassium impregnation and calcination—ensures clear, reproducible conditions essential for optimizing the catalyst’s structure and activity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a pre‐reaction reduction step designed to transform the precursor iron oxides into their active carbide forms for effective Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This activation is carried out under carefully controlled conditions to ensure optimal exposure of active sites and uniform reduction.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for driving the reduction of iron oxide precursors into the active phases while balancing phase evolution and catalyst integrity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fe/Mn/K catalyst is activated at 523 K.
- **Observations:** This temperature is selected to facilitate the conversion of Fe₂O₃ into active iron carbides without promoting excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure governs the interaction between the reducing gas and the catalyst surface, influencing reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction step is performed at a low pressure of 0.10 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at 0.10 MPa ensures a controlled reduction environment that is essential for effective phase transformation while avoiding excessive catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation determines the reduction environment and affects the degree of catalyst reduction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A synthesis gas with a H₂/CO ratio of 2.0 is employed, using a controlled flow via a mass flow controller in a quartz tube placed within a tubular furnace.
- **Observations:** This H₂-rich syngas facilitates the reduction of iron oxides, enabling the formation of active iron carbide phases. Its precise composition is crucial to prevent over‐hydrogenation and maintain catalyst selectivity.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity reflects the gas flow rate relative to catalyst mass, ensuring adequate contact between the gas and catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process utilizes a space-time velocity of 1000 h⁻¹.
- **Observations:** This high gas flow rate promotes uniform exposure across the catalyst bed, ensuring complete reduction.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is necessary to achieve a thorough reduction of catalyst precursors.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced for 32 hours.
- **Observations:** A prolonged activation time guarantees full phase transformation, thereby establishing a reproducible and effective active catalyst state for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase evolution of the Fe/Mn/K catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer effect spectroscopy (MES). These techniques are critical for identifying active phases and understanding the impact of potassium doping on phase composition.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis of reduced catalysts revealed Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) as the dominant phase, with Fe₂O₃ (hematite) also present. After Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS), broad and weak peaks at 2θ ≈ 31° and 44.5° were observed, corresponding to iron carbides (−Fe₅C₂ and ε-Fe₂.₂C). The intensity of these carbide peaks increased with higher potassium loading, indicating that potassium promotes iron carbide formation. MES further confirmed the presence of Fe₅C₂, with sextets exhibiting hyperfine fields of 188–191 kOe and quadrupole splittings of 0.27–0.28 mm/s. The hyperfine field of γ-Fe₂O₃ increased with crystallite size, reflecting a transition from superparamagnetic to ferromagnetic structure as potassium content rose.
- **Observations:** The formation and enrichment of iron carbides are enhanced by potassium, which is directly linked to improved catalytic activity and selectivity in FTS.

#### 6.1.2 Crystallite Size Evolution
- **Description:** Crystallite size was determined from XRD peak broadening using the Scherrer equation, providing insight into the textural changes induced by potassium addition.
- **Findings:** Increasing potassium content led to larger γ-Fe₂O₃ crystallite sizes, as evidenced by XRD. This growth is attributed to potassium-induced agglomeration during calcination at 773 K.
- **Observations:** Larger crystallite sizes correlate with decreased surface area and altered reduction behavior, impacting catalyst performance.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** BET surface area analysis was performed using N₂ adsorption at 77 K to assess the effects of potassium on textural properties, including surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter.
- **Findings:** Potassium addition resulted in lower BET surface areas and pore volumes, with an increase in average pore diameter. These changes are due to crystallite growth rather than pore blocking. Samples were degassed at 393 K for 6 hours prior to measurement.
- **Observations:** Reduced surface area and increased pore size with higher potassium content may limit active site accessibility but can also facilitate mass transport under certain conditions.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction Behavior (TPR/H₂)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) with hydrogen was used to evaluate the reducibility of the catalysts and the influence of potassium on hydrogen chemisorption.
- **Findings:** Potassium strongly inhibited the reduction of iron oxides, as shown by suppressed hydrogen uptake and delayed reduction profiles. This effect is attributed to the interaction between potassium oxide and iron oxide, which suppresses hydrogen adsorption.
- **Observations:** The inhibition of reduction is more pronounced at higher potassium loadings, which can affect the formation of active phases during activation.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Elemental Composition (XPS)
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the surface atomic ratios of Fe, Mn, and K, and to assess the distribution of potassium and manganese on the catalyst surface.
- **Findings:** XPS revealed that the K/Fe atomic ratio on the surface is significantly higher than in the bulk, confirming potassium surface enrichment. The Fe/Mn ratio suggests that potassium addition also promotes manganese enrichment at the surface. XPS measurements were conducted with Al Kα radiation (energy resolution: 0.8 eV), and peak positions were referenced to C 1s at 284.6 eV.
- **Observations:** Surface enrichment of potassium and manganese is critical for modulating catalytic properties, particularly olefin selectivity and suppression of undesired hydrogenation.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (2.50 MPa, 523–593 K, GHSV 1000 h⁻¹, H₂/CO = 2.0), CO conversion increased with potassium loading up to 0.7 wt.%, reaching a maximum at this level. For example, at 0.7 wt.% K, the catalyst exhibited the highest CO conversion across the tested temperature range. Further increases in potassium (>0.7 wt.%) led to a marked decline in CO conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal potassium content (0.7 wt.%) enhanced both FTS and water-gas shift (WGS) activities. Excessive potassium caused significant carbon deposition, blocking active sites and reducing conversion. Higher reaction temperatures generally increased CO conversion, but this effect diminished at high potassium loadings.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicative of WGS activity:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity increased with both potassium content and reaction temperature. The catalyst with 0.7 wt.% K showed enhanced CO₂ formation, consistent with increased WGS activity. At higher potassium levels, CO₂ selectivity remained elevated, even as overall CO conversion declined.
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium promotion facilitated the WGS reaction, with the highest selectivity observed at moderate K loading. This trend paralleled the increase in CO conversion up to the optimal K content.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity was significantly suppressed by potassium addition. The lowest CH₄ selectivity was observed at 0.7 wt.% K, with further increases in potassium maintaining low methane formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium acts as a promoter that restrains methane and light hydrocarbon formation, shifting selectivity toward olefins. Higher temperatures tended to increase methane selectivity in unpromoted catalysts, but this effect was mitigated by potassium doping.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** C₃⁺ selectivity peaked at 0.7 wt.% K, corresponding to the highest olefin/paraffin ratio and suppressed light hydrocarbon formation. Excessive potassium led to a decline in C₃⁺ selectivity due to deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal potassium level promoted longer-chain hydrocarbon formation, while higher K content reduced C₃⁺ selectivity as a result of increased carbon deposition and reduced active site availability.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, calculated from product distribution:
  - α values range from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (maximum chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** The α factor increased with potassium loading up to 0.7 wt.%, reflecting enhanced selectivity toward longer-chain hydrocarbons. Beyond this, α plateaued or decreased, correlating with catalyst deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate potassium doping favored chain growth, while excessive K led to a decline in α due to carbon deposition and loss of active sites.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Deactivation was most pronounced at high potassium loadings (>0.7 wt.%), where CO conversion declined rapidly due to carbon deposition. At 0.7 wt.% K, the catalyst maintained stable performance over extended operation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Excessive potassium promoted carbon buildup, accelerating deactivation. Optimal K content balanced activity, selectivity, and stability.

---
**Summary:** The potassium-promoted Fe/Mn catalyst exhibited optimal FTS performance at 0.7 wt.% K, with maximum CO conversion, high C₃⁺ and olefin selectivity, elevated α factor, and minimal deactivation. Higher potassium levels led to increased CO₂ selectivity and rapid deactivation due to carbon deposition, underscoring the importance of precise promoter optimization.
