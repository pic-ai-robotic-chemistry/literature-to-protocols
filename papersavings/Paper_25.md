## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalysts were synthesized using the incipient wetness impregnation method, which was optimized to achieve a targeted 20 wt% Fe loading by ensuring that the aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O solution exactly matches the total pore volume of the acid‐treated CNT supports. The acid treatment—conducted with 60 wt% nitric acid at 110°C for 16 hours—serves to remove impurities and functionalize the CNT surface, thereby enhancing capillary filling and uniform metal dispersion.

#### • Incipient Wetness Impregnation for Fe/np‑CNT
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagent: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O  
   - CNT Support: Acid-treated narrow pore CNT (≈12 nm), pre-verified by nitrogen adsorption measurements  
   - Process: The Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O aqueous solution is prepared such that its volume equals the CNT pore volume to ensure complete capillary filling.
   
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Note: An additional chemical precipitation–deposition step is not applied in this protocol, as the incipient wetness approach inherently ensures controlled deposition within the CNT channels.
   
3. Drying  
   - Conditions: Drying at 120°C in a temperature-controlled oven  
   - Duration: Until complete solvent removal is confirmed (typically several hours)
   
4. Calcination  
   - Conditions: Calcination at 400°C  
   - Duration: 3 hours in a furnace to convert the iron nitrate to iron oxide species
   - Equipment: Temperature-controlled furnace ensures reproducibility of heating profiles
   
5. Additional Post‑treatment  
   - In‑situ reduction is performed in a fixed-bed micro reactor at 380°C for 14 hours under a continuous H₂ flow to reduce iron oxide, as verified by H₂-TPR.

A similar procedure is applied for Fe/wp‑CNT, with only the support pore diameter (≈63 nm) differing. This difference in pore size explains the distinct deposition dynamics: the narrow pore CNTs facilitate better dispersion and smaller iron oxide particles, while the wide pore CNTs tend to result in larger, more agglomerated particles. Such detailed control of synthesis parameters—including solution volume matched to pore volume, precise drying and calcination conditions, and subsequent in-situ reduction—ensures the reproducibility and effectiveness of the catalyst synthesis process.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the impregnation procedure for both Fe/np-CNT (narrow pore) and Fe/wp-CNT (wide pore) catalysts is described. Although explicit numerical details for solid reagent weighing are not provided, the protocol outlines the use of an aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O solution prepared in an amount equal to the total pore volume of the corresponding acid‐treated CNT support. The following subsections detail the catalyst‐specific procedures for liquid transfer and mixing.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

No explicit numerical values (e.g., exact masses or balance precision) for the solid reagents, including Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and the CNT supports, are provided in the available documentation. The preparation implicitly assumes that the required quantities are calculated based on the targeted 20 wt% Fe loading and the measured pore volumes of the supports.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe/np-CNT  
- **Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O solution**: Transferred into the acid‐treated np-CNT support via capillary action, with the solution volume carefully matched to the support’s total pore volume to ensure complete imbibition.  

##### 3.1.2.2 Fe/wp-CNT  
- **Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O solution**: Poured onto the wp-CNT support using incipient wetness impregnation so that the solution volume equals the total pore volume of the support, facilitating capillary filling of the wide pores.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe/np-CNT  
- **Impregnated np-CNT suspension**: Mixed to achieve uniform dispersion of the iron precursor within the CNT pores. Post impregnation, the catalyst undergoes drying at 120°C followed by calcination at 400°C for 3 h. An ultrasonic dispersion step is later applied during sample preparation for microscopic analysis.

##### 3.1.3.2 Fe/wp-CNT  
- **Impregnated wp-CNT suspension**: Mixed in a similar manner to ensure even distribution of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O across the wide pore structure. Subsequent drying and calcination conditions are kept analogous to those for Fe/np-CNT, and ultrasonic treatment is employed when preparing specimens for TEM examination.

This structured approach, while not specifying exact weighing parameters, emphasizes the critical control of liquid volume and mixing conditions to ensure reproducibility in catalyst synthesis using the incipient wetness impregnation method.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/np-CNT
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (calculated to yield 20 wt% Fe), deionized water, and acid-treated narrow pore CNT (np-CNT) with an average pore diameter of ~12 nm.
- **Conditions**: Use magnetic stirring at room temperature; the deionized water volume is precisely adjusted to match the total pore volume of the np-CNT.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the appropriate amount of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in deionized water under continuous stirring to form a uniform solution.
  - Gradually add the np-CNT support into the precursor solution ensuring complete wetting by capillary action.
  - Maintain the mixture under stirring for uniform dispersion.
- **Key Steps**: Verify accurate reagent measurements and that the precursor solution exactly fills the CNT pores, ensuring optimal impregnation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Process**:
  - After impregnation, allow the catalyst slurry to equilibrate momentarily to ensure uniform dispersion.
  - If necessary, apply a gentle filtration to remove any insoluble residues before proceeding to drying.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the impregnated sample in a PID-controlled oven at 120°C to remove residual moisture.
- **Calcination**:
  - Heat the dried sample to 400°C at a controlled ramp rate under ambient air and maintain this temperature for 3 hours to convert the precursor into iron oxide (primarily Fe₂O₃).
- **Key Steps**: Strict temperature control during drying and calcination is critical to prevent agglomeration and ensure proper phase transformation.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe/wp-CNT
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and deionized water combined with wide pore CNT (wp-CNT) prepared via CVD using AAO templates (average pore diameter ~63 nm).
- **Conditions & Process**:
  - Repeat the same impregnation method as for np-CNT; ensure the aqueous solution volume is matched to the total pore volume of wp-CNT, facilitating complete capillary filling.
  
##### Steps 2 & 3: Catalyst Assembly, Drying & Calcination
- Follow identical procedures for catalyst assembly, drying (120°C), and calcination (400°C for 3 hours in air).  
- **Key Steps**: Extra consideration is advised to monitor dispersion, as larger pores can affect the metal oxide phase (notably Fe₃O₄ formation) and promote agglomeration.

Overall, rigorous adherence to reagent measurements, controlled mixing, and precise thermal treatments ensures reproducibility and optimal catalytic performance for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

In preparation for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction, the catalyst undergoes an in-situ activation (reduction) process designed to convert the iron oxide precursor into its active metallic state. This activation step is performed in a fixed-bed micro reactor where precise control of temperature and gas flow is critical for ensuring optimal catalyst performance.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
The catalyst reduction is initiated at room temperature and the temperature is ramped at a controlled rate of 1 °C/min until reaching 380 °C. This gradual increase ensures that the reduction process proceeds uniformly without thermal overshoot. Holding the catalyst at 380 °C is essential for effectively reducing the iron oxide species while preventing adverse effects such as sintering or support damage.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
A pure hydrogen atmosphere is employed during the activation step. The exclusive use of hydrogen facilitates complete reduction of the iron oxides to metallic iron, creating the active sites necessary for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The absence of carbon-containing gases during this phase is crucial in avoiding any inadvertent carbide formation, which could compromise catalyst performance.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time and Temperature Hold
Once the catalyst reaches 380 °C, it is maintained at this temperature for 14 hours. This extended dwell time is designed to maximize the reduction extent and ensure that all reducible species are fully activated. After this period, the temperature is then decreased to 275 °C to set the reactor conditions for the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

These carefully controlled parameters—namely, the temperature ramp, the hydrogen environment, and the prolonged 14-hour hold at 380 °C—are critical in ensuring that the catalyst exhibits high reduction efficiency with well-dispersed metal particles. Although explicit activation pressure conditions are not stated for this in-situ reduction step, the overall process is integrated into a fixed-bed reactor system that later operates at 2 MPa during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer-Tropsch reaction was conducted in a fixed-bed reactor under carefully controlled experimental conditions to ensure reproducibility and accurate evaluation of catalyst performance. The following subsections detail the key parameters:

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature was maintained at 275 °C throughout the catalytic evaluation. This temperature is critical in activating synthesis gas for effective CO hydrogenation, thus directly influencing the conversion rate and hydrocarbon product distribution. At 275 °C, the iron-based catalysts achieve a balance between adequate reaction rates and the stable formation of long-chain hydrocarbons, as evidenced by superior C₅⁺ selectivity for the Fe/np-CNT catalyst.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Experiments were performed at a reaction pressure of 2 MPa. This elevated pressure increases the concentration of synthesis gas within the reactor, which enhances the kinetics of the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis and favors the production of heavier hydrocarbons. The selected pressure level is standard in FT processes, contributing to improved overall conversion efficiencies under the fixed-bed reactor setup.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
A stoichiometric H₂/CO ratio of 2 was employed during the catalytic tests. This specific ratio is pivotal in controlling the hydrocarbon product distribution, as it directly affects the selectivity between methane and longer-chain hydrocarbons. In the context of the study, the chosen ratio helped promote the formation of C₅⁺ hydrocarbons while minimizing methane production, particularly for the Fe/np-CNT catalyst compared to the Fe/wp-CNT catalyst.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The reaction utilized a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 7200 cm³ (STP)/(h g_cat), which denotes the flow rate of synthesis gas relative to the catalyst mass. This parameter critically governs the residence time of reactants over the catalyst surface, influencing both CO conversion efficiency and catalyst stability. Under these conditions, the Fe/np-CNT catalyst maintained high activity over 120 hours of operation, with only a slight decline in CO conversion from approximately 31% to 26%, while the Fe/wp-CNT catalyst experienced more pronounced deactivation.

Each of these parameters—temperature, pressure, gas composition, and flow rate—was meticulously set to align with the standard protocols for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis, ensuring that the reported results can be directly reproduced and compared in future studies.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure (XRD)
- **Description:** X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to determine the crystalline phases and estimate the crystallite sizes of iron oxide species on the CNT supports. Accurate phase identification is essential for correlating catalyst structure with Fischer–Tropsch (FT) activity.
- **Findings:** For Fe/np-CNT, XRD patterns display diffraction peaks matching standard hematite (Fe₂O₃), with the most intense peak at 2θ = 36°. The calculated crystallite size using the Debye–Scherrer equation is 11 ± 2 nm. In contrast, Fe/wp-CNT exhibits peaks at 2θ = 30° and 35.7°, corresponding to magnetite (Fe₃O₄), with a larger crystallite size of 19 ± 1 nm.
- **Observations:** The narrow pore CNT support favors the formation of smaller Fe₂O₃ crystallites, while the wide pore support leads to larger Fe₃O₄ particles. The smaller crystallite size in Fe/np-CNT is associated with higher metal dispersion and improved FT performance.

#### 6.1.2 Reducibility (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) assesses the ease of reduction of iron oxides, which is critical for generating active metallic iron sites in FT synthesis.
- **Findings:** Fe/np-CNT shows four reduction peaks: A (Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄) at 401°C, B (Fe₃O₄ → FeO) at 438°C, C (FeO → Fe) at 667°C, and D (CNT gasification) at 747°C, with an overall extent of reduction of 79%. Fe/wp-CNT displays two main peaks: B (Fe₃O₄ → FeO) at 459°C and C (FeO → Fe) at 574°C, with a lower extent of reduction of 62%.
- **Observations:** The higher reducibility and lower reduction temperatures in Fe/np-CNT are attributed to smaller particle size and stronger metal-support interaction, resulting in a greater fraction of catalytically active iron.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Dispersion (TEM/SEM)
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provide insights into particle size distribution, spatial location, and agglomeration of iron oxide particles on CNT supports.
- **Findings:** TEM analysis of Fe/np-CNT reveals iron oxide particles predominantly in the 8–10 nm range, with approximately 80% located inside the CNT channels. After 120 h on stream, particles remain well-dispersed (7–16 nm). For Fe/wp-CNT, particles are larger (14–25 nm fresh, shifting to 22–38 nm after reaction) and exhibit significant agglomeration. SEM confirms the structural integrity and morphology of both CNT supports.
- **Observations:** The narrow pore structure of np-CNT restricts particle growth and agglomeration, maintaining high dispersion and stability, whereas the wide pores of wp-CNT permit larger, less stable particles.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity (BET)
- **Description:** Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis quantifies surface area and pore volume, which influence metal dispersion and reactant accessibility.
- **Findings:** After Fe loading, Fe/np-CNT exhibits a surface area of 155 m²/g (down from 212 m²/g for the support), while Fe/wp-CNT shows 186 m²/g. The decrease in surface area reflects pore filling by iron oxide.
- **Observations:** Although both catalysts retain high surface areas, the greater reduction in Fe/np-CNT indicates more effective pore utilization and metal dispersion within the narrow channels.

#### 6.1.5 Metal Content (ICP)
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis verifies the actual iron loading on the catalysts.
- **Findings:** Both Fe/np-CNT and Fe/wp-CNT have iron contents close to the target 20 wt% (19.6% and 20.1%, respectively).
- **Observations:** The close agreement with the intended loading confirms the reliability of the incipient wetness impregnation method for both CNT supports.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch (FT) reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Fe/np-CNT:** Initial CO conversion was 31%, decreasing slightly to 26% after 120 hours on stream.  
  - **Fe/wp-CNT:** CO conversion started at 18% and declined to less than 10% over the same period.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The Fe/np-CNT catalyst maintained higher and more stable CO conversion throughout the reaction, while Fe/wp-CNT exhibited rapid deactivation and a pronounced drop in conversion. The superior performance of Fe/np-CNT is attributed to smaller, well-dispersed iron particles confined within narrow CNT pores, enhancing reducibility and active site availability.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Fe/np-CNT:** 14.5 ± 0.5 wt%  
  - **Fe/wp-CNT:** 41.0 ± 0.5 wt%
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The Fe/np-CNT catalyst exhibited significantly lower methane selectivity compared to Fe/wp-CNT, indicating a strong preference for longer-chain hydrocarbon formation in the presence of narrow pore confinement.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, reflecting the catalyst’s ability to promote chain growth:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Fe/np-CNT:** 48.5 wt%  
  - **Fe/wp-CNT:** 12.0 wt%
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The Fe/np-CNT catalyst achieved a much higher selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons, underscoring the beneficial effect of narrow pore CNTs on chain growth probability and product distribution.

#### 6.2.4 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion over time, indicating long-term stability:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **Fe/np-CNT:** CO conversion decreased from 31% to 26% over 120 h (deactivation rate ≈ 0.042% h⁻¹).  
  - **Fe/wp-CNT:** CO conversion dropped from 18% to <10% over 120 h (deactivation rate ≈ 0.067% h⁻¹).
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The Fe/np-CNT catalyst demonstrated superior stability, with only a minor loss in activity, while Fe/wp-CNT deactivated rapidly, correlating with increased particle agglomeration and reduced active surface area.

#### 6.2.5 Summary of Performance Trends
- The Fe/np-CNT catalyst, featuring smaller and more dispersed iron oxide particles within narrow CNT pores, consistently outperformed Fe/wp-CNT in terms of CO conversion, selectivity for heavier hydrocarbons, and operational stability. The spatial restriction provided by the narrow pores is critical for maintaining high dispersion, reducibility, and resistance to deactivation, resulting in enhanced FT synthesis performance.
