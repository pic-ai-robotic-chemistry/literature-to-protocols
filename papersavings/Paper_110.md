## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis methods detailed in the article focus on the preparation of Ru-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts via controlled impregnation techniques and compare them with standard wet-impregnation approaches. Below are the step-by-step procedures extracted for the Ru-in/TNT catalyst, along with a brief mention of complementary methods for comparative purposes.

#### Ru-in/TNT Synthesis via Vacuum-assisted Incipient Wetness Impregnation

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagent: A calculated amount of RuCl₃ (provided by Sinopharm, China) in an aqueous solution is used to achieve an active Ru loading of ≈5.0 wt%.  
   - Support: The TiO₂ nanotube (TNT) support, which is synthesized by reacting TiO₂ with 10 M NaOH at 140 °C for 24 h in a Teflon container, is employed.  
   - Experimental Observation: Uniform distribution is indicated by the effective loading within the nanotube structure.

2. • Vacuum-assisted Impregnation  
   - Equipment: A vacuum setup, maintaining a pressure of <10⁻² Pa, is applied to facilitate the penetration of the RuCl₃ solution into the inner pores of the TNT.  
   - Impact: This method ensures confinement of the Ru nanoparticles inside the TNT channels, in contrast to the typical wet-impregnation method where deposition occurs largely on the outer surface.

3. • Drying  
   - Conditions: The impregnated sample is vacuum dried overnight at 40 °C.  
   - Purpose: Controlled drying removes excess moisture and stabilizes the precursor distribution without premature decomposition.

4. • Calcination and Post‑treatment  
   - Calcination: The dried sample is calcined at 300 °C for 2 h, a critical step that converts the precursor into active Ru nanoparticles and enhances metal–support interactions.  
   - Additional Reduction: A subsequent H₂ reduction at 300 °C for 12 h is performed to fully activate the metallic Ru and further improve dispersion and bonding within the TNT support.

#### Comparative Analysis

• The article contrasts this method with the conventional wet‑impregnation technique (used for Ru-out/TNT and Ru/P25 catalysts), highlighting that while the vacuum-assisted method is more complex and requires specialized equipment, it significantly improves the confinement and uniform dispersion of Ru nanoparticles, thereby enhancing catalytic performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This detailed methodology ensures reproducibility and clarity for researchers aiming to replicate the catalyst synthesis process accurately.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Ru-in/TNT

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • TiO₂ (as received, Hualisen Corp., China)  
  • Concentrated NaOH solution (100 mL, 10 M from Sinopharm Corp.)  
  • RuCl₃ (Sinopharm Corp.) in a concentration calculated for ~5.0 wt% Ru loading  
- **Conditions**:  
  • TNT synthesis at 140 °C for 24 hours in a Teflon container  
  • Washing with deionized water until solution conductivity falls below 100 s/cm  
- **Process**:  
  1. Mix TiO₂ with 100 mL of 10 M NaOH in a Teflon container and stir under controlled conditions.  
  2. Heat at 140 °C for 24 hours to form TNT support.  
  3. Rinse the resulting white precipitate repeatedly with deionized water until the conductivity remains below 100 s/cm, ensuring removal of unreacted NaOH.  
  4. Prepare an aqueous RuCl₃ solution with precise stoichiometry to be used in subsequent steps.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough washing to completely remove residual NaOH, critical for the proper formation of TNT.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**: TNT support from Step 1 and the prepared RuCl₃ solution  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Vacuum-assisted incipient wetness impregnation at a vacuum pressure below 10⁻² Pa  
- **Process**:  
  1. Introduce the RuCl₃ solution into the TNT support under vacuum, promoting deep penetration into the inner pores.  
  2. Maintain even distribution of Ru precursor to secure enhanced metal–support interactions and reduce metal leaching.  
- **Key Steps**: Uniform impregnation is essential to confine Ru nanoparticles within TNT’s inner structure.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Conditions & Process**:  
  1. Vacuum dry the impregnated catalyst at 40 °C overnight.  
  2. Calcine the dried sample at 300 °C for 2 hours with a controlled ramp rate in a vacuum atmosphere to develop the final catalyst structure.  
- **Key Steps**: Strict control of drying and calcination conditions prevents nanoparticle sintering.

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Conditions & Process**:  
  1. Activate the catalyst by reduction under a hydrogen (H₂) flow at 300 °C for 12 hours.  
- **Key Steps**: This activation step ensures formation of uniformly dispersed and active Ru nanoparticles, critical for high catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an X'Pert Pro MPD diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. XRD is essential for identifying the support phase and assessing the crystallinity and size of Ru nanoparticles.
- **Findings:** The XRD patterns of the TiO₂ nanotube (TNT) support exhibited characteristic peaks at 25°, 37°, 48°, 55°, and 62°, confirming the anatase phase. No distinct peaks corresponding to metallic Ru were observed in Ru-in/TNT, indicating that Ru nanoparticles were either amorphous or below the XRD detection limit (<4 nm).
- **Observations:** The absence of Ru peaks suggests highly dispersed, ultra-small Ru nanoparticles, consistent with the intended confinement within TNT.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were evaluated by N₂ adsorption–desorption (BET) using a Tristar 3010 analyzer. These parameters are critical for understanding catalyst dispersion and mass transfer properties.
- **Findings:** The TNT support in Ru-in/TNT exhibited a high BET surface area of 240 m²/g (fresh), with a pore size of 7.9 nm. After reaction, the surface area decreased slightly to 205 m²/g, indicating good thermal and structural stability.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and mesoporous structure facilitate uniform Ru dispersion and efficient reactant diffusion, supporting enhanced catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2010 at 200 kV) was used to visualize Ru nanoparticle dispersion and confinement within TNT. Particle size was determined by measuring 200 randomly selected Ru nanoparticles per sample.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed that Ru-in/TNT contained Ru nanoparticles with sizes predominantly in the 2–4 nm range, confined within the inner pores of the nanotubes. The lattice fringe spacing of 0.20 nm matched the d(101) plane of metallic Ru. In contrast, Ru-out/TNT showed Ru nanoparticles mainly on the outer surface, with increased aggregation after reaction.
- **Observations:** The vacuum-assisted impregnation method enabled uniform internal distribution and minimized sintering, while conventional impregnation led to surface aggregation and leaching.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Metal–Support Interaction
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS Ultra DLD) was employed to determine Ru oxidation states and probe metal–support interactions.
- **Findings:** In Ru-in/TNT, only a single metallic Ru⁰ phase was detected, with a 0.3 eV positive shift in the Ru 3d peak and a 0.1 eV negative shift in the Ti 2p peak compared to Ru-out/TNT. Ru-out/TNT exhibited both Ru³⁺ and Ru⁰ phases.
- **Observations:** The observed peak shifts indicate strong charge transfer and robust metal–support interaction in Ru-in/TNT, contributing to enhanced stability and resistance to leaching.

#### 6.1.5 Reducibility and Hydrogen Adsorption
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction/desorption (H₂-TPR/TPD, Autosorb-iQ-C) assessed catalyst reducibility and hydrogen adsorption capacity.
- **Findings:** Ru-in/TNT displayed a reduction peak at 150–200 °C, with a hydrogen adsorption capacity of 0.464 mmol/g. The reduction peak was slightly lower in temperature than Ru-out/TNT, indicating easier reducibility.
- **Observations:** Higher H₂ adsorption and facile reduction reflect more available active sites and stronger metal–support interaction, correlating with superior catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.6 Metal Content and Leaching Resistance
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) quantified Ru content and assessed leaching after reaction.
- **Findings:** Ru-in/TNT retained most Ru in the solid phase post-reaction (0.41 mmol/g in bulk, 0.08 mmol/g in liquid), while Ru-out/TNT suffered significant Ru loss to the liquid phase (0.12 mmol/g in bulk, 0.35 mmol/g in liquid).
- **Observations:** The confinement effect in TNT effectively suppresses Ru leaching, maintaining catalyst integrity and performance.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (H₂/CO = 1.0, 2.0 MPa, 240 °C, 3000 h⁻¹), the Ru-in/TNT catalyst exhibited an initial CO conversion rate approximately twice that of Ru-out/TNT and Ru/P25. After 20 h, Ru-in/TNT stabilized at ~20% CO conversion, while Ru-out/TNT and Ru/P25 declined to 3% and 4%, respectively. The initial activity for Ru-in/TNT reached 60 mol CO gRu⁻¹, surpassing most previously reported Ru catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior and sustained CO conversion of Ru-in/TNT is attributed to the confinement of Ru nanoparticles within the TNT support, which enhances metal–support interactions and active site availability.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Across all catalysts and temperatures, CH₄ selectivity remained consistent, with Ru-in/TNT showing values of 20.6% (200 °C), 22.7% (220 °C), 22.4% (240 °C), and 24.5% (260 °C).
- **Observations and Trends:** The similar CH₄ selectivity among catalysts indicates that the confinement effect primarily influences long-chain hydrocarbon formation rather than methane production.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Ru-in/TNT achieved high C₅⁺ selectivity: 64.4% (200 °C), 62.9% (220 °C), 62.0% (240 °C), and 63.5% (260 °C). After 48 h, C₅–₁₈ selectivity was 62.5%, with an additional 2.2% for C₁₉⁺.
- **Observations and Trends:** The enhanced C₅⁺ and C₁₉⁺ selectivity for Ru-in/TNT reflects the support’s ability to promote chain growth and re-adsorption of olefin intermediates.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model, indicates the probability of chain propagation:
  \[
  \alpha = \frac{\text{moles of (C}_{n+1}\text{ hydrocarbon)}}{\text{moles of (C}_n\text{ hydrocarbon)}}
  \]
  (determined from product distribution at similar CO conversion)
- **Experimental Data:** At ~20% CO conversion, Ru-in/TNT exhibited an α value of 0.65, compared to 0.80 (Ru-out/TNT) and 0.79 (Ru/P25).
- **Observations and Trends:** The lower α for Ru-in/TNT, despite higher long-chain selectivity, suggests more efficient chain growth and a product distribution favoring longer hydrocarbons due to enhanced re-adsorption and confinement effects.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Ru-in/TNT maintained stable CO conversion (~20%) over 48 h, while Ru-out/TNT and Ru/P25 experienced rapid deactivation, with CO conversion dropping by ~95% (to 3–4%) after 20 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The strong metal–support interaction and spatial confinement in Ru-in/TNT significantly suppress Ru leaching and sintering, resulting in superior stability and minimal deactivation compared to reference catalysts.
