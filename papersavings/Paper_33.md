## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the FeMn@Si‑c catalyst is achieved through a multi‑step procedure that combines precursor mixing, a precipitation–deposition stage, drying, calcination, and subsequent post‑treatments. The following outlines the detailed experimental procedure extracted from the article:

#### Synthesis of Fe₂O₃ Nanorods (Precursor Synthesis)
1. • Dissolve 15 g of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in 20 mL of water.
2. • Add 4 g of carbon black as a hard template and stir at 50°C for 11 h.
3. • Dry the mixture in a vacuum oven at 50°C for 6 h.
4. • Calcine the dried product in static air at 550°C for 5 h to obtain Fe₂O₃ nanorods.

#### SiO₂ Coating via the Modified Stöber Method
1. • Disperse 0.5 g of the prepared Fe₂O₃ in 300 mL of ethanol using ultrasonication.
2. • Add 2.5 mL of tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) and stir at 450 rpm for 4 h.
3. • Introduce 5 mL of ammonia (25–28%, AR) and 20 mL of water; continue stirring for an additional 4 h.
4. • Wash the coated product with ethanol and dry at 100°C for 11 h.

#### Incorporation of Mn Promoter via Precipitation–Deposition
1. • Disperse Fe₂O₃ in 300 mL of water and introduce a stoichiometric amount of Mn(NO₃)₂ (50%, AR).
2. • Heat the mixture in an oil bath at 90°C for 1 h; then add 2 mL of ammonia.
3. • Age the solution for 11 h under stirring to allow for uniform deposition.
4. • Follow with washing and drying at the reported conditions.

#### Hydrophobic Modification and Post‑treatment
1. • Disperse the resultant Fe@Si catalyst in n‑hexane and add 2.0 mL of chlorotrimethylsilane (TMCS) per gram of catalyst.
2. • Conduct an ultrasonic treatment at a frequency of 40 kHz and 400 W for 3 h at room temperature.
3. • Wash with n‑hexane and dry in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 11 h.
4. • Finally, reduce the dried catalyst at 350°C for 10 h in a pure H₂ atmosphere, followed by a carburization step at 320°C under a H₂/CO flow.

This comprehensive procedure ensures reproducibility through precisely controlled reagent concentrations, reaction temperatures, durations, and equipment settings, resulting in a catalyst with enhanced hydrophobic properties and optimized performance for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the detailed steps for reagent preparation of the FeMn@Si‑c catalyst are described under three subsections: weighing of solid reagents, liquid transfer of reagents, and mixing to obtain a homogeneous suspension. The following details reflect the experimental workflow, ensuring that every step is clearly documented for reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 FeMn@Si‑c
- **Fe₂O₃:** Weighed 0.5 g using an analytical balance. This solid precursor is prepared via a deposition–precipitation method and subsequently dried before use.  
  
(Note: While the incorporation of the Mn promoter is fundamental to achieving the desired promotion effect, specific weighing details for Mn(NO₃)₂ are not provided; its addition is described in the liquid transfer stage.)

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 FeMn@Si‑c
- **Deionized Water:** Poured 300 mL into the reaction vessel containing the pre-weighed Fe₂O₃, ensuring proper dispersion in the solvent.
- **Ammonia:** Poured 2 mL into the aqueous suspension using a beaker to adjust the pH for the subsequent coating process.
- **TMCS (Chlorotrimethylsilane):** Poured 2.0 mL per gram of Fe@Si into the preheated catalyst mixture after cooling in the vacuum oven. This hydrophobic modifier is crucial for grafting −CH₃ groups and enhancing surface hydrophobicity.
- **Mn(NO₃)₂ (50%, AR):** Transferred in a stoichiometric amount into the aqueous dispersion using a beaker to introduce the Mn promoter, which facilitates electron transfer during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 FeMn@Si‑c
- **Fe₂O₃ Suspension:** Mixed by stirring at 450 rpm for 4 hours at room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere. This step ensures the uniform dispersion of the solid reagent in water.
- **Hydrophobic Modification Mixture:** Mixed further through ultrasonic treatment at 40 kHz with a power setting of 400 W for 3 hours at room temperature. This ultrasonic treatment promotes a homogeneous integration of TMCS into the catalyst system, ensuring consistent hydrophobic encapsulation.

These detailed reagent preparation steps, organized by solid weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing procedures, provide clear operational guidance that supports the reproducibility of the FeMn@Si‑c catalyst synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: FeMn@Si‑c

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation – Fe₂O₃ Nanorods Synthesis
- **Reagents**: 15.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 4.0 g carbon black, 20 mL deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Stir at 50 °C for 11 hours; vacuum drying at 50 °C for 6 hours; calcination in static air at 550 °C for 5 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve 15.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in 20 mL water to form a homogeneous solution.
  - Add 4.0 g carbon black and stir at 50 °C for 11 hours to promote interaction.
  - Dry the mixture in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 6 hours.
  - Calcine the dried precursor at 550 °C for 5 hours to yield Fe₂O₃ nanorods.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and controlled calcination to maintain nanorod morphology.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly – SiO₂ Shell Coating via Modified Stöber Method
- **Reagents**: 0.5 g synthesized Fe₂O₃, 300 mL ethanol, 2.5 mL tetraethoxysilane (TEOS), 5 mL ammonia, 20 mL water.
- **Conditions**: Stir at 450 rpm; initial stirring for 4 hours at 100 °C.
- **Process**:
  - Disperse 0.5 g Fe₂O₃ in 300 mL ethanol.
  - Add 2.5 mL TEOS and subsequently 5 mL ammonia along with 20 mL water.
  - Stir the mixture at 450 rpm for 4 hours to form a homogeneous SiO₂ coating.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor stirring speed and temperature to ensure uniform shell formation.

##### Step [3]: Manganese Promoter Incorporation and Assembly Completion
- **Reagents**: Mn(NO₃)₂, incorporated to achieve a Mn/Fe ratio ≤ 0.3.
- **Process**:
  - After SiO₂ coating, disperse the catalyst in water and add Mn(NO₃)₂ with careful control of the promoter ratio.
  - Stir and allow sufficient aging to integrate manganese into the assembly.
- **Key Steps**: Strict ratio control to avoid the unwanted formation of Fe₃O₄.

##### Step [4]: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Process**:
  - Wash the catalyst with ethanol and water to remove residual impurities.
  - Dry at 100 °C for 11 hours.
  - If applicable, perform a gentle calcination with controlled ramp rates under atmospheric conditions.

##### Step [5]: Hydrophobic Modification and Final Activation
- **Reagents**: Chlorotrimethylsilane (TMCS) in n‑hexane.
- **Conditions**: Ultrasonic treatment at room temperature for 3 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Disperse Fe@Si in n‑hexane, add TMCS dropwise, and sonicate to graft −CH₃ groups, enhancing surface hydrophobicity.
  - Final in situ reduction at 350 °C under pure H₂ for 10 hours, followed by carburization at 320 °C under H₂/CO (4000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹, H₂/CO = 2).
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough ultrasonication and controlled gas flow during activation to guarantee reproducibility.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation of the FeMn@Si‑c catalyst is executed through a two-step pre‐reaction treatment designed specifically for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This procedure ensures the conversion of the catalyst’s iron oxide precursor into the active iron carbide phase, which is critical for promoting robust C–C coupling while reducing undesired by‐products.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential for converting iron oxide species into reduced metallic forms and subsequently into iron carbides.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is first reduced in situ at 350 °C under pure hydrogen. Following reduction, a carburization step is performed at 320 °C.
- **Observations:** The initial reduction at 350 °C enables complete removal of oxygen from the Fe₂O₃ nanorods, while the controlled carburization at 320 °C promotes the formation of well-dispersed iron carbide active sites without inducing excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the kinetics of the reduction and carburization reactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction step is conducted at atmospheric pressure (1 bar), and the subsequent carburization occurs under a slightly elevated pressure of 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at these pressures maintains an efficient gas-catalyst interaction necessary for effective transformation while preventing catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition directly impacts the reduction and carburization processes.
- **Experimental Conditions:** During reduction, the catalyst is exposed to pure H₂ to fully reduce the Fe₂O₃. In the carburization step, a syngas mixture with a H₂/CO ratio of 1 is applied.
- **Observations:** The pure hydrogen atmosphere is critical for achieving a complete reduction, and the introduction of CO during carburization drives the formation of the active iron carbide phase.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity and Time
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity controls the exposure intensity of the catalyst to the reactive gases, while the activation time ensures complete conversion.
- **Experimental Conditions:** For carburization, the syngas is introduced at a flow rate of 4000 mL/(gcat·h) for 5 hours, following 10 hours of reduction.
- **Observations:** Precise control over flow rate and duration is vital, ensuring that the active sites are optimally developed, leading to high catalytic stability and consistent performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is the operating temperature at which Fischer-Tropsch synthesis is performed. It directly influences syngas activation, CO conversion, and product selectivity.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic evaluation was conducted at a reaction temperature of 320 °C.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 320 °C ensures a high CO conversion rate with maintained catalyst efficiency. The specified temperature helped achieve robust performance during extended stability tests.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure refers to the total system pressure maintained during the process, impacting the concentration of reactants and the selectivity towards high-molecular-weight hydrocarbons.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reactor pressure was set at 2.0 MPa, a condition chosen to balance reaction kinetics and promote optimal product distribution.  
- **Observations:** The set pressure of 2.0 MPa was critical in suppressing undesired by-products such as CO₂ and CH₄, as higher pressures favor the formation of longer-chain olefins and improve overall catalyst performance.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio in the feed gas is essential for controlling the degree of hydrogenation during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis, which in turn affects the balance between methane and olefin production.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 2.0 was employed consistently throughout the catalytic tests.  
- **Observations:** This ratio was chosen to effectively moderate the hydrogenation process. As detailed in the article, increasing the H₂/CO ratio beyond the optimal value can lead to over-hydrogenation, resulting in higher methane selectivity. Thus, a ratio of 2.0 provided a strategic compromise, achieving favorable olefin yields while limiting excessive methane formation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity, typically presented as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), represents the ratio of the reactant flow rate to the catalyst mass, dictating the contact time between the gas phase and catalyst.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 4000 mL/(gcat·h) was maintained during the experiments.  
- **Observations:** Under these conditions, the catalyst displayed consistent CO conversion and stable product distribution over a 100-hour testing period, demonstrating that even at higher flow regimes, the catalyst maintains its activity and selectivity.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and evolution of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical X’Pert Pro) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are critical for identifying active phases and monitoring transformations during synthesis and reaction.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns reveal that after reaction, Fe@Si-c and FeMn@Si-c catalysts contain exclusively iron carbides, with no detectable iron oxides. In contrast, unmodified Fe@Si exhibits both iron carbides and Fe₃O₄ (magnetite). Mössbauer spectroscopy further confirms that FeMn@Si-c contains only iron carbide phases (notably χ-Fe₅C₂ and q-Fe₃C), while Fe@Si shows a mixture of carbides and Fe₃O₄. For Mn-promoted catalysts, Mn/Fe ratios ≤ 0.3 maintain pure carbide phases, whereas higher Mn content leads to partial Fe₃O₄ formation.
- **Observations:** The hydrophobic SiO₂ shell and controlled Mn promotion effectively suppress re-oxidation, ensuring the formation and stability of active iron carbide phases essential for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Core–Shell Structure
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HRTEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were employed to assess particle morphology, encapsulation integrity, and surface features.
- **Findings:** TEM and HRTEM images show that Fe₂O₃ nanorods are uniformly encapsulated by an amorphous SiO₂ shell, with the shell displaying micropores (0.3–2 nm). SEM confirms the nanorod morphology and reveals that moderate Mn addition preserves the core–shell structure, while excessive Mn causes aggregation and incomplete shell formation.
- **Observations:** The homogeneous SiO₂ encapsulation is crucial for hydrophobic modification and phase stability, while the presence of micropores facilitates reactant diffusion.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms (BET analysis) were used to evaluate surface area, pore size distribution, and porosity, which influence catalytic accessibility and performance.
- **Findings:** After SiO₂ encapsulation, the catalysts exhibit increased microporosity, with pore sizes predominantly in the 0.3–2 nm range. This is attributed to the formation of the SiO₂ shell.
- **Observations:** Enhanced microporosity supports improved reactant transport and provides additional active sites, contributing to higher catalytic efficiency.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Chemistry and Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS), and contact angle measurements were conducted to analyze surface functional groups and hydrophobicity.
- **Findings:** FTIR spectra display bands at 471, 800, and 1095 cm⁻¹ (Si–O–Si) confirming shell formation, and at 465 and 542 cm⁻¹ (Fe–O) for the core. After hydrophobic modification, new absorption at ~2925 cm⁻¹ appears, corresponding to –CH₃ groups. Contact angle measurements (JY-82B goniometer) show an increase from 40.2° (Fe@Si) to 137.6° (Fe@Si-c), indicating a transition from hydrophilic to highly hydrophobic surfaces.
- **Observations:** The successful grafting of –CH₃ groups and the resulting hydrophobicity are pivotal for suppressing water adsorption and related side reactions.

#### 6.1.5 Water Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** Water vapor adsorption/desorption measurements were performed to quantify the hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity of the catalyst surface.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si-c exhibits approximately 6.5 times lower water vapor adsorption at low humidity compared to Fe@Si, demonstrating effective suppression of water uptake.
- **Observations:** Reduced water adsorption minimizes water–gas shift activity and secondary CO₂ formation, directly benefiting olefin selectivity and catalyst stability.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively, determined by online gas chromatography.
- **Experimental Data:** Under optimal conditions (320 °C, 3.0 MPa, 4000 ml·h⁻¹·g⁻¹, H₂/CO = 2), FeMn@Si‑c achieves a CO conversion of 56.1%. The carbon balance during these tests is maintained between 96% and 98%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The introduction of a hydrophobic SiO₂ shell and Mn promotion significantly enhances CO conversion compared to unmodified or non-promoted catalysts. Mn/Fe ratios ≤ 0.3 are critical for maintaining high conversion and phase purity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction. It is defined as:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For FeMn@Si‑c, CO₂ selectivity is suppressed to as low as 13% under optimal conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic modification (contact angle increase from 40.2° to 137.6°) and Mn promotion both contribute to reduced CO₂ selectivity by inhibiting water adsorption and the WGS reaction. Excessive Mn (Mn/Fe > 0.3) increases CO₂ selectivity due to partial Fe₃O₄ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, a less desirable C₁ by-product. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The combined selectivity for CO₂ and CH₄ is maintained below 22.5% for FeMn@Si‑c, with CH₄ selectivity notably reduced compared to unmodified catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both hydrophobic encapsulation and Mn addition suppress CH₄ formation, favoring higher olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 Olefin Selectivity and Yield
- **Description:** Olefin selectivity is the percentage of converted CO forming olefinic hydrocarbons (C₂–C₄ and higher), while olefin yield represents the overall fraction of feed CO converted to olefins.
- **Experimental Data:** FeMn@Si‑c achieves an olefin selectivity of up to 83.3% (with 68.2% α-olefins) and an olefin yield of 36.6%. The olefin/paraffin molar ratio in C₂⁺ products reaches 5.0.
- **Observations and Trends:** The combination of hydrophobic surface and Mn promotion maximizes olefin production and selectivity, with minimal paraffin and C₁ by-product formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) plots, indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination:
\[
0 \leq \alpha \leq 1
\]
Higher α values favor longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** α increases from 0.41 (Fe@Si) to 0.53 (Fe@Si‑c) and further to 0.73 (FeMn@Si‑c).
- **Observations and Trends:** Both hydrophobic modification and Mn promotion synergistically enhance chain growth probability, correlating with increased olefin selectivity and longer hydrocarbon chains.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and product selectivity over extended operation. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** FeMn@Si‑c maintains stable CO conversion and product distribution over 100 hours on stream, with negligible deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The robust encapsulation and hydrophobic surface prevent water-induced deactivation, ensuring long-term catalyst performance.
