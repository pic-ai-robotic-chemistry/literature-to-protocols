## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the established coprecipitation method employed for synthesizing the Zn–Cr catalysts, encompassing each processing stage from precursor mixing to post‑synthesis annealing. The reported procedure offers a reproducible approach with clearly defined reaction conditions and equipment use, ensuring accurate control over catalyst composition.

#### Coprecipitation Method for Zn–Cr Catalysts

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Prepare precursor solution A by dissolving Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and Cr(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in deionized water, with the Zn/Cr ratio adjusted (e.g., 1:1, 1:3, 3:1, or 5:1) by regulating the amounts of each salt.  
   • Maintain vigorous stirring in a dedicated container at 60 °C; pH is controlled at 11 using a pH meter to ensure uniform conditions.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • Prepare solution B containing (NH₄)₂CO₃·CH₃NO₂ separately.  
   • Add solution B dropwise into solution A under continued violent stirring, ensuring thorough mixing and uniform precipitation, as the vigorous stirring promotes homogeneity across the reaction mixture.

3. **Settling and Washing**  
   • Allow the precipitate to settle at room temperature for 12 hours.  
   • Wash the sediment repeatedly with deionized water until the pH neutralizes at 7, removing residual impurities.

4. **Drying**  
   • Dry the washed precipitate at 120 °C for 12 hours to eliminate excess moisture, a step critical for achieving a consistent catalyst structure.

5. **Calcination**  
   • Calcine the dried material at 350 °C for 6 hours. This calcination step facilitates the formation of a nonstoichiometric Zn–Cr spinel phase while preserving surface area by preventing over‑sintering.

6. **Post‑treatment via Potassium Impregnation**  
   • Impregnate the calcined powder with 3.0 wt% potassium using a K₂CO₃ solution.  
   • Subject the impregnated catalyst to a second drying at 120 °C for 12 hours, followed by an annealing step at 400 °C for 6 hours to stabilize the catalyst structure and optimize active site dispersion.

This structured method—supported by precise reagent details, controlled mixing conditions, and well‐specified processing steps—ensures reproducibility in synthesizing catalysts tailored for enhanced Fischer–Tropsch performance, with critical control over the Zn/Cr element ratio and subsequent catalyst properties.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Zn1Cr1

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and Cr(NO₃)₃·9H₂O are dissolved in deionized water to form solution A. The exact quantities are adjusted to achieve a Zn/Cr ratio of 1:1. 
- **Conditions**: The solution is stirred in a violently stirred container maintained at 60 °C. The pH is adjusted and controlled at 11 by the dropwise addition of (NH₄)₂CO₃·CH₃NO₂ (solution B).
- **Process**:
  - Mix solution A (containing the metal nitrates) with solution B under vigorous stirring at 60 °C.
  - Ensure thorough coprecipitation through controlled pH (maintained at 11) and temperature throughout the reaction.
  - Allow the precipitate to form and settle at room temperature for approximately 12 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform mixing and strict control of pH and temperature are critical to achieving a homogeneous precipitate that eventually leads to the formation of a nonstoichiometric Zn–Cr spinel structure.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Process**:
  - Wash the precipitate thoroughly with deionized water until a neutral pH (pH 7) is reached, ensuring removal of residual ions.
  - Collect the precipitate by centrifugation or filtration as appropriate.
- **Key Steps**: Careful washing prevents impurities that could affect subsequent thermal treatments.

##### Step 3: Drying and Calcination
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the washed precipitate in an oven at 120 °C for 12 hours.
- **Calcination**:
  - Calcine the dried material at 350 °C for 6 hours, facilitating the formation of the Zn–Cr spinel phase.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform heat distribution and precise time control are vital for phase formation and catalyst homogeneity.

##### Step 4: Impregnation and Final Activation
- **Reagents**: Potassium is introduced using K₂CO₃ to impart 3.0 wt% promoter content.
- **Process**:
  - Impregnate the calcined catalyst by thoroughly mixing with the K₂CO₃ solution.
  - Dry the resultant material at 120 °C for 12 hours, followed by annealing at 400 °C for 6 hours to ensure proper potassium incorporation.
- **Key Steps**: The post-impregnation drying and annealing are crucial for enhancing the catalyst’s oxygen species stabilization.

The above procedure is adapted for varying Zn/Cr ratios (e.g., 1:3, 3:1, 5:1) by adjusting precursor amounts accordingly, thereby ensuring reproducibility and uniform catalyst properties across the Zn–Cr catalyst series.

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction temperature is a critical parameter, providing the thermal energy necessary for the activation of syngas components during the Fischer-Tropsch process. In this study, the reaction was conducted at 400 °C. Maintaining this temperature within a fixed-bed reactor ensures that CO and H₂ achieve proper activation, supporting the synthesis of isobutanol with optimal catalyst performance. This temperature was consistently monitored using calibrated thermocouples, which contributed to reliable reaction kinetics and reproducibility.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Operating at an elevated pressure plays a major role in enhancing reaction rates and shifting product equilibria. The experiments were performed at a reaction pressure of 10 MPa. This high-pressure condition increases the syngas density and improves the contact between the gas and catalyst, which in turn favors the formation of higher alcohols. Although the direct link between pressure and selectivity is not deeply elaborated, operating at 10 MPa is justified based on the improved conversion efficiency and product yields observed.

In practice, this ratio contributes to a favorable hydrocarbon distribution and has been correlated with enhanced isobutanol selectivity in the Zn–Cr catalyst system.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity

A GHSV of 3000 h⁻¹ was employed, ensuring balanced exposure that supports efficient CO conversion while maintaining catalyst integrity over the test period. This parameter was precisely controlled by adjusting the gas flow rates, allowing for consistent and reproducible measurements over an 8-hour time-on-stream period.

Collectively, these reaction parameters—400 °C, 10 MPa, an H₂/CO ratio of 2.6, and a GHSV of 3000 h⁻¹—were rigorously implemented in a fixed-bed reactor setup. The conditions ensured high CO conversion and isobutanol selectivity, underscoring their critical role in the successful catalytic evaluation of the Zn–Cr catalyst series.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** Crystal phase composition and structural features were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 diffractometer (Cu Kα, 40 kV, 100 mA, 2θ = 10–80°). This analysis distinguishes between spinel, ZnO, and Cr₂O₃ phases, which are critical for catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** The Zn1Cr1 catalyst (Zn/Cr = 1:1) exhibited only a nonstoichiometric Zn–Cr spinel phase, with no detectable ZnO peaks, indicating that excess Zn is incorporated into the spinel lattice or highly dispersed. In contrast, catalysts with higher Zn/Cr ratios (Zn3Cr1, Zn5Cr1) showed distinct and sharper ZnO reflections, signifying the formation and growth of discrete ZnO particles.
- **Observations:** The absence of ZnO peaks in Zn1Cr1 suggests optimal incorporation of Zn, forming a defect-rich, metastable spinel structure, which is beneficial for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM/HRTEM, JEM-2100F, 200 kV) was used to assess particle size and morphology, which influence surface area and active site accessibility.
- **Findings:** Spinel catalysts (Zn1Cr1, Zn1Cr3) displayed uniform, small particles (<10 nm), while ZnO particles in Zn3Cr1 and Zn5Cr1 exceeded 20 nm. Increasing Zn/Cr ratio led to larger ZnO domains and less uniformity.
- **Observations:** The small, well-dispersed spinel particles in Zn1Cr1 maximize active surface area, whereas larger ZnO particles in high-Zn catalysts may block active sites.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption–desorption (BET, ASAP-2400) provided surface area (SBET), pore volume (Vp), and pore radius (Rp), which are vital for reactant diffusion and catalytic turnover.
- **Findings:** Zn1Cr1 exhibited the highest surface area (101.2 m²/g), pore volume (0.536 cm³/g), and pore radius (14.4 nm) among the series.
- **Observations:** The mesoporous structure and high surface area of Zn1Cr1 facilitate efficient diffusion and adsorption of syngas components, enhancing catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility and Defect Structure
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR, TP-5050) assessed catalyst reducibility, reflecting defect and oxygen vacancy concentrations.
- **Findings:** Zn1Cr1 showed a single reduction peak with the highest H₂ consumption (1.53 mmol/g), indicating abundant reducible sites and defects.
- **Observations:** Enhanced reducibility in Zn1Cr1 is attributed to its nonstoichiometric, defect-rich spinel structure, which promotes hydrogen activation and catalytic efficiency.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition and Valence States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS ULTRA DLD, Al Kα, 2×10⁻⁸ Pa) determined surface elemental composition and oxidation states.
- **Findings:** Zn²⁺ and Cr³⁺ in Zn1Cr1 exhibited higher binding energies (Zn 2p₃/₂: 1022.2 eV; Cr 2p₃/₂: 576.3 eV) than in ZnO or Cr₂O₃, consistent with increased defect and oxygen vacancy levels. Minor Cr⁶⁺ species were also detected.
- **Observations:** Elevated binding energies confirm the presence of a nonstoichiometric spinel with significant surface disorder, supporting enhanced catalytic properties.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Basicity and Ion Distribution
- **Description:** CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) and synchrotron-based XANES/EXAFS probed surface basicity and cation distribution.
- **Findings:** Zn1Cr1 displayed the highest CO₂ uptake, indicating maximal surface basicity. XANES/EXAFS revealed Zn migration from tetrahedral to octahedral sites and pronounced disorder, confirming nonstoichiometric spinel formation.
- **Observations:** Increased basicity and cation disorder in Zn1Cr1 correlate with improved CO adsorption/activation and isobutanol selectivity.

Overall, the comprehensive characterization demonstrates that the Zn1Cr1 catalyst possesses a highly defective, nonstoichiometric spinel structure with optimal surface area, porosity, reducibility, and basicity—attributes directly linked to its superior performance in isobutanol synthesis from syngas.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed into products during the reaction. It is typically calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (400 °C, 10 MPa, GHSV = 3000 h⁻¹, TOS = 8 h), the CO conversion values for the Zn–Cr catalyst series are:
  - Zn1Cr1 (Zn/Cr = 1:1): 32.6%
  - Zn1Cr3 (1:3): 11.3%
  - Zn3Cr1 (3:1): 29.6%
  - Zn5Cr1 (5:1): 19.3%
- **Observations and Trends:** The Zn1Cr1 catalyst exhibits the highest CO conversion, indicating an optimal Zn/Cr ratio for syngas activation. Both lower (1:3) and higher (3:1, 5:1) Zn/Cr ratios result in reduced conversion, correlating with less favorable phase composition and textural properties.

#### 6.2.2 Isobutanol Selectivity
- **Description:** Isobutanol selectivity is defined as the weight percentage of isobutanol in the alcohol product phase, reflecting the catalyst’s ability to direct carbon flux toward higher alcohols.
- **Experimental Data:** Isobutanol selectivity values are:
  - Zn1Cr1: 24.1%
  - Zn1Cr3: 14.2%
  - Zn3Cr1: 16.5%
  - Zn5Cr1: 11.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** The Zn1Cr1 catalyst achieves the highest isobutanol selectivity, with methanol and isobutanol together comprising up to ~95% of the alcohol phase. Selectivity decreases as the Zn/Cr ratio deviates from 1:1, highlighting the importance of the nonstoichiometric spinel phase and optimal basicity.

#### 6.2.3 Space Time Yield (STY) of Alcohols
- **Description:** STY measures the mass of alcohols produced per unit catalyst volume per hour, providing a direct indicator of catalyst productivity.
- **Experimental Data:** While specific STY values are not detailed in the main text, the performance evaluation is standardized at TOS = 8 h, ensuring comparability across catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest STY is associated with the Zn1Cr1 catalyst, consistent with its superior CO conversion and isobutanol selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring performance metrics (CO conversion, selectivity) over the 8-hour time-on-stream (TOS) period.
- **Experimental Data:** All reported performance values are measured after 8 h TOS. The carbon balance remains within 96–105%, indicating reliable operation and minimal deactivation during this period.
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium-promoted Zn–Cr catalysts demonstrate stable activity and selectivity over the test duration, with no significant deactivation observed.

#### 6.2.5 Correlation with Structural Properties
- **Description:** The relationship between catalyst structure (phase composition, surface area, basicity, reducibility) and performance is established through comprehensive characterization.
- **Experimental Data:** Zn1Cr1 features the highest BET surface area (101.2 m²/g), largest pore volume (0.536 cm³/g), and greatest H₂ consumption (1.53 mmol/g), indicative of abundant active sites and oxygen vacancies.
- **Observations and Trends:** Superior catalytic performance is directly linked to the formation of a nonstoichiometric Zn–Cr spinel phase, enhanced basicity, and improved reducibility, all of which are maximized at a Zn/Cr ratio of 1:1.
