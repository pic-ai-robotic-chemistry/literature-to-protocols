## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis methods used for the K/Fe−Cu−Al catalyst, focusing on reproducibility and precision. The synthesis involves a two‐stage process: continuous co‑precipitation to form the Fe−Cu−Al precursor, followed by incipient wetness impregnation to incorporate potassium.

#### Continuous Co‑precipitation Method

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   – Metal nitrate solutions (Fe(NO₃)₃, Cu(NO₃)₂, and Al nitrate) are mixed in an aqueous medium.  
   – Ammonium hydroxide is added dropwise to achieve a controlled pH of 7.0.  
   – Equipment such as a calibrated pH meter and a mixing apparatus assures uniform reagent distribution.  

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   – The addition of ammonium hydroxide induces the formation of a homogeneous precipitate.  
   – Observations include the gradual formation of a solid phase, setting the stage for subsequent purification.

3. • Drying  
   – Following filtration and thorough washing to remove soluble impurities, the precipitate is dried at 110 °C overnight.  

4. • Calcination  
   – The dried precursor is calcined at 450 °C for 5 hours to achieve proper phase development and precursor purification.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation Method

1. • Impregnation  
   – The calcined Fe−Cu−Al material is impregnated with an aqueous solution of potassium carbonate using the incipient wetness technique.  
   – This step ensures the potassium is uniformly dispersed, critical for achieving the designated K/Fe mass ratio of 0.04.

2. • Post‑Impregnation Drying and Calcination  
   – The impregnated material is dried again at 110 °C overnight.  
   – A second calcination step is conducted at 450 °C for 5 hours to integrate potassium into the catalyst structure properly.

3. • Sieving  
   – The final catalyst is sieved to obtain particles within the target size range of 2–2.4 mm.

### Comparative Notes on Methods

• The continuous co‑precipitation method is straightforward, enabling enhanced dispersion of metal precursors with controlled nucleation, cost‐effectiveness, and scalability.  
• The incipient wetness technique, while adding a step, offers superior incorporation of potassium and consistent particle morphology, beneficial for catalytic activity and long‐term stability.  

This detailed synthesis scheme, with precisely documented conditions and equipment, ensures that the catalyst preparation is both reproducible and reliable for Fischer–Tropsch applications.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structural Evolution
- **Description:** The crystal phases present in the K/Fe−Cu−Al catalyst were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for determining the active and support phases that influence Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the fresh catalyst revealed two broad peaks at 2θ = 33.5° and 36.0°, corresponding to hematite (JCPDS 33-0664) and magnetite (JCPDS 39-1346), respectively. After FTS reaction, new peaks appeared at 2θ = 40.7° and 43.0°, assigned to cementite (iron carbide, JCPDS 39-1346). The FexC/Fe3O4 ratio, calculated from the integrated intensities of the 43.0° (carbide) and 36.0° (magnetite) peaks, decreased with increasing CO₂/(CO + CO₂) ratio and higher pressure.
- **Observations:** The emergence and intensity of cementite peaks indicate the formation of active iron carbide phases during reaction, which are critical for hydrocarbon synthesis. Lower FexC/Fe3O4 ratios at higher CO₂ content and pressure suggest suppressed carbide formation, correlating with reduced carbon deposition and enhanced catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.2 Carbide Formation and Quantification
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed decarburization (TPDC) was employed to monitor the formation and reactivity of iron carbides, providing insight into the catalyst’s active phase evolution under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** TPDC profiles exhibited distinct peaks at ~420 °C (surface carbides) and ~600 °C (bulk-phase carbides). The extent of carbide formation was quantified by integrating the areas under these peaks, with results showing a decrease in total carbide content as the CO₂/(CO + CO₂) ratio and reaction pressure increased.
- **Observations:** The quantitative reduction in carbide formation under CO₂-rich and high-pressure conditions aligns with XRD findings and is associated with improved catalyst longevity due to diminished carbon buildup.

#### 6.1.3 Carbon Deposition and Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and elemental analysis were used to assess carbon deposition and the nature of carbon species on the catalyst post-reaction.
- **Findings:** TGA, conducted by heating samples to 1000 °C at 10 °C/min under air, showed lower weight loss from carbonaceous materials at 20 bar compared to 10 bar, indicating reduced carbon deposition at higher pressure. Elemental analysis (Thermo Scientific Flash 2000) determined H/C ratios below 0.2 for all used catalysts, signifying that deposited carbon was predominantly in the form of carbide. All samples were pre-washed with cyclohexane to remove hydrocarbons, ensuring accurate quantification.
- **Observations:** The low H/C ratios and reduced TGA weight loss at elevated pressures confirm that optimized reaction conditions suppress excessive carbon formation, supporting catalyst stability during extended operation.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Verification of Catalyst Composition
- **Description:** The final catalyst composition was verified by elemental analysis to confirm the intended ratios of Fe, Cu, Al, and K after synthesis and reaction.
- **Findings:** Elemental analysis results (as summarized in Table 1 of the article) confirmed the target composition of 4K/100Fe−6Cu−16Al, with consistent elemental ratios observed across samples subjected to various reaction conditions.
- **Observations:** The reproducibility of the catalyst’s elemental composition across multiple runs validates the synthesis protocol and supports the reliability of the observed catalytic behavior.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The K/Fe−Cu−Al catalyst achieved CO conversions exceeding 95% under most conditions, particularly when the CO₂/(CO + CO₂) ratio was between 0 and 0.25. At higher CO₂/(CO + CO₂) ratios (0.5–0.75), CO conversion decreased below 95% due to increased CO generation via the reverse water–gas shift (RWGS) reaction. The highest conversions were observed at 300 °C and 20 bar, with a space velocity of 1800 mL NTP g⁻¹ catalyst h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** Elevated temperature and pressure enhanced CO conversion, while increasing the CO₂ fraction in the feed led to a moderate decline in conversion, attributed to RWGS activity.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted carbon appearing as methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity remained low (around 10%) across all tested conditions. At 320 °C and 10 bar, CH₄ selectivity was higher but decreased as the CO₂/(CO + CO₂) ratio increased.
- **Observations and Trends:** The iron-based catalyst, especially with potassium promotion, suppressed methane formation. Increasing CO₂ content and temperature further reduced CH₄ selectivity, a trend attributed to water inhibition of hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted carbon forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The highest C₅⁺ selectivity was observed at 300 °C and 20 bar, particularly as the CO₂/(CO + CO₂) ratio increased. At 320 °C, C₅⁺ selectivity continued to rise with higher CO₂ content, while CH₄ selectivity decreased.
- **Observations and Trends:** High pressure and moderate to high temperature favored chain growth, resulting in increased long-chain hydrocarbon production. Enhanced water formation from RWGS at higher CO₂ ratios promoted this effect.

#### 6.2.4 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and product selectivity over time, with deactivation inferred from declines in these metrics.
- **Experimental Data:** Over 40 hours on-stream, the catalyst maintained high CO conversion (>95%) and stable selectivity profiles. Carbon deposition, as measured by TGA and elemental analysis, decreased with increasing CO₂/(CO + CO₂) ratio and at higher pressure (20 bar).
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibited strong resistance to deactivation, with minimal performance loss over extended operation. Reduced carbon deposition at high CO₂ ratios and pressure contributed to sustained activity.
