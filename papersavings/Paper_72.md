## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the impregnation procedure used for the Co/TiO₂ (10 wt.% Co) catalyst synthesis, emphasizing reproducibility, precision, and scalability.

#### Impregnation Method for Co/TiO₂ Catalyst

1. Catalyst Support Preparation  
   • Mix TiO₂ (Degussa P25) with distilled water in a 1:1 mass ratio to ensure homogeneous dispersion.  
   • Dry the mixture in air at 120°C for 1 hour to remove residual moisture.  
   • Calcine the dried support in air at 400°C for 16 hours, ensuring complete thermal treatment and structural stabilization.  
   • Crush and sieve the calcined support to achieve a particle size range of 0.5–1.0 mm, critical for uniform impregnation and subsequent performance.

2. Precursor Incorporation via Impregnation  
   • Prepare a cobalt nitrate (Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) solution with precise concentration to achieve a 10 wt.% cobalt loading.  
   • Impregnate the pretreated TiO₂ support by thoroughly mixing with the cobalt nitrate solution. The controlled addition guarantees uniform dispersion of cobalt across the support.  
   • Note: Although the "Precipitation–Deposition" step is applicable in other methods, for this procedure the impregnation directly follows support preparation.

3. Post‑processing Steps  
   • Dry the impregnated material in air at 120°C for 16 hours. This drying step is essential to remove excess moisture and prevent agglomeration.  
   • Calcine the dried catalyst in air at 400°C for 6 hours. This final calcination converts cobalt nitrate into the active cobalt oxide phase, ensuring the catalyst is ready for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

Equipment such as fixed-bed reactor systems, drying and calcination furnaces, and standard crushing and sieving apparatus are employed throughout the process. ### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet molar flow rates of CO, respectively, determined using N₂ as an internal standard.
- **Experimental Data:** Under CO/H₂/N₂ (30%/60%/10%) at 20 bar and 180–220 °C, CO conversion remained stable at 180 °C and 200 °C. At 210 °C and 220 °C, a slight decline in CO conversion was observed over time, indicating mild deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher temperatures initially increased CO conversion, but stability decreased above 200 °C due to water-induced re-oxidation of cobalt. No significant deactivation was observed at lower temperatures or during CO₂ hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the percentage of converted CO (or CO₂) forming methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For CO₂ hydrogenation, CH₄ selectivity ranged from 87% to 95% as temperature increased from 180 °C to 220 °C, with the highest value at 180 °C. For CO hydrogenation, CH₄ selectivity was lower (approx. 8–12%) and increased with temperature, except for a reduction at 210 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ hydrogenation favored methane formation, while CO hydrogenation produced more higher hydrocarbons. CH₄ formation rates were highest during the initial CO₂ run and decreased after switching feeds.

#### 6.2.3 C₂⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₂⁺ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO (or CO₂) yielding hydrocarbons with two or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_2^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\sum F_{\text{C}_n,\text{out}} (n \geq 2)}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For CO₂ hydrogenation, C₂⁺ selectivity was 5–13%, increasing with temperature. CO hydrogenation yielded higher C₂⁺ selectivity and a broader hydrocarbon distribution.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher temperatures promoted longer-chain hydrocarbon formation, especially under CO feed. The presence of a liquid phase (waxes/oils) during CO hydrogenation further increased C₂⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, indicates the probability of chain growth:
  - \( \alpha \) close to 1 favors long-chain hydrocarbons; lower values favor methane.
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ hydrogenation resulted in a lower α (methane-rich products), while CO hydrogenation produced a higher α, favoring longer chains.
- **Observations and Trends:** Switching from CO₂ to CO feed increased α, correlating with the formation of waxes and oils. The α value stabilized after repeated feed switching.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Mild deactivation was observed at 210–220 °C during CO hydrogenation, evidenced by a gradual decrease in CO conversion and paraffin formation rates over 1600–2700 h on stream. No significant deactivation occurred at 180–200 °C or during CO₂ hydrogenation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Deactivation was attributed to cobalt re-oxidation by water, not carbon deposition. The catalyst remained stable under lower temperature and CO₂-rich conditions.

#### 6.2.6 Olefin/Paraffin (O/P) Ratio
- **Description:** The O/P ratio reflects the relative formation rates of olefins to paraffins, indicating secondary hydrogenation activity.
- **Experimental Data:** The O/P ratio peaked during initial CO hydrogenation after switching from CO₂, then stabilized with further switching cycles.
- **Observations and Trends:** Accumulation of liquid products increased the H/C ratio on the catalyst surface, promoting olefin hydrogenation and affecting the O/P ratio and overall selectivity.
