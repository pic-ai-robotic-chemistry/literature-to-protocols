## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalysts (Co10-c, Fe10-c, CoFe10/1-c, and CoFe10/5-c) were synthesized via a wetness impregnation method that ensures reproducible incorporation of metal nitrates onto a high-surface-area silica (Grace Davison, ~310 m²/g). The procedure was designed for accurate control of metal loading and uniform dispersion without a separate precipitation–deposition step.

#### Wetness Impregnation Method
1. • Precursor Mixing  
  – Dissolve cobalt nitrate (Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) in deionized water to achieve a nominal loading of 10 wt% for the monometallic Co10-c catalyst.  
  – For bimetallic catalysts (CoFe10/1-c and CoFe10/5-c), prepare an aqueous solution containing both cobalt nitrate and iron nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O) with a fixed cobalt amount (10 wt%) and iron doping at 1 wt% or 5 wt%, respectively.  
  – The metal precursors are mixed uniformly with the silica support ensuring homogeneous precursor distribution.

2. • (Precipitation–Deposition)  
  – This step is not separately employed as the wetness impregnation method allows direct mixing of metal salts with the support.

3. • Drying  
  – The impregnated catalyst is dried in a controlled oven at 393 K overnight.  
  – This step removes moisture to prevent agglomeration, ensuring effective precursor anchoring on the silica matrix.

4. • Calcination  
  – The dried sample is calcined at 773 K for 2 h under an airflow atmosphere.  
  – This high-temperature treatment decomposes the nitrate salts to form corresponding oxides (Co₃O₄ and Fe₂O₃), as confirmed by subsequent XRD analysis.

5. • Reduction  
  – Prior to catalytic testing, the calcined catalyst undergoes a reduction treatment at 773 K for 2 h under a gas mixture composed of H₂ (20%) and N₂ (80%) at a flow rate of 100 ml/min.  
  – Reduction converts the oxide species to metallic Co⁰ (and aids in forming a Co–Fe alloy for the bimetallic systems), which is essential for activating the catalysts in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This method, through its precise control of reagent concentrations, temperatures, and durations, enables a reproducible synthesis that fosters uniform metal dispersion and facilitates reliable catalyst performance in both fixed-bed and slurry reactor studies.

- **Reagents**:  
  - Prepared cobalt nitrate solution  
  - High-surface-area silica support  
- **Conditions**:

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dry the impregnated sample in an oven at 393 K overnight.  
  2. Calcine the dried sample in a tubular furnace at 773 K under an airflow for 2 hours to develop the active oxide phase.

  1. Reduce the calcined catalyst under a H₂ (20%)/N₂ (80%) atmosphere at 773 K for 2 hours to obtain the metallic cobalt phase.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pre-reaction treatment designed to convert the metal oxide precursors into their active metallic forms. This reduction step ensures that the catalyst, whether monometallic or bimetallic, exhibits enhanced metal dispersion and an optimal active site structure for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential for providing the thermal energy required to drive the reduction reaction efficiently. It helps in breaking the metal–oxygen bonds in the precursor, thereby exposing the active sites.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at an exact temperature of 773 K.
- **Observations:** This elevated temperature is critical for complete reduction, leading to improved metal dispersion and alloy formation (in bimetallic systems), which are directly linked to enhanced catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Activation time refers to the period during which the catalyst is exposed to the reducing environment. This duration ensures that the reduction process reaches completion without causing significant sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is carried out for 2 hours at 773 K.
- **Observations:** A 2-hour activation period provides sufficient time for the full transformation of the oxidized precursors into their active metallic states, resulting in a catalyst with high activity and stability under reaction conditions.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition and Flow
- **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas is vital for controlling the reduction kinetics; a balanced mixture ensures controlled reduction without excessive aggressiveness.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A gas mixture consisting of 20% H₂ and 80% N₂ is used, delivered at a flow rate of 100 mlN/min.
- **Observations:** The diluted hydrogen environment provided by this mixture facilitates a controlled reduction process. It effectively converts the catalyst precursor to its metallic form while mitigating risks of sintering or structural deterioration, thereby promoting superior catalyst performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** Crystal phases and structural transformations were identified using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Seifert 3000 P diffractometer (Cu Kα1, λ = 0.15406 nm, 2θ = 5–80°, step size 0.028°). This analysis distinguishes between oxide, metallic, and alloy phases in both calcined and hydrogen-reduced catalysts, which is critical for understanding active site formation in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** Calcined Co10-c exhibited Co₃O₄ (crystallite size 21 nm), while Fe10-c showed Fe₂O₃ (18 nm). Bimetallic calcined samples (CoFe10/1-c and CoFe10/5-c) displayed separate Co₃O₄ and Fe₂O₃ phases (14–17 nm), with no evidence of mixed oxides. After hydrogen reduction (H₂/N₂, 773 K, 2 h), monometallic catalysts formed metallic Co⁰ (Co10-c, 17 nm) or Fe⁰ (Fe10-c, 22 nm). Bimetallic reduced catalysts contained both Co⁰ (10 nm) and a Co–Fe alloy phase, with alloy crystallite size increasing from 11 nm (CoFe10/1-c) to 23 nm (CoFe10/5-c) as iron content increased.
- **Observations:** The absence of mixed oxide peaks in bimetallic calcined samples and the emergence of a Co–Fe alloy upon reduction indicate that iron promotes alloy formation, which is more pronounced at higher Fe loadings.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to assess particle size, dispersion, and morphological features, which directly influence catalyst activity and stability.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed that Co10-c contained relatively large, poorly dispersed cobalt particles, a result of the wetness impregnation and calcination process. In contrast, bimetallic catalysts exhibited smaller cobalt particles and improved dispersion due to alloy formation. For the spent Fe10-c catalyst, TEM identified helical carbon filaments, indicative of carbon deposition.
- **Observations:** Enhanced dispersion in bimetallic catalysts is attributed to iron-induced alloying, which inhibits cobalt particle growth. The presence of helical carbon filaments on Fe10-c correlates with rapid deactivation via CO disproportionation.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Composition and Metal Dispersion
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to evaluate the surface composition and dispersion of cobalt and iron species, with binding energies referenced to the C1s peak at 284.9 eV.
- **Findings:** XPS analysis showed low dispersion of cobalt metal particles in Co10-c, consistent with its lower catalytic activity. In bimetallic catalysts, the formation of a Co–Fe alloy led to increased dispersion of the active metal phase, as inferred from higher CO conversion rates.
- **Observations:** The improved surface dispersion in bimetallic systems is directly linked to alloy formation, which enhances catalytic performance compared to the monometallic cobalt catalyst.

#### 6.1.4 Iron Species and Alloy Formation
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy provided detailed identification of iron species and alloying effects, crucial for understanding the role of iron in catalyst structure.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra confirmed the presence of Fe₂O₃ (including nanosized Fe₂O₃) in calcined samples and the formation of a Co–Fe alloy in bimetallic catalysts after reduction.
- **Observations:** The detection of nanosized Fe₂O₃ and alloy phases supports the XRD and TEM findings, highlighting the structural evolution induced by iron doping and reduction treatment.

#### 6.1.5 Catalyst Activation and Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) studies informed the reduction protocol, ensuring optimal activation of the catalysts.
- **Findings:** Catalysts were reduced in H₂ (20%)/N₂ (80%) at 773 K for 2 hours, a procedure selected based on prior TPR results to achieve complete reduction and alloy formation.
- **Observations:** The standardized reduction conditions facilitated the reproducible formation of metallic and alloy phases, critical for consistent catalytic performance.





### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed by the catalyst, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** In fixed-bed reactors (20 bar, H₂/CO = 2:1, 493–533 K), Fe10-c exhibited the highest CO conversion at lower temperatures, while Co10-c showed the lowest conversion due to poor dispersion. Bimetallic CoFe10/1-c and CoFe10/5-c catalysts demonstrated enhanced conversion, with CoFe10/5-c reaching approximately 60% at 533 K. In slurry reactors (503 K, 4.0 MPa), Co10-c achieved 28% conversion, while Fe10-c reached 10% before rapid deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Fe to cobalt (especially at 1 wt%) significantly increases CO conversion, attributed to Co–Fe alloy formation and improved metal dispersion. Higher Fe content (5 wt%) further enhances conversion at elevated temperatures.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** While specific numerical values are not provided, iron-containing catalysts display higher CO₂ selectivity due to increased water gas shift activity, whereas cobalt catalysts exhibit lower CO₂ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of iron promotes CO₂ formation, with selectivity increasing alongside Fe content in bimetallic systems.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** In slurry reactors, all catalysts predominantly produce methane, with selectivity increasing upon Fe addition. Bimetallic catalysts show higher methane selectivity than monometallic Co10-c.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Fe content shifts selectivity toward methane and light hydrocarbons, reducing the formation of heavier products.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Monometallic Co10-c shows the highest C₃⁺ selectivity (C₅⁺ up to 65%), while bimetallic and Fe10-c catalysts exhibit lower values, favoring lighter hydrocarbons and alcohols.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Fe content diminishes C₃⁺ selectivity, correlating with enhanced methane and light hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, indicates the probability of chain growth:
  - Values near 1 favor long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** Co10-c exhibits a high α value of 0.85, consistent with its strong selectivity for heavier hydrocarbons. Bimetallic and Fe-rich catalysts display lower α values, reflecting suppressed chain growth.
- **Observations and Trends:** Alloying with Fe reduces α, shifting product distribution toward lighter hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** In slurry reactors, Fe10-c deactivates rapidly (complete loss of activity within 9 h), attributed to carbonaceous deposits. Co10-c maintains activity for at least 25 h, while bimetallic catalysts show intermediate stability.
- **Observations and Trends:** Iron-rich catalysts are prone to rapid deactivation due to carbon buildup, whereas cobalt-based and bimetallic systems exhibit greater stability under both fixed-bed and slurry conditions.
