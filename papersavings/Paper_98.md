## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article details two synthesis routes for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts. One route employs a one‑pot, solvent‑deficient method for the silica‑doped alumina support (AlSi), followed by nonaqueous wet impregnation, while the other route (for unsupported FeCuKSi catalysts) utilizes a precipitation–deposition approach.

#### • Silica‑Doped Alumina Support and Catalyst (40Fe/AlSi)
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
 • Combine aluminum isopropoxide (AIP) with water in a 5:1 molar ratio.  
 • Add tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) with additional water in a 1:2 molar ratio to achieve 5 wt% silica.  
 • Stir the mixture in a Bosch mixer for 30 minutes to ensure homogeneity.
2. Thermal Treatment of Support  
 • Heat the stirred mixture to 700 °C in air using a ramp rate of approximately 2.5 °C/min for 2 hours to form γ‑alumina.  
 • Cool the intermediate product, then heat to 1100 °C for 2 hours to enhance thermal stability and remove surface OH groups.
3. Catalyst Impregnation via Nonaqueous Wet Impregnation  
 • Dissolve ferric nitrate and copper nitrate (targeting 10 wt% Fe with suitable Cu addition) in a solvent mixture of 50% isopropanol/50% acetone.  
 • Impregnate the prepared support using a rotary evaporator with 12 hours of mixing.
4. Drying and Calcination  
 • Perform vacuum drying at 50 °C for 12 hours followed by air drying at 80 °C overnight.  
 • Calcine the dried samples at 300 °C for 16 hours.
5. Final Post‑treatment  
 • Introduce potassium bicarbonate via incipient wetness impregnation to complete the catalyst formulation.

#### • Unsupported FeCuKSi Catalyst by Precipitation–Deposition
• Conduct a co‑precipitation of iron nitrate, copper nitrate, and potassium bicarbonate together with a silica precursor under solvent‑deficient conditions.  
• Follow with controlled drying and calcination to achieve uniform precursor deposition.  

This comparative outline emphasizes reproducibility, detailing reagents, molar ratios, operational temperatures, durations, and specific equipment such as the Bosch mixer and rotary evaporator—ensuring clarity for replication while addressing synthesis complexity, scalability, and equipment requisites.

### 3.2 Detailed Synthesis Process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 40Fe/AlSi

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Silica-Doped Alumina Support (AlSi)
- **Reagents**:  
  • Aluminum isopropoxide (AIP)  
  • Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS)  
  • Deionized water  
- **Conditions**:  
  • AIP is mixed with water at a molar ratio of 5:1.  
  • TEOS is added with additional water at a 1:2 molar ratio, yielding 5 wt % silica.  
  • The mixture is stirred for 30 minutes in a Bosch mixer.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve AIP in deionized water (5:1 molar ratio) in a reaction vessel.  
  2. Introduce TEOS with the extra water (1:2 molar ratio) to ensure uniform silica distribution.  
  3. Stir the mixture for 30 minutes to achieve a homogeneous precursor solution.  
  4. Thermally treat the mixture in air by heating to 700 °C at a ramp rate of approximately 2.5 °C/min for 2 hours to form γ‐alumina.  
  5. Further heat the support to 1100 °C over 5 hours and hold for 2 hours to enhance thermal stability.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly – Metal Impregnation
- **Reagents**:  
  • Ferric nitrate (for iron phase)  
  • Copper nitrate (promoter)  
  • Solvent mixture: 50% isopropanol and 50% acetone  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Non-aqueous wet impregnation conducted in a rotary evaporator.  
  • Use a volume corresponding to 10% above incipient wetness to ensure uniform metal distribution.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve ferric and copper nitrates in the solvent mixture.  
  2. Impregnate the thermally treated AlSi support by mixing under rotary evaporation for 12 hours.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Purification**:  
  • Post-impregnation, slowly dry the samples in a vacuum at 50 °C for 12 hours, then air dry at 80 °C overnight.
- **Calcination**:  
  • Calcine the dried samples at 300 °C for 16 hours in air.
- **Note**: Following calcination, potassium bicarbonate is added via incipient wetness impregnation for texturization and additional promotion.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Conditions**:  
  • In situ reduction is performed by treating the catalyst in 10% H₂/He at 280–320 °C for 10 hours, followed by further reduction in 100% H₂ for 6 hours.
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Precise control of temperature ramps and solvent ratios is critical for reproducibility.  
  • Ensure that all primary precursors are fully prepared and characterized before proceeding to subsequent assembly and activation stages.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is an essential pre-reaction treatment designed to fully reduce the iron oxide phases and form the active metallic and carbide sites required for effective Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
Activation involves two temperature-controlled stages. Initially, the catalyst undergoes in situ reduction at a temperature range of 280–320 °C. This controlled temperature is critical to gradually remove oxygen from the precursor oxides, minimizing risks of rapid over-reduction or sintering. Following this, a subsequent activation is performed at 280 °C within the fixed-bed reactor. Maintaining this lower, consistent temperature during the reaction activation allows a gradual carburization process, thereby promoting the formation and stabilization of the active iron carbide phase.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
The gas environments are selected to optimize reduction and subsequent activation. During the first stage, the catalyst is exposed to a 10% H₂/He atmosphere. This diluted hydrogen environment ensures a gentle reduction of iron oxide, promoting uniform exposure of active sites while avoiding excessive sintering. In the second reduction step, the catalyst is treated with 100% H₂ for 6 hours to ensure complete oxygen removal and to prepare the surface for carburization during the reaction activation phase. The subsequent reaction activation is carried out under a syngas environment (H₂/CO with a 1:1 ratio), which is crucial for initiating controlled carburization.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
Time is a key parameter in both reduction and activation processes. The initial in situ reduction in 10% H₂/He is conducted for 10 hours, providing sufficient duration for uniform reduction across all active sites. This is followed by a 6-hour treatment with 100% H₂. Thereafter, the catalyst is activated at 280 °C in the fixed-bed reactor for a prolonged period of 48–90 hours. This extensive activation time promotes gradual formation of active iron carbide, ensuring enhanced long-term catalytic performance without compromising stability.

While detailed pressure and space‐time velocity parameters for the activation stages are not explicitly provided, these steps are clearly delineated from characterization techniques to ensure reproducibility in Fischer–Tropsch catalyst preparation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Panalytical X'Pert Pro diffractometer (Cu Kα, λ = 0.15418 nm). XRD is essential for identifying active phases and monitoring phase transformations during synthesis and activation.
- **Findings:** For the 40Fe/AlSi catalyst, XRD analysis revealed that the support retains the γ-alumina phase up to 1100 °C, with the Si–Al spinel structure present on the surface. After reduction and carburization, the active Fe₅C₂ phase is detected, with crystallite sizes of 7.9 nm (Fe₅C₂), 4.4 nm (Fe₃O₄), and 9.4 nm (Fe⁰). The 40Fe/AlG catalyst exhibits similar Fe₅C₂ crystallite size (8.1 nm) but larger Fe₃O₄ (6.4 nm) and Fe⁰ (11.6 nm) domains. The unsupported FeCuKSi catalyst shows significantly larger crystallites (Fe₃O₄: 20.8 nm, Fe⁰: 26 nm).
- **Observations:** The formation of Fe₅C₂ as the dominant phase after activation is directly linked to high Fischer–Tropsch activity. The small crystallite sizes in 40Fe/AlSi indicate effective dispersion and resistance to sintering, contributing to long-term catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption–desorption (BET) measurements (Micromeritics Tristar 3020) were used to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which are critical for metal dispersion and mass transfer.
- **Findings:** The AlSi support exhibits a high BET surface area of 184 m²/g and a large pore volume of 1.06 cm³/g after calcination at 1100 °C. The pore size distribution is bimodal, with peaks at 22.5 nm and 52 nm. After Fe loading, 40Fe/AlSi retains a pore volume of 0.64 cm³/g, while 40Fe/AlG shows a lower value (0.46 cm³/g).
- **Observations:** The unique bimodal porosity and high surface area of AlSi enable high Fe loading without pore blockage, facilitating efficient reactant diffusion and supporting superior catalytic performance and stability.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction and Carburization Behavior
- **Description:** Syngas temperature-programmed reduction (syngas-TPR) experiments (Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC 1 with GC 200 controller) were conducted to monitor reduction and carburization, providing insight into phase transitions and carbon deposition.
- **Findings:** For 40Fe/AlSi, incomplete carburization is observed up to 350 °C, with a 16.2% weight loss (theoretical: 24%). Above 330 °C, a sudden weight increase indicates carbon deposition via the Boudouard reaction. The extent of carburization in 40Fe/AlSi is about twice that of 40Fe/AlG.
- **Observations:** The AlSi support’s high pore volume accommodates greater carbon deposition, preventing deactivation and supporting sustained activity.

#### 6.1.4 Local Structure and Elemental Incorporation
- **Description:** Solid-state NMR (Bruker Avance I 400) and XPS (SSX-100 ESCA) were used to probe the local environments of Al and Si and the surface composition.
- **Findings:** ^27Al MAS NMR spectra show peaks at +15, 40, and 75 ppm, confirming the preservation of octahedral, tetrahedral, and five-coordinated Al environments. ^29Si MAS NMR reveals peaks at −81 to −89 ppm, indicating Si is incorporated as a Si–Al spinel phase, with no evidence of separate silica. XPS shows an Al (2p) peak at 76.15 eV, consistent with alumina.
- **Observations:** The effective incorporation of 5 wt% silica as a spinel phase stabilizes the support, retards phase transitions, and reduces surface acidity, as confirmed by low acid site concentration after calcination.

#### 6.1.5 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to assess particle morphology and size distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images of the AlSi support calcined at 1100 °C reveal particle sizes below 5 nm, corroborating XRD-derived crystallite sizes.
- **Observations:** The small, uniform particles and stable morphology contribute to high metal dispersion and resistance to sintering, underpinning the catalyst’s exceptional stability during extended operation.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed into products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For the 40Fe/AlSi catalyst, CO conversion under low-temperature Fischer–Tropsch (LTFT) conditions (260 °C, 2.1 MPa, H₂/CO = 1) is reported as 20%. The (H₂+CO) conversion is 20.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The 40Fe/AlSi catalyst demonstrates a high and stable CO conversion, outperforming both the conventional 40Fe/AlG and unsupported FeCuKSi catalysts. The high conversion is attributed to the optimized support structure and effective promotion by Cu and K.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 40Fe/AlSi catalyst exhibits a CO₂ selectivity of 38.1 mol%.
- **Observations and Trends:** This moderate CO₂ selectivity indicates balanced water–gas shift activity, supporting efficient hydrocarbon synthesis while limiting excessive CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO that yields methane, a key indicator of hydrocarbon distribution. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 40Fe/AlSi catalyst achieves a CH₄ selectivity of 9.4–9.6 mol%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The relatively low methane selectivity, compared to conventional supports, is attributed to the high pore volume and weak metal–support interactions of the AlSi support, which favor chain growth over methane formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms, indicating the catalyst’s ability to promote chain growth. It is defined as:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For 40Fe/AlSi, C₃⁺ selectivity is reported as 47.0 mol% (total basis) and 86.0 mol% (CO₂-free basis).
- **Observations and Trends:** The high C₃⁺ selectivity reflects the catalyst’s strong propensity for long-chain hydrocarbon formation, a direct result of the support’s structural and chemical properties.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring activity over extended time-on-stream, with deactivation rate typically defined as the decline in conversion per hour. In this study, stability is characterized by the change in activity over 700 h.
- **Experimental Data:** The 40Fe/AlSi catalyst exhibited a 13% increase in activity after 700 h on stream, with no observed deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** This exceptional stability is attributed to the thermally robust, silica-doped alumina support, which prevents sintering and maintains active phase dispersion. The catalyst’s performance surpasses conventional supports, which typically show gradual deactivation.

#### 6.2.6 Productivity and Rate Constant
- **Description:** Productivity (gHC/g_cat/h) and rate constant (mmol (CO+H₂)/gFe/h/MPa) provide additional measures of catalyst efficiency.
- **Experimental Data:** The 40Fe/AlSi catalyst achieves a productivity of 0.45 gHC/g_cat/h and a rate constant of 618 mmol (CO+H₂)/gFe/h/MPa.
- **Observations and Trends:** These values are significantly higher than those for 40Fe/AlG and unsupported FeCuKSi, underscoring the superior performance enabled by the AlSi support.

**Summary:**  
The 40Fe/AlSi catalyst demonstrates high CO conversion, low methane selectivity, high C₃⁺ selectivity, and outstanding long-term stability, all directly linked to its unique silica-doped alumina support and optimized synthesis. These results establish 40Fe/AlSi as a benchmark for supported iron Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.
