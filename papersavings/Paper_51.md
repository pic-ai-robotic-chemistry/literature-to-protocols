## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of FeK/rGO catalysts follows a one‑pot method that integrates precursor mixing, in‑situ precipitation–deposition, drying, and promoter incorporation in a sequential process designed for reproducibility.

#### • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
1. Disperse graphene oxide (GO) in deionized water using an ultrasonicator until a uniform yellowish brown suspension is obtained.  
   • Equipment: Ultrasonicator  
   • Duration: Approximately 2 hours (ensuring fine dispersion)  
2. Dissolve Fe(acac)₃ in ethanol and introduce it into the GO suspension under constant stirring.  
   • Reagent details: Fe(acac)₃ acts as the iron precursor  
   • Observation: Homogenization of the mixture is confirmed by a uniform color.

#### • Precipitation–Deposition  
3. Add hydrazine hydrate dropwise to the mixed solution.  
   • Function: Hydrazine hydrate serves both as a reducing agent for GO (converting it to reduced graphene oxide, rGO) and as a precipitant for ferric ions by increasing the pH from ~1.5 to 8.5.  
   • Observation: Rapid formation of a black precipitate marks the onset of ferric ion precipitation as Fe(OH)₃.

#### • Post‑reaction Processing  
4. Reflux the reaction mixture at 363 K (approximately 90°C) for 12 hours to ensure complete reduction and precipitation.  
5. Recover the black solid by filtration and dry at 333 K (approximately 60°C) for 8 hours.  
   • Equipment: Temperature‑controlled drying setup  
   • Outcome: Removal of residual solvents and stabilization of the catalyst structure.

#### • Promoter Incorporation (for FeK/rGO variants)  
6. For catalysts containing potassium, impregnate the dried Fe/rGO by reslurrying it in deionized water.  
   • Sonicate the slurry for 30 minutes to ensure dispersion.  
7. Add a calculated volume of aqueous K₂CO₃ solution (nominal loadings: 0.50, 1.0, 1.5, or 2.0 wt%) and sonicate for an additional 30 minutes, followed by stirring at room temperature for 12 hours.  
   • Observation: Even distribution of potassium is confirmed by the absence of phase segregation.

This method, with its well‐defined control over pH, temperature, and reagent addition, supports reproducibility and scalability while clearly highlighting the benefits and limitations of one‑pot processing compared to more traditional techniques.

- **Initial mixture (Fe(acac)₃ in GO)**: Stirred at room temperature for 4 hours as per the Fe/rGO protocol.  
- **Post-promoter addition mixture**: After reslurrying and K₂CO₃ solution transfer, the mixture is ultrasonically treated for 30 minutes and then stirred at room temperature for 12 hours to ensure a uniform distribution of the promoter.  

In these procedures, the sequential addition—starting with solid reagent weighing followed by liquid transfers and controlled mixing—ensures reproducibility and precise tailoring of each catalyst formulation as required for the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/rGO Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 100 mL deionized water, 0.50 g graphene oxide (GO), 0.50 g Fe(acac)₃, 25 mL ethanol, and 10 mL of 85% hydrazine hydrate.
- **Conditions**: Ultrasonication for 2 hours; stirring at room temperature; heating to 363 K; pH adjustment from 1.5 to 8.5 during hydrazine addition.
- **Process**:
  - Disperse 0.50 g of GO in 100 mL deionized water via ultrasonication for 2 hours to ensure a uniform yellowish-brown suspension.
  - Dissolve 0.50 g of Fe(acac)₃ in 25 mL ethanol. Combine this solution with the GO dispersion and stir at room temperature for 4 hours for complete mixing.
  - Heat the combined mixture to 363 K. Then, add 10 mL of 85% hydrazine hydrate dropwise. This addition simultaneously reduces GO to rGO and precipitates ferric ions, as evidenced by the pH increasing from 1.5 to 8.5 and the formation of a black precipitate.
  - Maintain reflux at 363 K for 12 hours to ensure thorough reaction progression.
- **Key Steps**: Careful control of hydrazine addition and maintenance of reflux temperature are critical to reproduce the reduction of GO and precipitation of iron species.

##### Step 2: Purification and Drying
- **Process**:
  - Recover the black precipitate by filtration.
  - Wash the solid repeatedly with deionized water to remove soluble impurities.
  - Dry the washed catalyst in an oven at 333 K for 8 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Consistent washing and controlled drying are essential for reproducibility.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Potassium-Promoted FeK/rGO Catalysts

##### Step 1: Promoter Incorporation
- **Reagents**: Aqueous K₂CO₃ solution (volumes adjusted to achieve nominal loadings of 0.50, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 wt% K).
- **Conditions**: Sonication for 30 minutes; stirring at room temperature for 12 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Reslurry the dried Fe/rGO catalyst in deionized water and sonicate for 30 minutes.
  - Add the calculated K₂CO₃ solution dropwise, then sonicate for an additional 30 minutes.
  - Stir the mixture at room temperature for 12 hours to ensure uniform distribution of the K promoter.
- **Key Steps**: Precise dosing of K₂CO₃ and thorough mixing through sonication and prolonged stirring enable consistent promoter incorporation across different loadings.

This detailed workflow, from the synthesis of primary precursors to final catalyst assembly, is designed to facilitate reproducibility of the Fischer–Tropsch catalyst synthesis using FeK/rGO, ensuring each critical processing step is explicitly addressed.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: Conventional XRD patterns were collected to determine the catalyst’s crystalline phases. Additionally, synchrotron radiation XRD was employed to resolve fine structural details during phase transformations.
- **Instruments and Models**: Conventional analysis was performed using a Bruker AXS D8 Advance diffractometer with Ni‑filtered Cu Kα radiation. Synchrotron measurements used a Huber5021 six‑circle diffractometer at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility.
- **Characterization Conditions**: For conventional XRD, the tube voltage and current were set at 40 kV and 40 mA, respectively; the 2θ range spanned 20°–80° with a 1°/min scanning rate and a step size of 0.01°. Synchrotron experiments used a wavelength of 0.12398 nm, scanning from 30° to 60° at 1°/min with a 0.02° step size.
- **Test Parameters & Key Notes**: Samples were protected with PEG200 to avoid oxidation; diffraction peak widths enabled crystallite size estimation.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: The reduction behavior was analyzed by subjecting the catalyst to a 5 vol % H₂/Ar mixture.
- **Instruments and Models**: Utilized a micro fixed‑bed reactor coupled with a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Reduction at 723 K for 16 hours with a gas flow of 50 mL/min.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and strict temperature control ensured consistent reduction profiles.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: CO pulses were injected using helium as the carrier gas until peak intensities stabilized, followed by purging and desorption.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Purge for 30 minutes then heat from 300 to 723 K at 10 K/min.
- **Key Notes**: This method differentiates reduction behavior via CO reactivity.

### 4.4 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
- **Operational Description**: Catalyst powders were ground, sonicated in anhydrous ethanol, and deposited onto carbon film-coated copper grids before air drying.

### 4.5 BET Surface Area Analysis

### 4.6 Temperature‑Programmed Desorption (TPD) for CO₂, CO, and H₂
- **Operational Description**: Approximately 100 mg of catalyst, reduced at 723 K in 5 vol % H₂/Ar for 16 hours, was cooled to 298 K for gas pulse injections until peak stabilization; purged at 25 mL/min followed by heating from 300 to 723 K at 10 K/min.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is a critical pre‐reaction treatment that ensures the formation of the active metallic phase from the iron precursor. The following subsections outline the key parameters used in the activation procedure.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Activation temperature is essential to effectively reduce the precursor and, in some cases, induce minor carburization. In this work, the catalyst is reduced at 723 K. This elevated temperature facilitates the reduction of the Fe(acac)₃ precursor to form the active iron phase while preserving the dispersion of particles, as the reduced graphene oxide (rGO) support minimizes sintering due to its inherent oxygen‐containing groups and high thermal conductivity.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
The activation is performed under atmospheric pressure. Operating at these conditions promotes standard gas–catalyst interactions without the additional complexity of elevated pressures. Atmospheric pressure is sufficient to provide a steady reduction environment in a tubular fixed-bed reactor.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
A gas mixture of 5 vol% H₂ in Ar is utilized for the activation process. This reducing atmosphere ensures a controlled reduction, where the hydrogen content is adequate to convert the iron precursor while the argon serves as an inert carrier. The catalyst is exposed to the gas at a flow rate of 50 mL/min, which guarantees uniform and continuous contact.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
Although not explicitly reported as space-time velocity in the article, the parameters indicate that the applied flow rate over approximately 100 mg of catalyst corresponds to an effective space-time velocity of around 30,000 mL/(g_cat·h). Such a high value ensures rapid and uniform exposure of the catalyst to the reducing atmosphere, thereby enhancing the reduction efficiency.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time and Equipment  
The reduction is carried out in a tubular fixed-bed reactor where the catalyst, diluted with quartz powder, is maintained at 723 K for 16 hours with a heating rate of 2 K/min. This prolonged exposure under well-controlled conditions ensures complete activation without significant sintering, thereby optimizing the catalyst performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

This section outlines the specific reaction conditions employed during the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation. Each parameter has been carefully controlled to ensure reproducibility and to facilitate a detailed understanding of catalyst behavior under defined operating conditions.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
• **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for activating syngas and directing the formation of the active Hagg carbide phase on the catalyst.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were performed at a temperature of 613 K.  
• **Observations:** This temperature supports the formation of Hagg carbide, which is essential for achieving high catalytic activity. Furthermore, operating at 613 K under these conditions ensures optimal kinetic rates while also controlling the extent of carbon deposition over time.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
• **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the concentration of syngas and can affect both the conversion rates and the product distribution in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was conducted at a maintained pressure of 20 bar.  
• **Observations:** The high pressure enhances syngas concentration, thereby increasing the reaction rate. It also plays a role in minimizing excessive carbon deposition that could obstruct active sites, although slight deactivation due to carbon accumulation was noted over extended durations.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
• **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is pivotal in determining the hydrocarbon product distribution, particularly affecting selectivity toward lower olefins as opposed to methane.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** A strict H₂/CO ratio of 1 was used during the catalytic evaluation.  
• **Observations:** This low ratio is beneficial for promoting lower olefin production while suppressing unwanted byproducts. The balance achieved by this ratio aids in maintaining the favorable formation of the active iron phase.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Duration
• **Definition:** Reaction duration defines the operational period during which the catalyst’s activity and stability are monitored.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was evaluated over a reaction period of 24 hours.  
• **Observations:** During this period, minor increases in carbon layer thickness (from approximately 2.4 nm at 24 hours to 2.7 nm after prolonged operation) were observed, indicating slight catalyst deactivation while confirming the overall stability of the FeK/rGO catalysts under these conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural evolution of the FeK/rGO catalysts were determined using conventional powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) and synchrotron radiation XRD. These techniques enable precise identification of iron oxide and carbide phases, which are critical for Fischer–Tropsch activity.
- **Findings:** Fresh catalysts exhibit diffraction peaks at 2θ = 33.1, 35.6, and 40.8°, corresponding to hematite (α-Fe₂O₃, JCPDS 33-0664). After Fischer–Tropsch operation, synchrotron XRD reveals the predominant formation of Hagg carbide (χ-Fe₅C₂, JCPDS 36-1248), with the (510) reflection at 2θ ≈ 44.1° intensifying and no α-Fe, Fe₃O₄, or other iron carbides detected.
- **Observations:** The transformation from α-Fe₂O₃ to Hagg carbide is complete under reaction conditions, and the intensity of Hagg carbide peaks increases with higher potassium loading, indicating enhanced carbide formation and stabilization.

#### 6.1.2 Particle Size and Morphology
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to assess nanoparticle size, dispersion, and carbon layer thickness, while XRD provided complementary crystallite size data via the Scherrer equation.
- **Findings:** Fresh α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles are well-dispersed on rGO, with sizes of 5.8–6.0 nm, unaffected by low K loading. After 24 h on stream, Hagg carbide particle sizes increase with K content: 11.1 nm (Fe/rGO), 11.6 nm (FeK0.5/rGO), 11.4 nm (FeK1/rGO), 16.0 nm (FeK1.5/rGO), and 17.0 nm (FeK2/rGO). Carbon layer thickness also rises with K: from 2.2 nm (0 wt% K) to 2.7 nm (2.0 wt% K).
- **Observations:** Low K loadings maintain small, uniform particles, while higher K promotes particle growth and carbon deposition, potentially reducing accessible active sites.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET method) quantified specific surface area, pore size, and pore volume, reflecting the textural properties relevant to catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** Surface area (SBET) increases with K loading: 122 m²/g (Fe/rGO), 126 m²/g (FeK0.5/rGO), 135 m²/g (FeK1/rGO), 138 m²/g (FeK1.5/rGO), and 145 m²/g (FeK2/rGO). Average pore size decreases slightly (6.1 nm to 5.5 nm), while pore volume increases (0.33 to 0.39 cm³/g).
- **Observations:** Potassium intercalation into rGO enhances porosity and surface area, supporting improved reactant diffusion.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Composition and Chemical States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyzed surface elemental composition and iron oxidation states, while ICP-AES confirmed bulk Fe and K loadings.
- **Findings:** XPS Fe 2p₃/₂ spectra show peaks at 711.0–711.5 eV (Fe³⁺), 709.5–710.0 eV (Fe²⁺), and 707.0–708.5 eV (FeCx). The FeCx peak intensifies with increasing K, indicating promoted carburization. ICP-AES results align with nominal Fe and K loadings.
- **Observations:** Potassium enhances surface carburization, correlating with higher catalytic activity and selectivity to lower olefins.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Basicity and Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) of CO₂, CO, and H₂ probed surface basicity and adsorption characteristics.
- **Findings:** CO and H₂ adsorption both increase with K, but CO adsorption rises more sharply, elevating the surface CO/H₂ ratio. CO₂-TPD indicates increased basicity with K loading.
- **Observations:** Enhanced basicity and CO adsorption suppress secondary hydrogenation, favoring olefin selectivity.

#### 6.1.6 rGO Support Structure and Defects
- **Description:** FTIR and Raman spectroscopy characterized the reduction of graphene oxide and defect density in rGO.
- **Findings:** FTIR shows diminished oxygen-containing groups and new aromatic CC/C–O stretches post-reduction. Raman spectra display D and G bands (ID/IG ≈ 1.26–1.31), confirming rGO with typical defect levels.
- **Observations:** The rGO support is effectively reduced and defect-rich, facilitating nanoparticle dispersion and catalytic function.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products (hydrocarbons and CO₂) during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 613 K, 20 bar, and H₂/CO = 1, all FeK/rGO catalysts (Fe/rGO, FeK0.5/rGO, FeK1/rGO, FeK1.5/rGO, FeK2/rGO) exhibited CO conversions in the range of 58–64%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The CO conversion remains relatively stable across the series, providing a consistent basis for comparing selectivity and activity metrics.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** All catalysts showed CO₂ selectivity values between 49% and 52%, regardless of potassium loading.
- **Observations and Trends:** The CO₂ selectivity remains largely unaffected by K promotion, indicating that potassium does not significantly alter the extent of CO₂ formation under these conditions.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the percentage of converted CO that yields methane, a key indicator of undesired product formation. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity decreases markedly with increasing K content: Fe/rGO (48%), FeK0.5/rGO (31%), FeK1/rGO (26%), FeK1.5/rGO (22%), FeK2/rGO (20%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium promotion effectively suppresses methane formation, favoring the production of longer-chain hydrocarbons and olefins.

#### 6.2.4 Lower Olefin Selectivity and O/P Ratio
- **Description:** Lower olefin selectivity refers to the fraction of converted CO forming C₂–C₄ olefins, while the olefin/paraffin (O/P) ratio indicates the relative abundance of olefins to paraffins in the C₂–C₄ range.
- **Experimental Data:** Lower olefin selectivity increases from 31% (Fe/rGO) to 68% (FeK2/rGO). The O/P ratio rises from 1.5 (Fe/rGO) to 11 (FeK2/rGO) with increasing K loading.
- **Observations and Trends:** The steady increase in both metrics with K content highlights potassium’s role in promoting olefin formation and suppressing secondary hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.5 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY measures catalytic activity as the amount of CO converted per gram of Fe per second:
  \[
  \text{FTY} = \frac{\text{μmol CO converted}}{\text{g Fe} \cdot \text{s}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FTY exhibits a volcano-type trend: Fe/rGO (333 μmolCO gFe⁻¹ s⁻¹), FeK0.5/rGO (556), FeK1/rGO (646), FeK1.5/rGO (271), FeK2/rGO (220).
- **Observations and Trends:** Activity peaks at 1.0 wt% K, then declines at higher K loadings due to increased Hagg carbide particle size and carbon deposition, which limit accessible active sites.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring FTY and selectivity over extended time-on-stream. Deactivation is linked to changes in FTY and product distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** For FeK1/rGO, FTY decreases from its maximum to 371 μmolCO gFe⁻¹ s⁻¹ after 120 h, with lower olefin selectivity dropping from 62% to 57%. TEM reveals carbon layer thickening from ~2.4 nm (24 h) to ~2.7 nm (120 h).
- **Observations and Trends:** The rGO support and optimal K loading confer good stability, with gradual deactivation primarily due to carbon deposition rather than sintering.

---
**Summary:**  
Potassium promotion of Fe/rGO catalysts in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis leads to enhanced selectivity for lower olefins and suppressed methane formation, with optimal activity at 1.0 wt% K. The catalysts maintain stable performance over extended operation, with deactivation mainly attributed to carbon layer growth on Hagg carbide nanoparticles.
