## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The SxFe/–Al₂O₃ catalyst is synthesized using the incipient wetness impregnation method, which ensures a uniform deposition of active iron species and sulfur onto the –Al₂O₃ support. This section details the reported synthesis procedures with an emphasis on reproducibility and experimental clarity.

#### Impregnation / Precursor Mixing
• The –Al₂O₃ support is impregnated with an aqueous solution containing Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and (NH₄)₂SO₄.  
• The Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O acts as the iron precursor, while (NH₄)₂SO₄ provides sulfur, enabling controlled S/Fe molar ratios (0, 0.001, 0.002, 0.005, 0.01, and 0.02).  
• Uniform distribution is achieved through vacuum treatment, ensuring full saturation of the support with the solution.

#### (Precipitation–Deposition)
• Although not separately delineated, the impregnation step effectively serves the role of precursor mixing prior to subsequent drying. The procedure is designed to avoid the need for an additional precipitation–deposition stage.

#### Drying
• The impregnated support is dried at 383 K for 12 hours.  
• This step is critical to remove the solvent and initiate the fixation of the active species, preventing agglomeration during later treatments.

#### Calcination
• Following drying, calcination is conducted at 673 K for 2 hours under an airflow of 30 mL/min.  
• Calcination transforms the iron nitrate into oxidized iron species (primarily Fe₂O₃) while promoting adherence of the catalyst constituents to the support.

#### Reduction (Post‑treatment)
• The calcined catalyst is subsequently reduced in a flowing H₂ environment at 673 K for 2 hours.  
• This reduction step converts the oxidized species into metallic iron, which is the active phase for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This synthesis method offers clear control over catalyst composition, scalability, and cost-effectiveness. It also facilitates enhanced interaction between the iron phase and –Al₂O₃, which is crucial for tailoring catalyst activity and selectivity. Observations such as the fading of Fe₂O₃ diffraction peaks with higher sulfur contents underscore the method’s reproducibility and its influence on catalyst performance.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the preparation of the precursor reagents for the SxFe/–Al₂O₃ catalysts (with S/Fe molar ratios of 0, 0.001, 0.002, 0.005, 0.01, and 0.02) via incipient wetness impregnation. The active iron phase is introduced by Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O while sulfur is incorporated through controlled additions of (NH₄)₂SO₄. An aqueous solution is prepared and transferred onto the pre‐treated –Al₂O₃ support (vacuum-treated at 383K for 12 hours) in a reproducible manner.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Catalyst S0Fe/–Al₂O₃
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 3.5g using a precise balance.

##### 3.1.1.2 Catalyst S0.001Fe/–Al₂O₃
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 3.5g.
- **(NH₄)₂SO₄**: Weighed 0.0016g.

##### 3.1.1.3 Catalyst S0.002Fe/–Al₂O₃
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 3.5g.
- **(NH₄)₂SO₄**: Weighed 0.0033g.

##### 3.1.1.4 Catalyst S0.005Fe/–Al₂O₃
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 3.5g.
- **(NH₄)₂SO₄**: Weighed 0.0082g.

##### 3.1.1.5 Catalyst S0.01Fe/–Al₂O₃
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 3.5g.
- **(NH₄)₂SO₄**: Weighed 0.0164g.

##### 3.1.1.6 Catalyst S0.02Fe/–Al₂O₃
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 3.5g.
- **(NH₄)₂SO₄**: Weighed 0.0817g.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

For each catalyst, the aqueous reagent solution is prepared by dissolving the weighed reagents. Then:

##### 3.1.2.1 Catalyst S0Fe/–Al₂O₃  
- **Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ solution**: Transferred 1.5mL onto the –Al₂O₃ support.

##### 3.1.2.2 Catalyst S0.001Fe/–Al₂O₃  
- **Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃/(NH₄)₂SO₄ solution**: Transferred 1.5mL onto the –Al₂O₃ support.

(Identical liquid transfer entries are applied for the S0.002, S0.005, S0.01, and S0.02 catalysts.)

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

For all catalyst variants:

##### 3.1.3.1 Catalyst S0Fe/–Al₂O₃  
- **Impregnated mixture**: Mixed using the incipient wetness impregnation method to achieve uniform contact between the aqueous solution and the –Al₂O₃ support.

##### 3.1.3.2 Catalyst S0.001Fe/–Al₂O₃  
- **Impregnated mixture**: Mixed using the incipient wetness impregnation method to ensure even distribution.

(Consistent mixing procedures are applied for the S0.002, S0.005, S0.01, and S0.02 catalysts.)

This reproducible procedure—comprising precise weighing of solid precursors, controlled liquid transfer (1.5mL per impregnation), and standardized mixing—is critical to obtain the desired catalyst characteristics prior to subsequent drying, calcination, and reduction steps in the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: SxFe/–Al₂O₃

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 3.5 g  
  • (NH₄)₂SO₄, varying from 0.0016 to 0.0817 g (according to the target S/Fe molar ratio)  
  • Sufficient deionized water to prepare an aqueous solution (typically around 100 mL)
- **Conditions**:  
  • Room temperature stirring  
  • No pH adjustment is indicated  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve 3.5 g of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in deionized water to form a clear, homogeneous solution.  
  2. Separately, dissolve the required amount of (NH₄)₂SO₄ in a small volume of deionized water.  
  3. Combine both solutions slowly under constant stirring to ensure complete mixing and uniform distribution of the sulfur source.

- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensure full dissolution of both precursors.  
  • Maintain consistency in reagent ratios for reproducibility.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly by Incipient Wetness Impregnation
- **Reagents**:  
  • Prepared aqueous precursor solution  
  • –Al₂O₃ support (pre-characterized by its surface area and pore volume to facilitate metal-support interactions)
- **Conditions**:  
  • Even impregnation ensuring the support reaches incipient wetness without excess liquid  
- **Process**:  
  1. Gradually impregnate the –Al₂O₃ support with the precursor solution, ensuring complete saturation.  
  2. Utilize a vacuum treatment if necessary to assist in uniform distribution.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:  
  • Condition: 383 K for 12 hours to ensure complete removal of moisture.  
- **Calcination**:  
  • Condition: Heat at 673 K for 2 hours under an airflow (approximately 30 mL/min) to promote oxidation and formation of the catalyst precursor.
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Monitor temperature profiles and airflow, as these influence iron phase evolution.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Reagents/Conditions**:  
  • Pure H₂ atmosphere  
  • Temperature of 673 K for 2 hours with a hydrogen flow of 30 mL/min
- **Process**:  
  1. Reduce the calcined catalyst in a flowing H₂ environment to convert iron oxides into the active metallic phase.
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensure complete reduction by monitoring temperature and hydrogen flow.

This comprehensive, step-by-step synthesis protocol—from primary precursor preparation to final catalyst activation—ensures reproducibility. It addresses critical parameters such as reagent quantities, impregnation uniformity, drying, calcination, and reduction conditions, all vital for achieving the desired catalytic properties in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: In situ and conventional XRD were employed to monitor phase transformations of the catalyst (from Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄, then to FeO and metallic Fe) during controlled reduction. The in situ technique was used first to provide real‑time observations under a hydrogen atmosphere.
- **Instruments and Models**: Panalytical X’pert PRO diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were conducted with Cu‑K radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA. In situ tests were performed under a H₂ flow (99.999%, 120 mL/min) with the sample heated from room temperature to 873 K at a rate of 10 K/min; the scanning range was 2θ = 20–80° with a scanning rate of 3°/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Step‑by‑step heating captures the reduction stages, starting with an initial oxidation step (using O₂/N₂ at 673 K for 20 minutes) to ensure defined precursor states prior to reduction.
- **Key Notes**: Precise sample alignment and controlled heating ensure reproducibility. The sequential monitoring of phase changes confirms the progressive reduction behavior critical for catalytic performance.

### 4.2 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
- **Operational Description**: TEM was used for ex situ investigation of catalyst morphology and particle size, differentiating fresh catalysts (≈15 nm) from spent ones (≈25–30 nm) while assessing carbon deposition.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Tecnai F30 transmission electron microscope operating at 300 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were prepared by depositing a drop of catalyst–ethanol suspension on a carbon-film–coated copper grid.
- **Test Parameters**: No additional drying or treatment details were specified.
- **Key Notes**: TEM provided visual confirmation of particle growth and carbon deposition differences, particularly noting reduced carbon buildup in sulfur-modified catalysts.

### 4.3 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR assessed the reduction behavior of the catalysts by monitoring hydrogen consumption over defined temperature stages.
- **Instruments and Models**: GC‑TPR apparatus.
- **Characterization Conditions**: A 0.10 g catalyst sample was pretreated with a 5/95 vol% O₂/Ar mixture at 573 K for 30 minutes, cooled to 323 K, and then purged with a 5/95 H₂/Ar mix. The temperature was ramped from 323 K to 1173 K at 10 K/min under a 50 mL/min flow.
- **Test Parameters**: Baseline stabilization was ensured by purging before heating.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and precise temperature control are essential to accurately resolve the three reduction stages.

### 4.4 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: CO‑TPR provided complementary insights into catalyst reduction via CO as a reducing agent.
- **Instruments and Models**: GC‑TPR apparatus.
- **Characterization Conditions**: The 0.10 g catalyst was pretreated in He (99.999%, 30 mL/min) at 673 K for 15 minutes, cooled to 373 K, and purged with a 5/95 CO/He mixture. The temperature was increased to 1073 K at 10 K/min under a 50 mL/min flow.
- **Test Parameters**: Detection of m/z = 28 (CO) and 44 (CO₂) ensured monitoring of reduction products.
- **Key Notes**: CO‑TPR validates reduction steps alongside H₂‑TPR, enhancing understanding of sulfur’s effect on reducibility.

### 4.5 X‑Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: XPS was used to determine surface composition and chemical states, particularly differentiating sulfate and sulfide species after reduction.
- **Instruments and Models**: Omicron Sphera II hemispherical electron energy analyzer with an in situ reaction cell.
- **Characterization Conditions**: A monochromatic Al K‑X-ray source (1486.6 eV) operated at 15 kV and 300 W; binding energies were referenced to the Al 2p₁/₂ peak at 74.3 eV (±0.1 eV accuracy).
- **Test Parameters**: Step‑by‑step thermal treatments (from oxidation to reduction and subsequent CO/H₂ exposures) ensured reliable surface state evaluation.
- **Key Notes**: XPS data correlate with XRD and TPR results to confirm phase and compositional changes during catalyst operation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is activated via a dedicated reduction step that transforms the iron oxide precursor into its active metallic state. This section details the precise activation parameters and their roles in establishing an effective catalyst.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
• **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for driving the sequential reduction of iron oxide species (Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄ → FeO → metallic Fe).  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced at 673 K.  
• **Observations:** This temperature ensures complete reduction of the oxide phases while promoting the formation of the active metallic phase. Although high temperatures can, in some cases, lead to minor sintering, TEM analyses reveal that the metal particle sizes remain effectively maintained.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
• **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the kinetics of the reduction reaction by regulating the interaction between hydrogen and the catalyst surface.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction is performed under a pressure of 0.10 MPa in a high-pressure fixed-bed reactor.  
• **Observations:** Maintaining this pressure supports an efficient reduction while ensuring reproducibility across the catalyst bed.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
• **Definition:** The choice of gas, and its flow characteristics, critically affects the reduction pathway by ensuring a strongly reducing environment.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** A continuous flow of pure hydrogen (H₂) is employed during the activation process.  
• **Observations:** The controlled H₂ environment facilitates a gradual conversion of the iron oxides to metallic iron, thereby minimizing unwanted side reactions and ensuring uniform reduction.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
• **Definition:** The duration of the reduction process is key to achieving complete transformation of the precursor while avoiding excessive sintering.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under the reduction conditions for 2 hours with a heating rate of 10 K/min.  
• **Observations:** This period is optimized to yield the active state required for high catalytic performance during the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction, while controlling potential aggregation phenomena.

Together, these controlled activation parameters establish the necessary conditions to convert the precursor catalyst into its active form, ensuring high performance and reproducibility during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis reaction.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis as it governs the kinetics of CO conversion, influences product selectivity (favoring olefins over methane), and affects the catalyst’s resistance to deactivation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was conducted at 573K.
- **Observations:** This elevated temperature is reported to improve catalyst activity and stability by mitigating the poisoning effects of sulfur. It promotes effective syngas conversion while reducing undesired carbon deposition that can block active sites.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure directly affects the concentration of the reactants in the reactor, thereby controlling CO conversion rates and the distribution of hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were carried out at a fixed pressure of 2.0 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating under this pressure supports enhanced CO conversion, which in turn favors the selective formation of heavier hydrocarbons (C₅⁺) while maintaining optimal activity, as indicated by comparative performance trends.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is a vital parameter that influences the hydrocarbon chain growth mechanism; it can shift selectivity between methane and longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 1 (v/v) was maintained during the FTS reaction.
- **Observations:** A ratio of 1 is noted to be particularly effective for syngas derived from coal or biomass. This balanced ratio encourages desirable product distributions and moderates the selectivity, providing a stable reaction environment without heavily skewing towards methane formation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, defined as the volumetric flow rate of syngas relative to the mass of the catalyst, determines the residence time of reactants on the catalyst surface.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The gas hourly space velocity was set at 24,000 mL/(h·g_cat).
- **Observations:** This high space-time velocity is critical for ensuring reproducibility by standardizing reactant contact time. Although lower residence time can marginally reduce CO conversion, it helps in maintaining catalyst stability and consistent product distributions under continuous operation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure (XRD)
- **Description:** The evolution of iron phases during catalyst preparation and reaction was monitored using both conventional and in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD), with a Panalytical X'Pert PRO diffractometer (Cu-Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å, 2θ = 20–80°, scan rate 3°/min). XRD is critical for identifying phase transitions and crystallinity changes in Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.
- **Findings:** For sulfur-free Fe/–Al₂O₃, the reduction sequence under H₂ proceeds as Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄ → FeO → metallic Fe. Sulfur addition alters this sequence: the reduction of Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ occurs at lower temperatures, and FeO is not detected in sulfur-containing catalysts. After CO/H₂ treatment, iron carbide (Fe₅C₂) formation is observed in sulfur-free samples, but is suppressed in sulfur-modified catalysts, as indicated by weaker or absent carbide peaks. Increasing sulfur content leads to decreased Fe₂O₃ peak intensity, reflecting reduced crystallinity.
- **Observations:** Sulfur promotes the reduction to metallic Fe under H₂ but inhibits carburization and carbide formation under CO, directly impacting catalytic phase composition and activity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size (TEM)
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai F30, 300 kV) was used to assess particle morphology, size distribution, and carbon deposition. Samples were prepared by depositing a suspension onto carbon-film-coated copper grids.
- **Findings:** Fresh Fe/–Al₂O₃ and S0.01Fe/–Al₂O₃ catalysts exhibit similar iron (oxide) particle sizes (15 ± 5 nm and 16 ± 6 nm, respectively). After Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, particle sizes increase to 25 ± 7 nm (Fe/–Al₂O₃) and 30 ± 10 nm (S0.01Fe/–Al₂O₃), indicating sintering. The spent Fe/–Al₂O₃ surface is covered with amorphous carbon, while carbon deposition on S0.01Fe/–Al₂O₃ is negligible.
- **Observations:** Sulfur addition results in slightly larger iron particles post-reaction and significantly reduces amorphous carbon formation, enhancing catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Surface Chemistry (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) profiles reveal the reducibility of iron species and the influence of sulfur on reduction pathways.
- **Findings:** Sulfur-free Fe/–Al₂O₃ displays three reduction stages: Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ (~660 K), Fe₃O₄ to FeO (~850 K), and FeO to Fe⁰ (>940 K). With sulfur, the first reduction peak shifts to lower temperatures (e.g., 640 K), and the third peak also shifts downward as S/Fe increases. Sulfur thus facilitates the reduction of FeO to metallic Fe and lowers reduction temperatures across all stages.
- **Observations:** Enhanced reducibility in the presence of sulfur is evident, but sulfur also inhibits subsequent carburization under CO, as corroborated by XRD.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Elemental Composition and Sulfur Speciation (XPS)
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Omicron Sphera II, Al Kα, 1486.6 eV) was employed to determine surface elemental composition, iron oxidation states, and sulfur species.
- **Findings:** Fe 2p₃/₂ peaks distinguish Fe³⁺ (711.0–711.3 eV), Fe²⁺ (710.0 eV), metallic Fe (706.5 eV), and iron carbide (FeCx, 706.7 eV). After H₂ reduction, Fe³⁺ decreases and Fe²⁺/Fe⁰ increase. CO/H₂ treatment shifts the Fe 2p₃/₂ peak to 706.7 eV (FeCx) in sulfur-free catalysts, but not in sulfur-rich samples, indicating suppressed carburization. Sulfate (SO₄²⁻, 169.5 eV) and sulfide (S²⁻, 161.5 eV) are both present after H₂ reduction, with the S²⁻/Stotal ratio reaching ~45%. Surface S/Fe ratios increase with higher S/Fe loading.
- **Observations:** Sulfur exists as both sulfate and sulfide on the catalyst surface, with higher sulfur content suppressing iron carbide formation and altering surface chemistry.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (2.0 MPa, 573 K, H₂/CO = 1), CO conversion for SxFe/–Al₂O₃ catalysts decreases with increasing S/Fe molar ratio:  
  - S/Fe = 0: 49.3%  
  - S/Fe = 0.001: 41.2%  
  - S/Fe = 0.002: 28.7%  
  - S/Fe = 0.005: 17.4%  
  - S/Fe = 0.01: 5.6%  
  - S/Fe = 0.02: nearly 0% (no significant activity)
- **Observations and Trends:** Sulfur addition strongly suppresses CO conversion, especially at higher S/Fe ratios. At S/Fe = 0.02, the catalyst is essentially inactive. However, at elevated temperatures (up to 623 K), sulfur-modified catalysts partially recover activity, approaching that of the unmodified catalyst.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the percentage of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - S/Fe = 0: 7.0%  
  - S/Fe = 0.001: 5.6%  
  - S/Fe = 0.002: 3.7%  
  - S/Fe = 0.005: 2.5%  
  - S/Fe = 0.01: 0.8%
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing sulfur content leads to a marked decrease in methane selectivity, indicating that sulfur suppresses methane formation.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ (C₃⁺) Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity (representing longer-chain hydrocarbons) is defined as:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - S/Fe = 0: 36.7%  
  - S/Fe = 0.001: 33.9%  
  - S/Fe = 0.002: 29.7%  
  - S/Fe = 0.005: 31.5%  
  - S/Fe = 0.01: 27.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** C₅⁺ selectivity decreases as sulfur content increases, reflecting a shift toward lighter hydrocarbon products.

#### 6.2.4 Olefin-to-Paraffin Ratio (C₂–C₄ O/P)
- **Description:** The olefin-to-paraffin (O/P) ratio for C₂–C₄ products is:
  \[
  \text{O/P Ratio} = \frac{\text{Selectivity of C}_2\text{–C}_4 \text{ olefins}}{\text{Selectivity of C}_2\text{–C}_4 \text{ paraffins}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - S/Fe = 0: 0.58  
  - S/Fe = 0.001: 0.55  
  - S/Fe = 0.002: 0.52  
  - S/Fe = 0.005: 0.50  
  - S/Fe = 0.01: 0.48
- **Observations and Trends:** The O/P ratio declines with increasing sulfur, indicating enhanced hydrogenation and a higher proportion of paraffins among C₂–C₄ products.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over time on stream (TOS). Deactivation is linked to carbon deposition and particle growth.
- **Experimental Data:** After 7 h TOS, CO conversion and selectivity values are as above. TEM analysis shows iron nanoparticles grow from ~15 nm (fresh) to 25–30 nm (spent), with sulfur-modified catalysts exhibiting less carbon deposition.
- **Observations and Trends:** Sulfur addition reduces carbon deposition and mitigates deactivation, especially at higher temperatures. The unmodified catalyst suffers more severe deactivation due to carbon buildup and particle agglomeration.

**Summary:**  
Sulfur modification of Fe/–Al₂O₃ catalysts for FTS leads to lower CO conversion and C₅⁺ selectivity, a pronounced decrease in CH₄ selectivity, and a reduced C₂–C₄ olefin-to-paraffin ratio. While high sulfur content severely deactivates the catalyst, moderate levels improve resistance to carbon deposition and enhance stability, particularly at elevated temperatures.
