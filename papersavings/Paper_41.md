## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Precipitated Fe–Mn–K–SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalyst is synthesized via a combined co‑precipitation and spray drying process. This approach enables careful control over the catalyst’s stoichiometry, phase uniformity, and morphology. Below is a detailed, reproducible step‑by‑step description based strictly on the experimental protocol:

#### • Precursor Mixing and Co‑precipitation  
1. Mix Fe(NO₃)₃ and Mn(NO₃)₂ in de‑ionized water using a continuously stirred tank.  
2. Initiate the co‑precipitation by adding an NH₄OH solution at 80 °C while maintaining the pH at 9.0.  
   - Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ for the active phase, Mn(NO₃)₂ as promoter, with the pH adjusted by NH₄OH.  
   - Observation: Formation of a precipitate indicates successful co‑precipitation.

#### • Precipitation–Deposition Step  
1. Following initial mixing, incorporate the support precursor by adding silica sol along with a K₂CO₃ solution dropwise.  
   - This deposition stage ensures that the silica support and alkali dopant (K) are uniformly integrated into the precipitate.
2. Stir the mixture for an additional period to promote complete deposition.

#### • Washing, Filtration, and Re‑slurrying  
1. Wash and filter the precipitate thoroughly to remove any unreacted species and impurities.  
2. Redispense the filtered material into de‑ionized water and re‑slurry by combining it with the previously added silica sol and K₂CO₃ solution.  
   - These steps guarantee that the desired molar ratio (Fe:Mn:K:Si = 100:15:3:30) is maintained throughout the process.

#### • Spray Drying and Calcination  
1. Subject the homogeneous slurry to spray drying using a dedicated spray dryer.  
   - Equipment: Spray dryer designed for catalyst precursor processing.
2. Calcine the resultant precursor at 450 °C for 3 hours in a controlled environment.  
   - This calcination step is crucial for achieving the intended phase transformations and catalyst performance.

### Comparative Analysis  
• Advantages: The co‑precipitation step allows precise control over metal ion distribution, whereas the subsequent spray drying ensures uniform particle morphology.  
• Disadvantages: The methodology is sensitive to variations in pH and temperature, requiring strict control over reaction conditions.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Phase Evolution and Crystal Structure
- **Description:** The evolution of iron phases during catalyst activation and reaction was systematically investigated using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) and Mössbauer Effect Spectroscopy (MES), providing complementary surface and bulk information, respectively.
- **Findings:** XPS analysis, calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.6 eV, revealed a sequential transformation of surface iron species: α-Fe₂O₃ (Fe³⁺, 710.6 eV) → Fe₃O₄ (broader, lower binding energy) → FeO (Fe²⁺, 709.2 eV) → metallic Fe (Fe⁰, 706.9 eV) as the reduction temperature increased. MES confirmed this pathway in the bulk, with isomer shift, quadrupole splitting, and magnetic hyperfine field parameters identifying and quantifying Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺, α-Fe, and iron carbide phases. Both techniques demonstrated that higher reduction temperatures (260–300 °C) promote the formation of FeO and α-Fe, which are rapidly carburized to iron carbides (notably χ-Fe₅C₂) upon CO exposure.
- **Observations:** The strong agreement between XPS and MES data confirms that phase transformations occur throughout the catalyst, not just at the surface. The rapid and complete conversion of α-Fe to iron carbides is particularly evident after CO adsorption, supporting the identification of χ-Fe₅C₂ as the active phase for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Species and Carburization Behavior
- **Description:** Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was employed to monitor CO adsorption and the formation of surface carbonate and hydrocarbon species, providing insight into carburization processes.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS spectra (resolution: 4 cm⁻¹, 64 scans) showed that CO adsorption on Fe(II) sites produced a band at 2143 cm⁻¹, which disappeared as carburization proceeded. The emergence of hydrocarbon bands at 1558, 1541, and 1508 cm⁻¹ indicated the formation of iron carbides (χ-Fe₅C₂). Carbonate species were identified in the 1700–1100 cm⁻¹ region, with bands at 1686, 1645, 1357, and 1298 cm⁻¹ corresponding to Fe(III) and Fe(II) oxides.
- **Observations:** The disappearance of CO adsorption bands and the growth of hydrocarbon features directly correlate with the transformation of reduced iron phases to iron carbides, confirming the catalyst’s strong carburization ability, especially after reduction at higher temperatures.

#### 6.1.3 Adsorption/Desorption Properties (TPD Analysis)
- **Description:** Temperature-Programmed Desorption (TPD) of H₂ and CO was conducted to probe the adsorption characteristics and phase-specific reactivity of the catalyst.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPD revealed a desorption peak at 336 °C for Fe₃O₄, with additional peaks at 150 °C (FeO) and 100 °C (α-Fe) as reduction temperature increased. CO-TPD after reduction at 260 °C showed desorption peaks at 200 °C (FeO) and 280 °C (Fe₃O₄), while a high-temperature peak at 440 °C was attributed to iron carbides. Notably, no low-temperature CO desorption peak was observed for α-Fe, indicating its rapid conversion to iron carbides upon CO exposure.
- **Observations:** The evolution of TPD profiles with reduction temperature, corroborated by MES and XPS, highlights the increasing carburization ability in the order α-Fe > FeO > Fe₃O₄, which is directly linked to enhanced catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Phase Stability under Reaction Conditions
- **Description:** The stability of active phases during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was assessed using in situ MES and DRIFTS.
- **Findings:** Under reaction conditions, both iron carbides and Fe(II) oxide species reached a balanced state, with no intermediate α-Fe detected. The relative amounts of these phases remained stable over time, as confirmed by MES.
- **Observations:** The maintenance of a stable phase composition under reaction conditions is indicative of the catalyst’s long-term stability and resistance to deactivation, supporting its suitability for sustained Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
