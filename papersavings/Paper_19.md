## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Co‑precipitated Fe–Zr catalysts are synthesized by combining co‑precipitation with an impregnation step to ensure reproducibility and uniformity. This section details the step‐by‐step procedures and key experimental conditions as reported.

#### • Co‑precipitation Method

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Reagents: A solution containing Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and ZrOCl₂·8H₂O is prepared in water with designated Zr/Fe mole ratios (0, 0.2, 1, 2, and pure Zr for Zrp).  
   - The mixed solution is added dropwise into an aqueous ammonia solution (14 wt%) maintained at 16 °C. The dropwise addition is carefully controlled to achieve and maintain a pH of 11.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - Immediately after the initial co‑precipitation step, the formed precipitate is subjected to a deposition process. This involves the addition of the precipitate into an aqueous potassium carbonate (K₂CO₃) solution with continuous stirring for 3 hours at 16 °C. This step ensures a uniform dispersion of the promoter elements and improves overall catalyst homogeneity.

3. **Washing**  
   - The precipitate is washed thoroughly with deionized water until the filtrate reaches a neutral pH of 7, thereby removing residual impurities such as ammonia and unreacted salts.

4. **Drying**  
   - The washed precipitate is directly dried at 120 °C overnight. This controlled drying step ensures complete moisture removal and preserves the catalyst’s microstructure.

5. **Calcination**  
   - Finally, the dried sample is calcined in air at 600 °C for 5 hours. Calcination facilitates the crystallization of α‑Fe₂O₃ and the incorporation of Zr into the oxide matrix.
   - Post‐processing involves crushing and sieving (20–40 mesh) to obtain a uniform particle size distribution.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the co-precipitated Fe–Zr catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD). This analysis identifies the presence and interaction of Fe and Zr oxide phases, as well as lattice modifications due to dopant incorporation.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns reveal that Zr0 and Zrp display single-phase α-Fe₂O₃ (JCPDS 33-0664, R-3c) and ZrO₂ (JCPDS 89-9096, Fm-3m), respectively. Mixed-phase compositions are observed in Zr0.2, Zr1, and Zr2, with both α-Fe₂O₃ and ZrO₂ present. Notably, the d₁₁₁ spacing of ZrO₂ decreases from 0.2959 nm (Zrp) to 0.2938 nm (Zr0.2), 0.2929 nm (Zr1), and 0.2919 nm (Zr2), while the 2θ value of the ZrO₂ (111) peak increases with Fe content.
- **Observations:** These shifts indicate substitution of smaller Fe³⁺ ions (r = 0.065 nm) for Zr⁴⁺ (r = 0.084 nm) in the ZrO₂ lattice, resulting in lattice contraction and the formation of Fe–Zr solid solutions. The mutual inhibition of Fe₂O₃ and ZrO₂ grain growth leads to smaller, more dispersed crystallites.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were assessed by N₂ adsorption–desorption (BET) to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size, which are critical for reactant accessibility and catalyst dispersion.
- **Findings:** BET surface area increases markedly with Zr addition: 13.59 m²/g (Zr0), 42.35 m²/g (Zr0.2), 93.35 m²/g (Zr1), and 108.91 m²/g (Zr2). Pore volume rises from 0.10 cm³/g (Zr0) to 0.22 cm³/g (Zr1), while average pore size expands from 3.34 nm (Zr0) to 7.82 nm (Zr2). The isotherms transition from type III (Zr0, Zr0.2, Zr1) to type IV (Zr2, Zrp), with hysteresis loops evolving from H4 (slit-like pores) to H2 (ink-bottle pores).
- **Observations:** The development of mesoporosity and increased surface area are attributed to ZrO₂’s inhibition of Fe₂O₃ grain growth, enhancing catalyst dispersion and providing more active sites for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Surface Chemistry
- **Description:** H₂-TPR and CO-TPR analyses were performed to probe the reduction behavior of Fe species, distinguishing between surface and bulk reducibility, which influences catalyst activation and performance.
- **Findings:** TPR profiles exhibit multiple reduction peaks, with the first (a) below 500 °C (H₂-TPR) or 350 °C (CO-TPR) corresponding to α-Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ reduction. Deconvolution yields a1 (surface) and a2 (bulk) components; the a1/a2 area ratio increases from 0.44 (Zr0) to 5.11 (Zr2), indicating a higher proportion of surface Fe₂O₃ with increasing Zr.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface reducibility is linked to smaller Fe₂O₃ particles and greater dispersion, facilitating more efficient catalyst activation.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** SEM and TEM were used to examine catalyst morphology and particle size distribution, which affect surface exposure and catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** SEM images show nanosphere morphologies, with average particle sizes decreasing from ~130 nm (Zr0) to ~25–30 nm (Zrp). Intermediate Zr/Fe ratios (Zr0.2, Zr1, Zr2) yield particles around 25 nm. TEM of Zr1 confirms high crystallinity and polycrystalline nature, with lattice spacings of 0.27 nm (α-Fe₂O₃ (104)) and 0.29 nm (ZrO₂ (111)).
- **Observations:** Zr addition effectively suppresses Fe₂O₃ grain growth, resulting in smaller, more uniform particles and improved catalyst dispersion.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Composition and Defect Structure
- **Description:** XPS was employed to analyze surface elemental composition, oxidation states, and defect structures, including oxygen vacancies.
- **Findings:** XPS reveals surface enrichment of Zr, with the surface Zr/Fe atomic ratio exceeding the bulk value. The Zr 3d₅/₂ binding energy shifts to lower values with increasing Fe content (except Zr2), attributed to electron transfer and increased oxygen vacancies. Fe is predominantly present as Fe³⁺, and K is detected on the surface, with its content decreasing as Zr increases.
- **Observations:** The presence of oxygen vacancies and surface Zr enrichment are associated with enhanced catalytic activity, providing additional active sites and facilitating CO activation.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})} - M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO})}}{M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})}} \times 100\%
  \]
  where \( M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})} \) and \( M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO})} \) are the inlet and outlet molar flows of CO, respectively, determined by on-line gas chromatography.
- **Experimental Data:** Under 270 °C, 1.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, and WHSV = 3 NL_H₂+CO g⁻¹ Fe h⁻¹, the CO conversion at 10 h on stream is:
  - Zr0: Peaks at ~5 h, then declines; lower initial activity.
  - Zr0.2: Higher and more stable conversion, peaking at ~10 h.
  - Zr1: Highest and most stable, peaking at ~30 h; reaches 82.1%.
  - Zr2: Peaks at ~50 h, then rapidly deactivates.
  - Zrp: Very low, dropping from 3.3% at 1 h to 1.0% at 11 h, then stabilizing below 1.0%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate Zr doping (Zr0.2, Zr1) significantly enhances CO conversion and stability, while excessive Zr (Zr2) or pure ZrO₂ (Zrp) results in poor activity and rapid deactivation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating water-gas-shift activity:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO}_2)}}{M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})} - M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO})}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 10 h on stream:
  - Zr0: 5.3%
  - Zr1: 48.2%
  - Zr2: 19.3%
  - Zrp: 1.0%
  - (Zr0.2: Value between Zr0 and Zr1)
- **Observations and Trends:** Zr1 exhibits markedly higher CO₂ selectivity, indicating enhanced water-gas-shift reaction. Excessive Zr or absence of Fe (Zrp) suppresses CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CH}_4)}}{M_{\text{in}}^{(\text{CO})} - M_{\text{out}}^{(\text{CO})}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** As Zr content increases from Zr0 to Zr1, CH₄ selectivity rises, attributed to increased H₂/CO ratio from the water-gas-shift reaction. Excessive Zr (Zr2, Zrp) does not further increase CH₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal Zr/Fe ratios favor moderate CH₄ formation; excessive Zr is detrimental.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbons:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \sum S_{C_n} \quad (n = 1,2,3,4)
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Increasing Zr content reduces C₃⁺ selectivity, indicating inhibition of chain growth and favoring lower olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** Zr1 achieves a balance, maximizing lower olefins while suppressing long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation in hydrocarbon formation; lower α favors shorter chains:
  - Calculated from product distribution using the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model.
- **Experimental Data:** Zr1 exhibits a lower α value, correlating with higher lower olefins selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal Zr/Fe ratios decrease α, enhancing selectivity for C₂–C₄ olefins.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Zr0.2 and Zr1 maintain stable CO conversion over 100 h, indicating slow deactivation. Zr2 shows rapid deactivation after 50 h; Zrp deactivates quickly within 11 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate Zr doping enhances catalyst stability, while excessive Zr accelerates deactivation due to carbon deposition and phase changes.

---
**Summary:** The co-precipitated Fe–Zr catalysts demonstrate that a Zr/Fe molar ratio of 1 (Zr1) optimizes CO conversion (82.1%), lower olefins selectivity, and catalyst stability, with balanced CO₂ and CH₄ selectivities. Excessive Zr content impairs performance and stability, underscoring the importance of precise dopant control.
