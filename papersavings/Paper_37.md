## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑Processing Steps

The Fe–Mn–K catalyst is synthesized via an Organic Combustion Method (OCM), which integrates both precursor mixing and in situ calcination to yield a carbon‐free powder. This method is favored for its simplicity, cost efficiency, and scalability compared to traditional routes such as co‑precipitation or impregnation that often require additional drying and calcination steps.

#### Organic Combustion Method

• Step 1: Precursor Mixing / Co‑Precipitation  
 - Reagents: Iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate, manganese(II) nitrate tetrahydrate, and potassium nitrate are used. Citric acid is added as both a complexing agent and fuel, at a molar ratio of 2:1 relative to the combined metal precursors, while the weight ratio of (precursors + citric acid) to water is maintained at 2:1.  
 - Equipment: A controlled stirring system ensures the formation of a homogeneous aqueous solution.  
 - Observations: A uniform solution is achieved with a slight color change confirming proper mixing.

• Step 2: Precipitation–Deposition  
 - Although a distinct precipitation–deposition step is common in other methods, here it is integrated with the mixing process. No separate deposition stage is needed prior to drying.

• Step 3: Drying  
 - The homogeneous solution is heated at 50°C for 1–2 hours using a temperature-controlled apparatus (e.g., a calibrated laboratory oven) to form a licit slurry.  
 - This step is critical for ensuring even distribution of metal complexes before ignition.

• Step 4: Calcination (Ignition)  
 - The slurry is ignited at 350°C in air for 4 hours in a programmable furnace equipped with a digital temperature controller.  
 - Observations: The high-temperature treatment combusts the citric acid completely, ensuring that no residual carbon remains in the final powder.
  
• Step 5: Post‑Treatment  
 - No additional drying or calcination is needed, as the combustion step simultaneously produces the active, carbon-free catalyst powder.  

### Comparative Analysis  
The OCM offers advantages such as reduced water usage, a one‑pot synthesis, and fewer processing steps, which contribute to lower costs and enhanced scalability. However, precise control during both the initial heating and ignition stages is essential to avoid any residual carbon and to ensure reproducibility. This method, by maintaining strict molar and weight ratios and by utilizing controlled temperature conditions throughout, enables other researchers to exactly replicate the synthesis process for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst preparation.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe–Mn–K Catalyst

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • Iron(III) Nitrate Nonahydrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, e.g., 10.0 g)  
  • Manganese(II) Nitrate Tetrahydrate (Mn(NO₃)₂·4H₂O, e.g., 2.0 g)  
  • Potassium Nitrate (KNO₃, e.g., 1.0 g)  
  • Citric Acid (serving as both complexing agent and fuel at a 2:1 molar ratio relative to the total metal precursors)  
  • Deionized water, maintaining a weight ratio of (precursors + citric acid)/water = 2:1  
- **Conditions**:  
  - Temperature: 50°C  
  - Stirring: Continuous for 1–2 hours to ensure complete dissolution and chelation  
- **Process**:  
  1. Combine the metal nitrates and citric acid in deionized water in accordance with the specified ratios to form a homogeneous aqueous solution.  
  2. Stir the mixture at 50°C for 1–2 hours, ensuring that all reagents fully dissolve and interact to form stable chelates.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Verify the homogeneity of the precursor solution; any deviation in ratios may affect the uniformity of subsequent combustion and catalyst performance.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly and Combustion
- **Reagents**:  
  - Utilize the homogenous precursor solution prepared in Step [1].  
- **Conditions**:  
  - Post-preparation slurry is heated under controlled conditions; ignition occurs at 350°C in an air atmosphere for 4 hours  
- **Process**:  
  1. Heat the uniform slurry to further consolidate the precursors into a cohesive paste.  
  2. Ignite the slurry at a controlled temperature of 350°C for 4 hours, which facilitates organic combustion, ensures effective calcination, and yields a carbon-free powder.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Strict temperature control during ignition is essential to achieve the desired phase transformation, notably the formation of the active χ-Fe₅C₂ phase.

##### Step [3]: Activation
- **Conditions**:  
  - Catalyst activation is performed in situ using syngas (H₂:CO = 2:1) or an H₂/CO₂ mixture with a flow rate of 40 mL/min at approximately 320°C for 24 hours to fully reduce the catalyst.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Place the calcined powder in a fixed-bed reactor and subject it to the specified syngas environment, ensuring complete reduction and carburization.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - The activation step is critical for achieving the tandem mechanism between χ-Fe₅C₂ and residual Fe₃O₄, optimizing the catalyst’s performance for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is designed to prepare the Fe–Mn–K catalyst for efficient Fischer–Tropsch reaction by converting the precursor into its active phases under controlled conditions. The following subsections detail the key activation parameters as derived from the experimental protocol.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for reducing iron oxide precursors to their active metallic or carbide forms.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 320 °C, ensuring sufficient thermal energy to drive the reduction.  
- **Observations:** This temperature promotes the rapid formation of active phases such as χ-Fe₅C₂ and Fe₃O₄, which are essential for optimal catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Pressure during activation influences the kinetics of the reduction reactions and the interaction between the gas phase and the catalyst surface.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The in situ activation is conducted at atmospheric pressure, ensuring a controlled environment that supports consistent reduction.  
- **Observations:** Maintaining atmospheric pressure helps avoid catalyst aggregation while promoting uniform activation.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
- **Definition:** The gas composition determines the reducing environment; in this case, syngas is used to induce the conversion of the precursor material.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with a hydrogen-to-carbon monoxide ratio of 2:1 is employed, ensuring effective reduction of iron oxides into active phases.  
- **Observations:** This specific composition enhances CO₂ conversion during subsequent hydrogenation, while also balancing the risk of over‐reduction.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity (gas flow rate per unit catalyst weight) controls the exposure time of the catalyst to the reducing gas, influencing the effectiveness of activation.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A gas hourly space velocity of 1000 mL/(gcat·h) is maintained, enabling a steady interaction between the catalyst and syngas.  
- **Observations:** This controlled flow rate mitigates incomplete reduction or over-reduction, contributing to catalyst stability.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time and Reactor Setup
- **Definition:** Activation duration is essential to ensure complete transformation of the catalyst precursor, while reactor configuration provides the necessary environment for the reduction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst, loaded at 2.0 g, is subjected to the activation process for 24 hours in a stainless steel fixed-bed reactor.  
- **Observations:** This extended period ensures full activation, resulting in immediate improvements in CO₂ and H₂ conversion upon subsequent reaction testing.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature governs the kinetics of Fischer–Tropsch synthesis by providing the necessary thermal energy for syngas activation and hydrocarbon chain growth.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were performed at a temperature of 300 °C.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 300 °C was critical to ensure catalyst stability over extended reaction times. This condition effectively balanced the activation energy for CO₂ conversion while mitigating rapid catalyst deactivation, as evidenced by stable product selectivity after an initial period.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure directly affects reactant concentration and plays an essential role in controlling the kinetics and equilibrium of the Fischer–Tropsch process.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reactor was maintained at a fixed pressure of 10 bar during the catalytic evaluation.  
- **Observations:** The selected pressure enhances syngas density, thereby improving CO₂ conversion rates and favoring the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons, which are crucial for jet fuel synthesis.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction Gas Ratio (H₂/CO₂)  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO₂ ratio is essential for dictating the balance between hydrogenation and chain growth in Fischer–Tropsch reactions.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A strict H₂/CO₂ ratio of 3:1 was employed during the reaction step.  
- **Observations:** This specific ratio was optimized to promote the synthesis of liquid hydrocarbons while suppressing unwanted byproducts, such as methane. Notably, methane selectivity was observed to decrease significantly from 30% to 10% as the reaction stabilized.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) describes the volumetric flow rate of syngas per unit mass of catalyst, essentially controlling the reactant residence time.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was set at 2400 mL/(gcat·h).  
- **Observations:** This flow rate ensured that the reactants had sufficient time to interact with the catalyst while preventing overexposure that might lead to reduced conversion efficiency, thus maintaining the overall catalyst performance during extended runs.

Collectively, these well-defined reaction parameters underpin the reproducibility of the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation and highlight the Fe–Mn–K catalyst’s capacity for efficient CO₂ hydrogenation into jet fuel range hydrocarbons.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases present in the Fe–Mn–K catalyst were identified using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for determining the active species responsible for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) activity and selectivity.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis of the catalyst precursor revealed reflections corresponding to Fe₃O₄ (magnetite), indicating partial reduction of Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ during synthesis via the Organic Combustion Method. Upon in situ activation with syngas (CO/H₂ = 1:2), all XRD reflections were assigned to χ-Fe₅C₂ (Hägg carbide), confirming complete carburization of Fe₃O₄. After reaction, the used catalyst exhibited a mixture of Fe₃O₄, Fe₂O₃, and χ-Fe₅C₂ phases, reflecting dynamic phase transformations under reaction conditions.
- **Observations:** The formation of χ-Fe₅C₂ is widely recognized as critical for C–C chain growth in FTS, while the presence of Fe₃O₄ supports the reverse water–gas shift reaction, generating CO for subsequent hydrocarbon synthesis. The tandem mechanism involving both χ-Fe₅C₂ and Fe₃O₄ underpins the catalyst’s high activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Valence States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to determine the surface elemental composition and oxidation states, providing insight into the chemical environment of active sites.
- **Findings:** XPS survey spectra confirmed the presence of Fe, Mn, K, and O. High-resolution Fe 2p spectra displayed two spin-orbit doublets (Fe 2p₃/₂ and Fe 2p₁/₂) with a binding energy gap of 13.7 eV and a shakeup satellite characteristic of Fe³⁺, consistent with Fe₃O₄. The Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ molar ratio was measured as 1:2.38, closely matching Fe₃O₄ stoichiometry. The Mn 2p region showed a spin-orbit doublet with a binding energy gap of 11.6 eV, assigned to Mn₂O₃. The O 1s peak at 529.4 eV was attributed to metal–O bonds. The C 1s region revealed 40% C sp² (284.7 eV), 15% C=O (288.4 eV), and 45% C sp³ (285.3 eV).
- **Observations:** The presence of FeCx (χ-Fe₅C₂) is crucial for catalytic performance, facilitating CO and CO₂ hydrogenation. Mn promotion enhances surface basicity and carburization, supporting higher selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons and suppressing methane formation.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) were used to examine catalyst morphology and nanoparticle size, which influence catalytic activity and stability.
- **Findings:** SEM images of the precursor showed closely packed, regular particles, while the used catalyst exhibited morphological changes post-reaction. STEM-BF images revealed nanoparticles with an average size of ~15 nm, with lattice spacings corresponding to the (311) and (220) planes of Fe₃O₄. Crystallite sizes calculated via the Scherrer equation from XRD data were consistent with these observations. No significant change in particle size was observed after reaction, indicating structural stability.
- **Observations:** The small, uniform nanoparticle size achieved through the Organic Combustion Method is linked to enhanced catalytic performance and durability. The persistence of nanoscale features and active phases after reaction underscores the robustness of the Fe–Mn–K catalyst under FTS conditions.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 300 °C, 10 bar, and a GHSV of 2400 mL g⁻¹ h⁻¹ (H₂/CO₂ = 3:1), the Fe–Mn–K catalyst achieved a CO₂ conversion of 38.2%. Conversion increased rapidly within the first 5 hours and stabilized near 40% over extended operation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst demonstrates efficient activation and rapid attainment of steady-state conversion, indicating robust performance under the tested conditions.

#### 6.2.2 Product Selectivity

##### 6.2.2.1 Jet Fuel Range Hydrocarbons (C₈–C₁₆)
- **Description:** Selectivity to jet fuel range hydrocarbons reflects the proportion of converted CO₂ forming C₈–C₁₆ products:
\[
\text{C}_8\text{–C}_{16}\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_8\text{–C}_{16},\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe–Mn–K catalyst exhibited a selectivity of 47.8% for C₈–C₁₆ hydrocarbons, with a corresponding yield of 17.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** This high selectivity surpasses that of Fe–Cu–K (40.8%) and Fe–Zn–K (45.1%) analogues, confirming the superior performance of Mn as a promoter for jet fuel synthesis.

##### 6.2.2.2 Methane (CH₄) Selectivity
- **Description:** Methane selectivity is defined as:
\[
\text{CH}_4\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity decreased from 30% at the start of the reaction to 10.4% after 20 hours, remaining stable thereafter.
- **Observations and Trends:** The rapid suppression of methane formation is attributed to the Mn promoter, which favors chain growth and reduces methanation.

##### 6.2.2.3 Light Olefins (C₂–C₄) Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity for light olefins (C₂–C₄) is calculated as the fraction of converted CO₂ yielding these products.
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe–Mn–K catalyst achieved a light olefin yield of 8.7% and a selectivity of 25%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst maintains a favorable balance between light olefin and long-chain hydrocarbon production.

##### 6.2.2.4 Carbon Monoxide (CO) Selectivity
- **Description:** CO selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO₂ forming CO.
- **Experimental Data:** CO selectivity was measured at 5.6% after 20 hours of reaction.

#### 6.2.3 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, indicates the probability of hydrocarbon chain propagation:
\[
\alpha = \text{slope of ASF plot (log(mole fraction) vs. carbon number)}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For the Fe–Mn–K catalyst, α₁ = 0.79 for C₁–C₁₂ hydrocarbons and α₂ = 0.57 for C₁₂⁺ hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high α₁ value reflects a strong tendency toward long-chain hydrocarbon formation, aligning with the observed jet fuel selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring conversion and selectivity over time. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** After an initial stabilization period (first 5–10 hours), CO₂ conversion and product selectivities remained nearly constant over 20–40 hours, indicating minimal deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe–Mn–K catalyst exhibits excellent stability, with negligible loss in activity or selectivity during prolonged operation.
