## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the microemulsion method employed to synthesize Fischer–Tropsch catalysts such as 100Fe/4Cu, 100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba. The method is designed for reproducibility by providing precise reagent concentrations, temperatures, durations, and equipment details.

#### Microemulsion-Based Catalyst Synthesis

• Step 1: Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
 – An aqueous solution of FeCl₃·6H₂O is prepared alongside an oil phase containing 1‑butanol and chloroform.  
 – Sodium dodecyl sulfate acts as a surfactant to stabilize the microemulsion.  
 – Hydrazine is introduced as the precipitating agent while stirring continuously for 4 hours.  
 – Catalyst promoters (Cu, La, Ba) are incorporated with specific ratios (e.g., 100Fe/4Cu, 2 wt% La, or 1 wt% La with 1 wt% Ba) during the mixing.

• Step 2: Precipitation–Deposition  
 – Following initial mixing, a controlled dropwise addition of support precursors is performed.  
 – The mixture is stirred for an additional 1 hour at ambient temperature to promote uniform deposition of promoters and ensure a homogeneous dispersion of metal precursors.

• Step 3: Drying  
 – The resulting solid is recovered via centrifugation and sequentially washed with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.  
 – Drying is conducted at 110 °C for 15 hours in an oven to remove all residual solvents.

• Step 4: Calcination  
 – The dried precursor is calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 hours.  
 – A furnace equipped with a temperature controller ensures precise heating to form the desired cubic hematite structure.

• Step 5: Post‑calcination Reduction  
 – Reduction is performed under a 10% H₂–90% N₂ atmosphere at 400 °C for 3 hours.  
 – This step is crucial for the formation of active Fe nanoparticles, with Cu facilitating the reduction process.

### Comparative Analysis of the Method

The microemulsion method enables controlled nanoscale formation of the iron catalyst, ensuring uniform morphology and high surface area. Advantages include the precise regulation of synthesis conditions and effective incorporation of promoters, whereas challenges such as crystallite aggregation may affect BET surface areas and pore volumes. This detailed protocol facilitates reproducibility and consistent catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe/4Cu

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O and an appropriate Cu precursor (e.g., Cu(NO₃)₂·xH₂O) are used. An aqueous solution is prepared in a 1:1 mixture of 1-butanol and chloroform with sodium dodecyl sulfate as the surfactant.  
- **Conditions**: Stir the mixture at room temperature for 4 hours to ensure full dissolution.  
- **Process**: Combine the salts with the solvent mixture, generating a homogeneous microemulsion where all primary precursors are well dissolved.  
- **Key Steps**: Verify complete dissolution before initiating catalyst assembly.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**: The solution from Step 1 is now combined with the Cu precursor solution.  
- **Conditions**: Continue stirring for 4 hours and allow the mixture to decant overnight.  
- **Process**: Mix the precursors thoroughly; centrifuge the decanted mixture and wash with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone to remove impurities.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure uniform integration of the Fe and Cu precursors.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**: Dry the recovered solid in an oven at 110 °C for 15 hours to avoid nanoparticle agglomeration.  
- **Calcination**: Calcine in air at 400 °C for 4 hours for formation of the cubic hematite phase.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain consistent temperature control to ensure phase purity.

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Conditions**: Activate in a fixed-bed reactor under a 10% H₂–90% N₂ stream at 270 °C for 3 hours.  
- **Process**: This reduction step converts oxides to active Fe species and ensures proper dispersion of nanoparticles.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is carried out as a two-step procedure that comprises an initial reduction step followed by an activation under synthesis gas conditions. This method is designed specifically for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts to ensure that the iron precursors are fully converted into active phases while maintaining controlled metal dispersion.

#### 5.1.1 Catalyst Reduction Step
• **Definition:** This pre-treatment stage reduces the iron oxide precursors using a controlled reducing atmosphere, facilitating the formation of active iron phases.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to a gas mixture of 10% H₂ and 90% N₂ for 3 hours in a fixed-bed reactor.  
• **Observations:** This reduction step is critical in transforming the hematite to metallic or lower oxidation state iron, setting the stage for further activation without inducing sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Temperature
• **Definition:** The temperature during activation is pivotal for promoting the desired surface reactions and formation of active iron carbide species.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is performed at 270 °C under synthesis gas conditions.  
• **Observations:** Operating at this moderate temperature supports effective reduction while minimizing the risk of excessive carbon deposition or sintering.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Pressure
• **Definition:** Pressure influences gas–solid interactions and reduction kinetics during catalyst activation.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is conducted under atmospheric pressure.  
• **Observations:** This condition ensures a controlled reduction environment that limits rapid structural changes in the catalyst.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Gas Composition
• **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas is crucial to balance the reduction and carburization processes.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** A synthesis gas stream with an equimolar H₂/CO ratio (1:1) is used.  
• **Observations:** This balanced composition fosters the formation of productive active sites without over-hydrogenating the catalyst.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Space-Time Velocity and Time
• **Definition:** The space-time velocity controls the catalyst’s exposure to the activation gas, while the overall activation time determines the completeness of the process.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** A gas space velocity of 1.5 nl/(h·g) is maintained, and the catalyst is activated for 24 hours.  
• **Observations:** The selected gas flow rate and duration ensure uniform gas distribution across the catalyst bed, promoting full activation with minimal risk of sintering, thereby optimizing catalyst performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer-Tropsch catalytic evaluation was conducted under a set of well-defined and reproducible conditions that ensure optimal activation of syngas and effective catalyst performance. The following subsections describe the reaction parameters in detail.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for promoting the activation of CO and H₂, facilitating the hydrogenation reactions that lead to hydrocarbon chain growth during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reactor was maintained at 290 °C throughout the catalytic tests.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 290 °C enhances syngas activation, contributing to improved catalyst performance and higher hydrocarbon yields.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the concentration of syngas, thereby affecting both the reaction rate and product selectivity.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was carried out at a total system pressure of 1.8 MPa (equivalent to 18 bar) in a fixed-bed stainless-steel reactor.  
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure increases the availability of reactants on the catalyst surface, promoting the formation of heavier hydrocarbons while maintaining controlled reaction kinetics.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is vital in balancing the hydrogenation and chain growth reactions inherent to the Fischer-Tropsch process.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A feed gas mixture with an H₂/CO ratio of approximately 1.1 was used for the catalytic tests.  
- **Observations:** This stoichiometric ratio favors the production of longer-chain hydrocarbons, optimizing the reaction by reducing excessive methane formation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity (represented by the Gas Hourly Space Velocity, GHSV) determines the residence time of the reactants over the catalyst, directly impacting CO conversion and product distribution.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was set at 3 nl/(g·h) to ensure a precise balance between reactant flow and catalyst mass.  
- **Observations:** This parameter provides a controlled exposure of reactants to active sites, which, coupled with a 72-hour time-on-stream, maintains catalyst stability and consistent performance during extended runs.

These reaction conditions, implemented in a fixed-bed stainless-steel reactor with electronic mass flow and pressure controls, ensure high reproducibility and form the basis for the observed catalytic performance enhancements.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for confirming the formation of the desired iron oxide phase prior to reduction and for assessing the impact of promoters on phase stability.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of all catalysts after calcination revealed a cubic hematite (Fe₂O₃) structure, with the characteristic (104) reflection at 2θ = 33.3°. No additional phases were detected upon the incorporation of Cu, La, or Ba promoters, indicating that the hematite phase was preserved across all samples.
- **Observations:** The presence of a well-defined hematite structure ensures proper transformation to catalytically active iron phases during reduction, which is critical for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis activity.

#### 6.1.2 Particle Size and Morphology
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to assess the morphology and particle size distribution of the catalysts, providing direct visualization of nanoscale features that influence catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed that the iron nanoparticles in all catalyst samples exhibited a uniform size distribution in the range of 10–30 nm. No significant morphological differences were observed among the variously promoted catalysts.
- **Observations:** The nanoscale particle size, confirmed by both TEM and XRD (via the Scherrer equation), supports a high surface area-to-volume ratio, which is beneficial for maximizing the number of active sites available for the Fischer-Tropsch reaction.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was conducted to determine the specific surface area and pore volume, parameters that affect reactant accessibility and catalyst dispersion.
- **Findings:** The addition of La, Ba, or their combination to the 100Fe/4Cu catalyst resulted in a decrease in both BET surface area and pore volume. This trend was attributed to the aggregation of catalyst crystallites and partial pore blockage induced by the promoters.
- **Observations:** Although promoter addition led to denser catalyst structures, the resulting decrease in porosity did not adversely affect catalytic performance, likely due to enhanced surface properties imparted by the promoters.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Basicity
- **Description:** CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) was used to evaluate the surface basicity, which influences CO adsorption and activation during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** Promoted catalysts exhibited significantly higher CO₂ desorption compared to the non-promoted sample, indicating increased surface basicity. The order of basicity was 100Fe/4Cu < 100Fe/4Cu/2La < 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba < 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba.
- **Observations:** Enhanced basicity, especially in the dual-promoted catalyst, is associated with improved CO adsorption and activation, contributing to higher catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.5 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) profiles provided insights into the reducibility of metal oxides and the effect of promoters on reduction temperatures.
- **Findings:** The reduction process occurred in distinct steps: initial reduction of CuO to Cu at lower temperatures, followed by Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄, and finally Fe₃O₄ to metallic Fe. The presence of La and Ba promoters increased the reduction temperature, indicating stronger metal-support interactions.
- **Observations:** These reduction steps are crucial for catalyst activation, as the formation of metallic Fe is necessary for Fischer-Tropsch activity. The altered reducibility due to promoters is linked to improved catalytic performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet CO molar flow rates, respectively, determined by gas chromatography.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS) conditions (290 °C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.1, GHSV 3 nl/(g·h), 72 h), the following CO conversions were observed:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 59.6%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 71.8%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 68.5%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 79.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The dual-promoted 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba catalyst exhibited the highest CO conversion, demonstrating a synergistic effect of La and Ba promoters in enhancing CO adsorption and activation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift (WGS) reaction. It is defined as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity values after 72 h on stream:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 17.29%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 21.67%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 23.41%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 25.03%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity increased with promoter addition, particularly for Ba and the dual-promoted catalyst, indicating enhanced WGS activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Reported CH₄ selectivities:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.96%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 12.32%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 10.44%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 10.81%
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of La and Ba, especially in combination, suppressed methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of chain growth. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \text{(CO}_2 + \text{CH}_4 + 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_2 + 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_6) \text{ Selectivities}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivities:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 54.2%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 59.7%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 62.1%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 61.3%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition, particularly Ba and the La/Ba combination, increased C₅⁺ selectivity, indicating improved chain growth.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
\[
\alpha = \frac{\text{moles of C}_{n+1}}{\text{moles of C}_n}
\]
Higher α values favor longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 0.61
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 0.66
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 0.71
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 0.68
- **Observations and Trends:** The dual-promoted and Ba-promoted catalysts exhibited higher α values, correlating with increased selectivity for heavier hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability was assessed by monitoring CO conversion over 72 h. Deactivation rate is defined as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time on Stream}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** All promoted catalysts maintained stable CO conversion over 72 h, with negligible deactivation observed for 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba.
- **Observations and Trends:** The dual-promoted catalyst demonstrated superior stability, attributed to enhanced active site properties and resistance to sintering or carbon deposition.
