## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fe–Ce Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were synthesized using two distinct methods: batchwise coprecipitation and physical mixing. Both approaches emphasize reproducibility and precise control of synthesis parameters.

• Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques  
 - The batchwise coprecipitation method allows for homogeneous mixing of 1 M aqueous solutions of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O.  
 - A 5.6 M NH₄OH solution is added simultaneously using a perfusion pump (50 mL/h) to maintain a constant pH of 8.0.  
 - In contrast, the physical mixture is prepared by fine grinding the separately dried precursors, followed by calcination, thereby lacking the intimate Fe–Ce interaction observed in the coprecipitated series.

### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
 • Combine 1 M Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and 1 M Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O solutions in the required ratios (e.g., 100Fe, 95Fe, 85Fe, 50Fe, and pure Ce).  
 • Use a perfusion pump (Becton Dickinson SE 400) to add these solutions into 500 mL of distilled water maintained at 343 K.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
 • Simultaneously add 5.6 M NH₄OH to control the pH at 8.0.  
 • Allow the resulting precipitate to form as the metal hydroxides deposit, a critical step before subsequent processing.

3. **Drying**  
 • Age the precipitate under stirring for 16 h at 343 K.  
 • Filter and wash the solid using successive portions of distilled water at 323 K.  
 • Dry the washed precipitate in air at 333 K for 24 h.

4. **Calcination**  
 • Calcine the dried catalyst precursor in ambient air at 573 K for 6 h to develop the hematite-like and cubic CeO₂ phases.

5. **Physical Mixture (Alternate Process)**  
 • Finely grind the individually prepared pure precursors to obtain a uniform blend.  
 • Calcine the grounded mixture at 573 K for 6 h to achieve the final physical catalyst structure.

This structured procedure, with precise numerical conditions and equipment details, ensures reproducibility and efficient synthesis of catalysts optimized for Fischer–Tropsch applications.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

The preparation of catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis follows two distinct routes: a batchwise coprecipitation process for the Fe–Ce mixed oxides (100Fe, 95Fe, 85Fe, 50Fe, and Ce) and a physical mixing method (PM50Fe and PM85Fe). The following subsections detail the steps for weighing of solid reagents, liquid transfer, and mixing as explicitly described in the text.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

For all catalyst batches, the precursors were weighed before solution preparation.  
  
##### 3.1.1.1 Coprecipitated Catalysts (100Fe, 95Fe, 85Fe, 50Fe, Ce)  
• The solid reagents – Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O – were measured out using an analytical balance. Specific masses and precision details are not provided in the manuscript.

##### 3.1.1.2 Physically Mixed Catalysts (PM50Fe, PM85Fe)  
• Pure Fe and Ce precursor solids were weighed prior to physical blending. No explicit quantities or balance specifications are mentioned.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

The liquid transfer step applies only to the coprecipitated catalysts.  
  
##### 3.1.2.1 Coprecipitated Catalysts (100Fe, 95Fe, 85Fe, 50Fe, Ce)  
• **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O Solutions**: Transferred simultaneously at a constant rate of 50 mL/h using a perfusion pump (Becton Dickinson SE 400) into a 500 mL distilled water batch maintained at 343 K.  
• **NH₄OH Solution**: A 5.6 M NH₄OH solution was added concurrently to maintain a constant pH of 8.0 during precipitation.

##### 3.1.2.2 Physically Mixed Catalysts (PM50Fe, PM85Fe)  
• No liquid transfer is involved, as the synthesis involves direct blending of the preformed solid precursors.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

Mixing procedures differ between the two synthesis approaches.  
  
##### 3.1.3.1 Coprecipitated Catalysts (100Fe, 95Fe, 85Fe, 50Fe, Ce)  
• **Precipitate Suspension**: After reagent addition, the reaction mixture was stirred continuously and aged for 16 hours at 343 K to promote homogeneity.  
• **Post-Aging**: The precipitate was subsequently filtered, washed with water heated to 323 K, and dried in air at 333 K for 24 hours before undergoing calcination at 573 K for 6 hours.

##### 3.1.3.2 Physically Mixed Catalysts (PM50Fe, PM85Fe)  
• **Solid Mixture**: The dried Fe and Ce precursors were finely ground and blended to achieve a uniform mixture prior to calcination at 573 K for 6 hours.

This detailed description of the weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing steps provides a reproducible framework for catalyst preparation as documented in the experimental procedure.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Coprecipitated Fe–Ce Mixed Oxide Catalyst (e.g., 95Fe)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 1 M Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and 1 M Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O solutions; 5.6 M NH₄OH solution.
- **Conditions**: The 500 mL distilled water batch is maintained at 343 K. A perfusion pump introduces the metal nitrate solutions at a constant rate of 50 mL/h while a pH-stationary device keeps the pH at 8.0 throughout the process.
- **Process**:
  - Simultaneously add the Fe and Ce solutions into the batch while slowly introducing NH₄OH to adjust pH.
  - Ensure vigorous stirring throughout to achieve homogeneous precipitation.
  - Allow the precipitate to age under continuous stirring for 16 hours at 343 K, ensuring complete formation and stabilization of the metal hydroxides.
- **Key Steps**: Precise control of reagent addition and pH during precipitation is critical for forming an intimate Fe–Ce interaction.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly & Purification
- **Process**:
  - Isolate the precipitate by filtration and wash with successive 400 mL portions of distilled water heated to 323 K to remove residual ions.
  - For physically mixed catalysts (e.g., PM85Fe), blend finely ground pure Fe and Ce precursors in the required ratio prior to the thermal treatment.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform washing and controlled removal of impurities ensure purity and consistency.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Air-dry the washed precipitate in an oven at 333 K for 24 hours.
- **Calcination**: Calcine the dried material at 573 K for 6 hours in ambient air. This step promotes the formation of a stable mixed oxide phase and enhances Fe–Ce solid-state interactions.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Process**: Activate the calcined catalyst in situ by reducing under H₂ (using a H₂/N₂ mixture, typically at 673 K for 16 hours). This reduction step is essential to generate active Fe sites necessary for Fischer–Tropsch performance.

**Special Cautions & Key Considerations**:  
Maintain constant temperature, pH, and stirring rates throughout each step. The reproducibility of the catalyst microstructure critically depends on tight control of reagent addition and post-precipitation aging conditions. This workflow, beginning from raw chemical precursors and proceeding through purification, drying, calcination, and finally activation, ensures high precision and reliability for catalyst synthesis.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to determine the crystalline phases of the catalysts, identifying hematite‐like (R‑Fe₂O₃) and cubic CeO₂ structures. Finely ground samples, sometimes mixed with powdered Si as an internal standard, were analyzed in Bragg–Brentano θ/2θ geometry.
- **Instruments and Models**: Seifert 3000 diffractometer equipped with a PW goniometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were conducted over a 2θ range of 5–80°.
- **Test Parameters**: A step size of 0.02° and a scanning rate corresponding to 2 seconds per step were used. Calibration via powdered Si ensured accurate peak position determination.
- **Key Notes**: Meticulous sample mounting and homogeneous powder layering are crucial to accurately differentiate broadened or amorphous reflections from well-defined crystalline peaks.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: The surface area and porosity were quantified via nitrogen adsorption isotherms.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2000 apparatus.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed at 77 K (liquid nitrogen temperature).
- **Test Parameters**: Samples were degassed at 413 K for 12 hours prior to analysis to remove residual moisture.
- **Key Notes**: The reliable removal of adsorbed species via degassing is essential to ensure accurate BET data which is later correlated with catalyst composition.

### 4.3 Raman Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: Raman spectroscopy assisted in phase identification, complementing XRD data through vibrational mode detection.
- **Instruments and Models**: Renishaw 1000 spectrophotometer with a cooled CCD detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were pretreated in situ at 523 K for 30 minutes under air, and subsequently analyzed at 473 K.
- **Test Parameters**: Excitation used a 514-nm Ar laser with spectral acquisition comprising 5 accumulations of 60 seconds each.
- **Key Notes**: Special attention is given to potential signal attenuation due to strong absorption in hematite, impacting sample depth and spectral intensity.

### 4.4 Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: This technique distinguished between iron in the hematite structure and iron within a CeO₂-like matrix. Measurements provided insights into hyperfine magnetic interactions.
- **Instruments and Models**: A constant-acceleration spectrometer employing a 57Co (Rh) source.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Spectra were recorded at room temperature and, for select samples, at low temperatures (10–100 K) using a closed-cycle He cryogenerator.
- **Test Parameters**: Absorbers were prepared to achieve an effective thickness of about 10 mg cm⁻² of natural Fe. Data analysis involved fitting Lorentzian line profiles and hyperfine magnetic field distributions.
- **Key Notes**: This method effectively differentiates between sextet and doublet signals, correlating with distinct iron environments in the catalysts.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The in situ activation of the Fe–Ce Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is performed directly within a fixed-bed microreactor to ensure that the catalyst’s active phases are properly developed prior to the reaction. This procedure is a critical pre-reaction treatment focused solely on catalyst synthesis, distinct from any activation steps used for characterization.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
• **Definition:** The activation temperature is fundamental for inducing the reduction and partial carburization of the calcined iron oxides, thereby exposing the active sites essential for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst sample is heated to 673 K at a controlled ramp rate of 10 K/min.  
• **Observations:** This precise thermal treatment facilitates the formation of Fe carbide-like phases and enhances Fe–Ce interactions without causing sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
• **Definition:** Maintaining an appropriate pressure during activation influences gas–catalyst interactions and the kinetics of reduction.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is carried out at atmospheric pressure, ensuring a stable environment for catalyst reduction.  
• **Observations:** Operating at atmospheric pressure helps in avoiding uneven heat distribution and potential agglomeration of catalyst particles.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
• **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas determines the extent of reduction achieved via hydrogen, while nitrogen moderates the process by diluting the reducing atmosphere.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to a H₂/N₂ mixture in a 1:2 ratio. Hydrogen is delivered at a flow rate of 5.9 NL/min using a mass flow controller, ensuring a consistent supply throughout the activation period.  
• **Observations:** The presence of hydrogen effectively reduces the calcined oxides, while the nitrogen component helps prevent overheating, thereby ensuring a controlled environment for catalyst activation.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
• **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is crucial to allow complete transformation of the catalyst, ensuring full reduction and the formation of active Fe–Ce interfaces.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst remains under the aforementioned conditions for 16 hours.  
• **Observations:** This extended activation period guarantees thorough catalyst restructuring, critically influencing the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction performance through improved conversion rates and product selectivities.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch reaction conditions were rigorously defined to ensure reproducibility and optimal catalytic performance using the Fe–Ce catalysts. Each parameter was carefully selected to provide a stable reactive environment in the fixed-bed microreactor system.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The catalytic reaction was maintained at 573 K. This temperature facilitates optimal syngas activation and promotes the conversion of CO and H₂ into higher hydrocarbons. Maintaining 573 K is critical as it ensures that the kinetic parameters remain constant over the reaction duration, enhancing catalyst performance and product consistency.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Experiments were conducted at a reaction pressure of 1.01 MPa. This elevated pressure increases the concentration of synthesis gas within the catalyst bed, thereby enhancing reaction rates and influencing the overall selectivity of hydrocarbon products. The controlled pressure condition fosters efficient contact between the catalyst and the reactants, which is essential for favorable catalytic activity.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The feed gas for the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was prepared with a H₂/CO ratio of 1:1. This stoichiometric balance is pivotal in ensuring that the reaction proceeds with a consistent distribution of reactant molecules, thereby influencing the product slate toward a desired range of hydrocarbons. The choice of a 1:1 ratio also supports reproducibility in scaling the reaction process.

#### 5.2.4 Reactor Configuration and Space-Time Velocity
A fixed-bed microreactor, measuring 150 mm in length and 9 mm in inner diameter, was employed for the catalytic tests. The reactor’s design contributes to efficient heat and mass transfer, ensuring precise temperature control and uniform reactant distribution. The gas hourly space velocity, measured at 0.0043 L·g⁻¹·s⁻¹, sets the contact time between the reactants and catalyst, which is vital for achieving steady CO conversion rates and stable product formation over the course of the reaction.

These carefully extracted and defined parameters streamline the experimental procedure, allowing for direct reproduction of the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions reported in the article.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural features of the Fe–Ce catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman spectroscopy. XRD identifies the presence and crystallinity of hematite-like (R-Fe₂O₃) and cubic CeO₂ (fluorite-type) phases, while Raman spectroscopy provides complementary information on local structure and phase mixing.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of pure Fe (100Fe) display only hematite reflections, and pure Ce (Ce sample) shows only cubic CeO₂ reflections. Physically mixed samples (PM50Fe, PM85Fe) exhibit reflections from both phases, indicating phase segregation. In contrast, coprecipitated samples show broadened and less intense reflections, especially at intermediate Fe/Ce ratios (e.g., 85Fe and 50Fe), signifying smaller crystallite sizes and/or microstrain. The 85Fe sample is highly amorphous, with weak, broad reflections assignable to both phases, while the 50Fe sample shows only broadened CeO₂-like reflections. Raman spectra corroborate these assignments, with pure CeO₂ showing a strong 460 cm⁻¹ band and pure Fe₂O₃ displaying bands at 218, 286, 404, and 604 cm⁻¹. Mixed samples exhibit broader, less intense features, indicating the formation of solid solutions or amorphous mixed oxides.
- **Observations:** Coprecipitation leads to the formation of mixed oxides or solid solutions with reduced crystallite size and enhanced structural disorder, in contrast to the phase segregation observed in physical mixtures.

#### 6.1.2 Iron Local Environment and Valence State
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to probe the local environment and oxidation state of Fe species, distinguishing between Fe in hematite-like and ceria-like environments.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra of pure Fe oxide (100Fe) and physical mixtures show a hyperfine magnetic sextet characteristic of hematite. Coprecipitated samples display both a sextet (hematite-like Fe³⁺) and a paramagnetic doublet, the latter attributed to high-spin Fe³⁺ ions dissolved in the ceria lattice (CeO₂-like solid solution). The doublet dominates in samples with higher Ce content (e.g., 50Fe), indicating significant Fe incorporation into the ceria structure.
- **Observations:** The coexistence and relative intensities of sextet and doublet components reflect the extent of Fe–Ce interaction and the formation of mixed oxide phases, with higher Ce content promoting Fe dispersion within the ceria matrix.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Textural Properties
- **Description:** Specific surface area was measured by nitrogen adsorption using the BET method, providing insight into the textural properties relevant to catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** BET analysis reveals a volcano-type dependency of surface area on Ce content. The 95Fe and 85Fe coprecipitated samples exhibit markedly higher surface areas—up to fourfold greater than pure Fe—while further Ce addition (50Fe) leads to a decrease, though still above the values for pure Fe or Ce. Measurements were performed at 77 K after degassing at 413 K for 12 hours, with surface area values reported in m²/g.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface area in coprecipitated samples is attributed to inhibited crystal growth and improved dispersion, both of which are facilitated by the coprecipitation method and the presence of Ce.

#### 6.1.4 Phase Homogeneity and Mixed Oxide Formation
- **Description:** The integration of XRD, Raman, BET, and Mössbauer results provides a comprehensive assessment of phase homogeneity and the extent of mixed oxide formation.
- **Findings:** Coprecipitated catalysts exhibit features consistent with true mixed oxides or solid solutions, including broadened XRD and Raman features, high surface area, and Mössbauer doublets indicative of Fe in ceria-like environments. Physical mixtures, by contrast, retain distinct Fe and Ce oxide phases with lower surface areas and no evidence of solid solution formation.
- **Observations:** The coprecipitation method is critical for achieving intimate Fe–Ce mixing, leading to improved dispersion, smaller crystallite size, and the formation of catalytically favorable mixed oxide phases.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** After 5700 min time-on-stream (TOS) at 573 K and 1.01 MPa, the coprecipitated 85Fe catalyst exhibited the highest CO conversion, nearly double that of the pure Fe (100Fe) catalyst. The 95Fe sample also showed enhanced conversion, while the 50Fe and Ce-only samples displayed significantly lower activity. Physically mixed catalysts (PM85Fe, PM50Fe) performed similarly to the linear combination of their pure components, lacking the synergistic effect observed in coprecipitated samples.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior performance of the 85Fe catalyst is attributed to the Fe–Ce interaction achieved via coprecipitation, which enhances dispersion and increases the number of active sites. The trend follows a volcano-type relationship with Ce content, peaking at 15 at.% Ce.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Coprecipitated catalysts with Ce (especially 85Fe and 95Fe) exhibited slightly higher CO₂ selectivity compared to pure Fe, reflecting increased water-gas shift activity. Physically mixed catalysts did not show significant changes relative to pure Fe.
- **Observations and Trends:** The increase in CO₂ selectivity correlates with the presence of Ce, suggesting that Fe–O–Ce bonding facilitates the water-gas shift reaction alongside Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The 85Fe coprecipitated catalyst demonstrated higher CH₄ formation rates than both pure Fe and physically mixed catalysts. The physically mixed samples (PM85Fe, PM50Fe) showed CH₄ selectivity similar to or slightly below that of pure Fe.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced CH₄ selectivity in coprecipitated samples is linked to improved Fe dispersion and increased active site availability due to Fe–Ce interactions.

#### 6.2.4 Hydrocarbon (CH₂) Formation Rate
- **Description:** The CH₂ formation rate represents the overall hydrocarbon production, measured as the rate of CH₂ unit formation per gram of catalyst.
- **Experimental Data:** The 85Fe catalyst achieved a CH₂ formation rate nearly twice that of the pure Fe catalyst after 5700 min TOS. The 50Fe sample, while having a lower total product rate, exhibited the highest intrinsic activity per gram of Fe.
- **Observations and Trends:** The increased hydrocarbon formation rate is directly associated with higher surface area and Fe–Ce synergy in coprecipitated catalysts.

#### 6.2.5 Selectivity to Hydrocarbon Fractions and Olefinicity
- **Description:** Selectivity to C₂–C₅, C₆–C₁₂, and C₁₂⁺ fractions, as well as olefinicity (percentage of olefins in total hydrocarbons), provides insight into product distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** Coprecipitated catalysts, particularly 85Fe, showed increased selectivity toward olefins and maintained or slightly enhanced selectivity for heavier hydrocarbons compared to pure Fe. The Ce-only sample was nearly inactive.
- **Observations and Trends:** Ce addition by coprecipitation increases olefinicity and sustains the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons, reflecting improved chain growth and product diversity.

#### 6.2.6 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory (ASF) distribution, indicates the probability of chain propagation:
  - Higher α values (closer to 1) favor longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Data:** Coprecipitated catalysts with Ce exhibited higher α values than pure Fe, consistent with increased selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons and olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** The enhanced α factor in Fe–Ce coprecipitated catalysts is attributed to improved Fe dispersion and Fe–O–Ce bonding.

#### 6.2.7 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. Deactivation is inferred from the change in conversion between initial and later TOS (e.g., after 5700 min).
- **Experimental Data:** All catalysts showed initial deactivation, followed by stabilization or increased activity. The 85Fe catalyst maintained high conversion and product rates over extended TOS, indicating superior stability.
- **Observations and Trends:** The improved stability of coprecipitated Fe–Ce catalysts is linked to the structural stabilization provided by Ce, which inhibits sintering and preserves active sites.
