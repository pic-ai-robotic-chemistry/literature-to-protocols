## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fe/Si and Fe/Si/K Fischer–Tropsch catalysts are synthesized via a controlled precipitation/hydrolysis process that guarantees reproducibility and scalability. The method is organized into defined procedural steps:

#### • Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - 40 g of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O is dissolved in 40 mL of distilled water under continuous stirring.  
   - Tetraethylorthosilicate (Si(OC₂H₅)₄) is then added to the solution, initiating hydrolysis.  
   - Vigorous stirring is maintained for 4–6 hours to ensure complete hydrolysis, as indicated by the emergence of a noticeable oil-like layer.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - Simultaneously, NH₄OH is added dropwise to maintain the slurry’s pH at 9, as confirmed with a pH meter.  
   - This controlled addition facilitates the co‑precipitation process, depositing the precursor uniformly.

3. **Drying and Washing**  
   - The resulting slurry is subjected to vacuum filtration using a standard vacuum filter.  
   - The filter cake is washed three times with 20 mL of distilled water to remove any impurities and unreacted materials.  
   - The washed cake is then oven dried at 110 °C for 24 hours.

4. **Milling**  
   - After drying, the cake is milled into a fine powder to maximize the surface area for subsequent reactions.

5. **Post‑treatment for Fe/Si/K Catalyst**  
   - For the Fe/Si/K catalyst, 50 mg of K₂CO₃ is first dissolved in 5 mL of water and is added to the milled powder, ensuring homogeneous incorporation.  
   - A second drying step is conducted at 110 °C for another 24 hours.

6. **Calcination**  
   - The final catalyst (Fe/Si/K) is calcined at 350 °C for 4 hours to remove organic residues, while the Fe/Si catalyst follows the same process excluding the K₂CO₃ addition.

This structured approach, with explicit reagent quantities, equipment use (e.g., pH meter, vacuum filter, drying oven), and step order, ensures that the catalyst synthesis is both accurate and replicable. Advantages such as controlled hydrolysis and pH adjustment are balanced by the additional complexity when incorporating the potassium promoter, impacting synthesis scalability and equipment requirements.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/Si Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 40 g of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 1 g of tetraethylorthosilicate (Si(OC₂H₅)₄), and distilled water (40 mL).  
- **Conditions**: Stirring vigorously at room temperature for 4–6 hours; pH maintained at 9 using NH₄OH added dropwise.  
- **Process**:  
  • Dissolve 40 g of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in 40 mL of distilled water to form a uniform solution.  
  • Add 1 g of tetraethylorthosilicate to the iron nitrate solution while stirring; continue vigorous stirring for 4–6 hours to promote complete hydrolysis.  
  • Simultaneously add NH₄OH gradually to adjust and maintain the pH at 9, ensuring efficient precipitation of iron hydroxide and silica species.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintaining the precise pH during precursor mixing is crucial for uniform precursor formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification  
- **Process**:  
  • Transfer the resulting slurry to a vacuum filtration setup to separate the precipitate.  
  • Wash the filter cake three times with distilled water to remove residual ions.  
  • Ensure that all primary precursors are fully formed before proceeding to the drying stage.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry the washed precipitate in an oven at 110 °C for 24 hours; subsequently, mill the dried cake into a fine powder.  
- **For the Fe/Si Catalyst**:  
  • Use the ground powder directly for further catalytic evaluation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst pre-reaction activation for the Fe/Si and Fe/Si/K Fischer–Tropsch catalysts relies on a defined thermal treatment sequence. This procedure ensures the removal of organic residues from the synthesized precursor and promotes the formation of an active catalyst phase suitable for subsequent reaction testing.

#### 5.1.1 Pre-Activation Drying
- **Definition:** The drying step is essential for eliminating residual moisture and volatile solvents from the catalyst precursor. This gentle thermal treatment ensures that no residual water interferes with the subsequent high-temperature calcination.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is dried in an oven at 110 °C for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** This controlled drying is critical for stabilizing the physical structure of the precursor, thereby ensuring that the calcination proceeds uniformly without structural collapse or unexpected reactions.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** Activation temperature is a key parameter in the calcination step, directly impacting the removal of organics and the development of the active catalyst phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The dried catalyst, including both the Fe/Si and the potassium-promoted Fe/Si/K formulations, is calcined at 350 °C. For the Fe/Si/K catalyst, this thermal treatment follows the incorporation of 50 mg K₂CO₃ dissolved in water into the dried precursor.
- **Observations:** Operating at 350 °C provides sufficient thermal energy to decompose and burn off organic residues. This controlled thermal activation is instrumental in achieving a uniform and reproducible active phase, as corroborated by subsequent stable CO conversion rates, with performance maintained even over prolonged reaction times (~300 hours on stream).

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the calcination step is critical to allow complete removal of organic contaminants while preventing excessive sintering of active sites.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Both catalyst types are calcined for 4 hours.
- **Observations:** The 4-hour calcination period has been verified to effectively eliminate residual organics, thus ensuring that the catalysts exhibit robust performance and reproducibility under Fischer–Tropsch reaction conditions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is the operating condition at which the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is conducted. It plays a key role in activating syngas (CO and H₂), promoting the formation of hydrocarbons, and ensuring optimal catalyst performance.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction temperature was maintained at 270 °C throughout the catalytic tests.  
- **Observations:** At 270 °C, the catalyst demonstrated robust performance with consistent CO conversion over extended reaction periods (approximately 300 hours), even when the syngas composition was alternated between H₂ and D₂ conditions.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the concentration of reactant gases in the reactor, directly affecting the rates of CO conversion and hydrocarbon yield.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were conducted at a reactor pressure of 188 psi (approximately 1.3 Mpa).  
- **Observations:** This pressure setting ensured high syngas concentration, which contributed to optimal conversion rates and maintained catalyst activity during prolonged operation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio defines the proportion of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed gas, which directly impacts the product distribution and selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The syngas mixture consisted of 60% H₂ and 30% CO (with the balance being N₂).  
- **Observations:** This specific ratio was critical in tailoring the hydrocarbon profile. Variations in this ratio were found to influence the selectivity of C₁⁺ products, where an adjustment in the ratio affected the propagation and termination steps of the reaction.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Gas Hourly Space Velocity (GHSV) is defined as the volumetric flow rate of the syngas (mL) per gram of catalyst per hour, determining the contact time between the gas and the catalyst.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** GHSV values reported in the experiments were 1.403, 1.748, and 2.257 mL/(gcat·h) in different experimental runs.  
- **Observations:** Lower GHSV values generally allowed for longer contact times, resulting in higher CO conversion, whereas higher values led to reduced contact time but demonstrated improved catalyst stability.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For the Fe/Si/K catalyst (Run 1), CO conversion was 59.5% under CO/H₂ and increased to 70.8% under CO/D₂, returning to 61.6% after switching back to CO/H₂, demonstrating stability over ~300 hours. In Run 2 (lower activity, Fe/Si/K), conversions were 11.8% (CO/H₂) and 27.5% (CO/D₂). Run 3 (Fe/Si/K, lower GHSV) showed higher conversions: 78.3% (CO/H₂) and 84.8% (CO/D₂). The Fe/Si catalyst (Run 4) exhibited lower conversions: 8.2% (CO/H₂) and 18.8% (CO/D₂).
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion consistently increased when switching from H₂ to D₂, indicating an inverse isotope effect ([COconv]H/[COconv]D = 0.44–0.92). Lower GHSV enhanced conversion, while potassium promotion (Fe/Si/K) significantly improved activity compared to Fe/Si.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity was higher with CO/H₂ than CO/D₂ for all catalysts. For Fe/Si/K (Run 1), values were 75.2% (CO/H₂) and 67.1% (CO/D₂). For Fe/Si (Run 4), selectivities were 80.1% (CO/H₂) and 74.2% (CO/D₂).
- **Observations and Trends:** The normal isotope effect was observed for CO₂ formation, with more CO₂ produced under H₂. Potassium promotion increased both CO conversion and CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe/Si/K (Run 1), CH₄ selectivity was 3.67% (CO/H₂) and 3.87% (CO/D₂). In Run 3 (lower GHSV), values increased to 6.74% (CO/H₂) and 7.56% (CO/D₂). Fe/Si (Run 4) showed 2.12% (CO/H₂) and 3.07% (CO/D₂).
- **Observations and Trends:** CH₄ selectivity increased slightly with D₂. Potassium addition led to higher CH₄ selectivity, and lower GHSV further increased methane formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbons:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe/Si/K (Run 1) produced 20.3% C₁⁺ (CO/H₂) and 37.3% (CO/D₂); Fe/Si (Run 4) yielded 4.1% (CO/H₂) and 14.2% (CO/D₂).
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium promotion and D₂ syngas both increased C₃⁺ selectivity, indicating enhanced chain growth.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory distribution, represents the probability of chain growth:
  \[
  r_n = r_1 \alpha^{n-1}
  \]
  where \( n \) is the carbon number.
- **Experimental Data:** α values for Fe/Si/K were 0.77 (Run 1), 0.67 (Run 2), and 0.81 (Run 3); Fe/Si (Run 4) had α = 0.62.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values with potassium promotion and lower GHSV correlate with increased selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability was assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended operation.
- **Experimental Data:** In Run 1, CO conversion remained nearly constant (59.5% to 61.6%) after ~300 hours, including H₂/D₂ switching.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both Fe/Si and Fe/Si/K catalysts demonstrated robust stability, with negligible deactivation under the tested conditions.
