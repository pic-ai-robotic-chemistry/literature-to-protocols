## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the reproducible synthesis approaches for Fe–Mn bimetallic catalysts via two distinct methods, providing a comparative analysis of advantages and challenges related to synthesis complexity, scalability, cost, and equipment requirements.

#### • Fe4Mn1-SCM: Solution Combustion Synthesis  
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation:**  
   - Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and 50 wt% Mn(NO₃)₂ in deionized water using a Fe:Mn molar ratio of 4:1.  
   - Add citric acid as a reductant at a molar ratio of 2:1 relative to the total metal content (Fe+Mn).  
2. **Precipitation–Deposition:**  
   - Form a homogeneous gel; although no explicit precipitation–deposition step is required, gel formation ensures uniform dispersion of precursors.  
3. **Drying:**  
   - Maintain the gel at 50 °C for 2 hours, allowing controlled solvent evaporation and precursor consolidation.  
4. **Calcination and Combustion:**  
   - Transfer the gel to a muffle furnace. A self‑sustained combustion occurs at 350 °C, followed immediately by a calcination step at the same temperature for 4 hours.  
5. **Post‑processing:**  
   - The rapid combustion and calcination yield a porous, loose catalyst morphology that enhances surface area and catalytic activity.

#### • Fe4Mn1-CP: Co‑precipitation Method  
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation:**  
   - Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and 50 wt% Mn(NO₃)₂ in ethylene glycol.  
2. **Precipitation–Deposition:**  
   - Prepare a Na₂CO₃ solution (0.025 mol in deionized water) and add it dropwise to the metal precursor solution at a controlled temperature of 10 °C (using a temperature-controlled water bath) to ensure uniform nucleation.  
3. **Drying:**  
   - After an aging stage of 1 hour under stirring, wash the precipitate extensively (typically five times with deionized water) and dry at 60 °C overnight, promoting tight nanoparticle aggregation.  
4. **Calcination:**  
   - Calcine the washed and dried material at 450 °C for 4 hours, which yields a compact aggregation of nanoparticles with distinct morphology.  

Both methods maintain precise reagent concentrations and molar ratios to ensure reproducibility. The SCM method leads to a porous structure with less carbon deposition, while the CP method, due to controlled precipitation and higher calcination temperature, may result in a denser catalyst, impacting overall catalytic performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pre‐reaction treatment designed to convert the precursor iron oxides into active phases suitable for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The following subsections detail the precise parameters extracted directly from the article.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is key to initiating both the reduction of iron oxides and the early stages of carburization, thereby establishing the active metallic and carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 350 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature effectively drives the reduction process with CO and H₂, while also facilitating controlled carburization. However, care is taken as excessive temperature may lead to over-carburization and potential deactivation, particularly evident in formulations like Fe4Mn1-SCM.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the kinetics of gas-solid interactions during the reduction process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The process is carried out at atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Operating under ambient conditions supports a stable activation process and mitigates risks associated with high-pressure sintering or agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas directly impacts the reduction of iron oxides and subsequent carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture composed of 5% CO, 5% H₂, with the balance (90%) being N₂ is employed.
- **Observations:** CO, as a strong reducing agent, ensures effective oxygen removal, while the moderate presence of H₂ limits over-reduction. The resultant gas-phase environment supports a balanced formation of active carbide phases.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, expressed as the flow rate per unit catalyst weight, ensures uniform exposure of the catalyst to the activating gas.
- **Experimental Conditions:** With a gas flow of 25 mL/min over 25 mg of catalyst, the calculated space-time velocity is approximately 60,000 mL/(g_cat·h).
- **Observations:** This high velocity promotes effective activation by minimizing mass transfer limitations.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Activation time is critical to ensuring complete reduction and initiation of carburization without initiating deleterious effects.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under the above conditions for 5 hours.
- **Observations:** While sufficient for the formation of active phases, extended durations may lead to excessive carbon deposition and reduced catalyst reactivity.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature sets the stage for effective syngas activation and the formation of active iron carbide phases. In the experiments, the reaction was conducted at 280 °C. This temperature was carefully selected to initiate the dissociation of CO and H₂, enabling the transformation of Fe oxide phases into active carbide species. Furthermore, maintaining 280 °C contributes to an optimal balance between reaction kinetics and product formation, fostering conditions that favor the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons such as C₅⁺.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Pressure plays a critical role in dictating the density of the syngas and the mass transfer dynamics at the catalyst surface. The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis in this study was carried out under an applied pressure of 2.0 MPa. This relatively high pressure enhances the diffusion of reactants, thereby improving overall CO conversion rates. Additionally, the increased syngas density under 2.0 MPa is instrumental in promoting the formation of heavier hydrocarbon products, as observed by improved performance metrics over extended reaction periods.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The syngas composition utilized in the experiments comprised 45% CO and 45% H₂, resulting in an effective H₂/CO ratio of 1:1. This balanced ratio is pivotal, as it directly influences the hydrocarbon distribution. While a higher hydrogen ratio can lead to increased methane formation, the 1:1 ratio helps maintain a favorable environment for C–C coupling reactions, promoting the formation of more valuable longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) for the catalytic evaluation was maintained at 7500 h⁻¹. GHSV is a measure of the volumetric flow rate of the reactants relative to the catalyst mass and is critical in defining contact time. A GHSV of 7500 h⁻¹ results in shorter interaction intervals between the syngas and the catalyst, which can contribute to lower overall CO conversion rates. However, this parameter also aids in maintaining stable reaction conditions and minimizing catalyst deactivation, thereby supporting reproducibility and reliability in the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis process.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** Phase composition and structural evolution were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), providing insight into the crystalline states of Fe and Mn species before and after activation and reaction.
- **Findings:** For Fe4Mn1-SCM, XRD patterns revealed diffraction peaks at 2θ = 30.2°, 35.6°, 43.2°, 57.2°, and 62.8°, corresponding to c‑Fe₂O₃ and Fe₃O₄ phases. In contrast, Fe4Mn1-CP exhibited peaks at 2θ = 24.1°, 33.4°, 35.6°, and 49.6°, characteristic of pure α‑Fe₂O₃. After reaction, Fe4Mn1-CP transformed almost exclusively to v‑Fe₅C₂, while Fe4Mn1-SCM displayed a mixture of v‑Fe₅C₂, e‑Fe₂.₂C, and Fe–Mn mixed oxides (Fex‑Mn₃‑xO₄, FexMn₁‑xO).
- **Observations:** The SCM method promotes the stabilization of mixed oxide phases and retards complete carburization, whereas the CP method facilitates rapid transformation to iron carbide.

#### 6.1.2 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was used to probe the reducibility and metal–promoter interactions.
- **Findings:** Fe4Mn1-SCM showed overlapping reduction peaks (main at 321 °C, shoulder at 477 °C), indicating simultaneous reduction of Fe and Mn oxides due to intimate contact. Fe4Mn1-CP displayed distinct reduction steps at 280 °C, 355 °C, and a broad peak from 400–700 °C, reflecting sequential reduction and easier carburization.
- **Observations:** Overlapping peaks in SCM confirm enhanced Fe–Mn interaction, while distinct peaks in CP suggest phase segregation and facilitate carbide formation.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET analysis) quantified surface area, pore volume, and pore size, which are critical for catalytic accessibility.
- **Findings:** Fe4Mn1-SCM exhibited a surface area of 65 m²/g, pore volume of 0.197 cm³/g, and average pore size of 8.6 nm. Fe4Mn1-CP had a lower surface area (48 m²/g), pore volume (0.109 cm³/g), and similar pore size (8.5 nm).
- **Observations:** The higher porosity and surface area in SCM are attributed to gas evolution during combustion, enhancing reactant diffusion and Fe–Mn proximity.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** SEM, TEM, and EDX mapping characterized particle morphology and elemental dispersion.
- **Findings:** Fe4Mn1-SCM displayed a porous, foamy structure with uniform Fe–Mn distribution. Fe4Mn1-CP showed compact nanoparticle aggregation and segregated MnOx domains. EDX confirmed homogeneous Fe–Mn mixing in SCM and isolated MnOx in CP.
- **Observations:** The SCM method yields a more open structure and intimate metal–promoter contact, while CP results in phase segregation.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Composition and Iron Valence States
- **Description:** XPS and Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES) provided information on surface oxidation states and quantification of iron carbide phases.
- **Findings:** XPS revealed Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺ as dominant surface species in both catalysts post-reaction, with negligible surface FeCx due to passivation. MES quantified 82.7% v‑Fe₅C₂ in Fe4Mn1-CP and 52% v‑Fe₅C₂ plus 5.1% e‑Fe₂.₂C in Fe4Mn1-SCM.
- **Observations:** CP promotes extensive bulk carburization, while SCM stabilizes mixed phases and limits excessive carbide formation.

#### 6.1.6 Carbon Deposition and Carburization
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy and EDX mapping assessed carbonaceous species and carburization.
- **Findings:** Operando Raman detected D- and G-bands (1350, 1580 cm⁻¹) for carbon, appearing earlier and more intensely in CP (at 200 °C) than SCM (at 350 °C). EDX and XPS showed higher C/Fe ratios and more isolated carbon in CP, indicating greater carbon deposition.
- **Observations:** SCM’s structure suppresses carbon accumulation, while CP’s rapid carburization leads to severe carbon deposition.

#### 6.1.7 Bulk Metal Content
- **Description:** ICP-OES measured Fe and Mn content to confirm compositional accuracy.
- **Findings:** Fe4Mn1-SCM contained 55.3 wt% Fe and 9.9 wt% Mn; Fe4Mn1-CP had 53.7 wt% Fe and 9.6 wt% Mn.
- **Observations:** Both catalysts maintained the intended Fe:Mn ratio, ensuring comparability of structural and catalytic properties.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions (280 °C, 2.0 MPa, GHSV 7500 h⁻¹), Fe4Mn1-SCM achieved a CO conversion of 22.5%, while Fe4Mn1-CP reached 32.4%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher CO conversion of Fe4Mn1-CP is attributed to its greater content of v‑Fe₅C₂ and less intimate Fe–Mn interaction, which accelerates CO dissociation. In contrast, the SCM catalyst’s moderate conversion is linked to stabilized Fe–Mn mixed oxides that retard over-carburization.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe4Mn1-SCM exhibited a CO₂ selectivity of 24.8%, whereas Fe4Mn1-CP showed a significantly higher value of 42.8%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The lower CO₂ selectivity in SCM is associated with abundant Fe–MnOx interfaces, which suppress oxygen removal and favor C–C coupling, while the CP catalyst’s segregated MnOx phases promote CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the percentage of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe4Mn1-SCM demonstrated a CH₄ selectivity of 12.9%, while Fe4Mn1-CP reached 19.8%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SCM catalyst’s lower methane selectivity indicates enhanced chain growth and suppressed methanation, in contrast to the CP catalyst, which favors short-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\sum_{m \geq 5} m \cdot F_{\text{C}_m\text{H}_n,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe4Mn1-SCM achieved a C₅⁺ selectivity of 45.5%, while Fe4Mn1-CP yielded only 15.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SCM catalyst’s high C₅⁺ selectivity is attributed to its stabilized Fe–Mn structure, which promotes chain growth, whereas the CP catalyst predominantly produces C₁–C₄ hydrocarbons (85%).

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, quantifies the probability of chain growth:
  \[
  \alpha = \frac{\text{Rate of chain propagation}}{\text{Rate of chain propagation} + \text{Rate of chain termination}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe4Mn1-SCM displayed a double ASF distribution with α¹ = 0.53 (C₁–C₉) and α² = 0.79 (C₉⁺), while Fe4Mn1-CP showed a single α of 0.43.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher α values for SCM reflect its superior ability to promote long-chain hydrocarbon formation, consistent with its elevated C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time-on-stream (TOS); deactivation rate is the decline in conversion per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe4Mn1-SCM maintained stable or slightly increasing CO conversion over 100 h TOS, indicating negligible deactivation. In contrast, Fe4Mn1-CP experienced rapid activity loss after 40 h, correlating with severe carbon deposition.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SCM catalyst’s resistance to deactivation is linked to its porous morphology and intimate Fe–Mn mixing, which suppress carbon buildup, while the CP catalyst’s rapid deactivation is due to excessive carburization and carbon accumulation.

#### 6.2.7 Olefin-to-Paraffin Ratio (O/P)
- **Description:** The O/P ratio measures the selectivity for olefins relative to paraffins in the C₂–C₄ fraction.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe4Mn1-SCM exhibited an O/P ratio of 3.42 for C₂–C₄ hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high O/P ratio for SCM indicates a strong preference for olefin production, further supporting its enhanced chain growth and stability.

---
**Summary:**  
The Fe4Mn1-SCM catalyst, prepared via solution combustion, demonstrates moderate CO conversion, low CO₂ and CH₄ selectivity, high C₅⁺ selectivity, elevated α factors, and exceptional stability, all attributed to its unique Fe–Mn proximity and porous structure. In contrast, Fe4Mn1-CP, synthesized by co-precipitation, shows higher CO conversion but is prone to rapid deactivation and favors short-chain hydrocarbon production due to excessive carburization and carbon deposition.
