## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis is executed via an established co‑precipitation method, followed by a precipitation–deposition step, drying, and calcination. This section outlines the detailed experimental procedure ensuring reproducibility and clarity for subsequent replication.

#### Co‑precipitation Method:
1. • Catalyst precursor solutions are prepared by dissolving Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O in deionized water, maintaining a total molar amount of 0.18 mol. The Fe to Zn molar ratios are controlled (e.g., 4:1, 2:1, or 1:1) as per the desired catalyst composition.
2. • The mixed solution is stirred at 700 rpm for 30 minutes using a calibrated stirring apparatus.
   
#### Precipitation–Deposition:
3. • Ammonia is then added slowly to the stirred solution to adjust the pH precisely to 9. This controlled addition facilitates simultaneous precipitation and deposition of the metal hydroxide species.
4. • After pH adjustment, the suspension is maintained under stirring for an additional 1 hour to promote uniform precipitation.
5. • The mixture is subsequently allowed to age under static conditions for 2 hours. Visual observations such as color change and precipitate formation are noted to confirm the process progress.

#### Post‑processing:
6. • The precipitate is filtered and carefully washed with deionized water to remove residual ions.
7. • Drying is carried out in an oven at 100°C for 12 hours.
8. • Calcination is performed in a muffle furnace at 400°C for 4 hours in air to form the required ZnFe₂O₄ spinel precursor and active iron phases.
9. • For promoter integration, Na₂CO₃ and Na₂S are introduced via an impregnation step on the dried powders. This step is designed to adjust electronic properties, facilitate hydrophobic modification when needed (e.g., using SiO₂@C16), and ultimately enhance catalytic performance.

This systematic sequence—from precursor mixing through calcination—ensures consistency in catalyst production and aligns with the reported experimental conditions for synthesizing 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1 and NaS–Fe1Zn1 catalysts as detailed in the article.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1 and NaS–Fe1Zn1

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (totaling 0.18 mol with adjustable Fe:Zn ratios of 4:1, 2:1, or 1:1) are used as the primary metal precursors.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Dissolution in deionized water followed by stirring at 700 rpm.  
  • pH adjustment to 9 using a slow addition of ammonia.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve the metal nitrates in deionized water and stir at 700 rpm for 30 minutes to form a homogeneous solution.  
  2. Add ammonia dropwise until the pH reaches 9; continue stirring for an additional hour to ensure complete precipitation of the respective hydroxides.  
  3. Allow the suspension to age under static conditions for 2 hours, ensuring the stabilization of the precipitated precursors.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Accurate pH control and constant stirring speed are critical to achieve uniform nucleation and precipitation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:  
  • The aged and precipitated FeZn mixture is combined with Na₂CO₃ for sodium promotion and, for NaS catalysts, Na₂S is introduced at varying loadings (0.1, 0.2, or 0.3 wt%).  
- **Process**:  
  1. Filter the aged suspension and wash thoroughly with deionized water to remove any soluble by-products.  
  2. Impregnate the dried precipitate with a solution of Na₂CO₃ and Na₂S, ensuring controlled incorporation of the promoters.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Maintain consistency in the impregnation process to support reproducible catalyst performance.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  • The washed material is dried at 100 °C for 12 hours.  
- **Calcination**:  
  • The dried precursor is then calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 hours, which facilitates the formation of the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel structure.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Uniform drying and a controlled calcination profile are essential for the reproducible formation of the desired spinel phase.

##### Step 4: Post-Processing and Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • After the promoter impregnation, a final drying step at 100 °C for 12 hours is performed to ensure complete solvent removal.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Consistent temperature control during both drying and calcination is crucial for achieving the Fe⁰@ZnO structure upon subsequent reduction, thereby enhancing catalyst dispersion and stability.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD analysis determines the crystalline phases and structural transformations of the catalyst. The protocol involves diluting approximately 2 g of the catalyst with an equal volume of quartz powder, reducing the sample in flowing H₂ at 450 °C for 5 hours, and then passivating in a 1% O₂/99% N₂ mixture for 2 hours to prevent oxidation.
- **Instruments and Models**: Brucker D2 PHASER X‑ray diffraction analyzer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 30 kV and 10 mA, with a scanning range from 2θ = 5° to 90° at 0.02°/step.
- **Test Parameters**: Sample reduction, passivation, and precise alignment on the sample holder.
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample preparation ensures accurate phase identification and minimizes oxidation artifacts.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR characterizes the reduction behavior of the catalyst by detecting distinct peaks: transformation from ZnFe₂O₄ to Fe₃O₄ (~335 °C), Fe₃O₄ to FeO (~425 °C), and finally FeO to metallic Fe (~610 °C). Prior to analysis, samples are pretreated in Ar at 100 °C for 1 hour.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micro fixed-bed reactor coupled with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Reduction in 10% H₂/Ar at 40 mL/min under 0.2 MPa.
- **Test Parameters**: Temperature ramping sequences that pinpoint reduction intervals.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and controlled heating are essential for reproducibility.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for reducing iron oxides into active iron phases required for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It directly affects the exposure of active sites and influences metal dispersion.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced at 450 °C in a fixed-bed reactor.  
- **Observations:** At 450 °C, the reduction of iron oxides is efficiently achieved. However, there is a risk for metal particle aggregation (sintering), which is mitigated by the presence of Zn promoters that enhance dispersion and stabilize the resulting phases.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
- **Definition:** The activation pressure influences the kinetics of the hydrogen reduction process and contributes to achieving a uniform active phase without excessive sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The pre-reaction reduction is carried out under a pressure of 0.2 MPa.  
- **Observations:** This moderate pressure supports effective reduction while preserving catalyst structure and dispersion, which is crucial for long-term stability.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation is paramount to ensuring complete reduction of metal oxides, ultimately forming the active metallic (and iron carbide) phases.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed using a hydrogen-rich atmosphere, with the catalyst exposed to a flowing H₂ stream at a rate of 40 mL/min.  
- **Observations:** A H₂-rich environment effectively drives the reduction reaction while controlling the risk of over-reduction or undesired phase transitions. In contrast, alternative gas mixtures were not implemented for synthesis activation.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process guarantees that the reduction proceeds to completion, establishing the active catalyst state necessary for the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under the above conditions for 5 hours.  
- **Observations:** An activation period of 5 hours ensures thorough reduction with minimized sintering effects, as supported by long-term stability observations during catalytic testing (up to 1000 hours).  

*Note:* While reaction parameters such as a gas hourly space velocity of 1200 mL/(g_cat·h) are detailed for the Fischer–Tropsch tests, the activation protocol does not specify a dedicated space-time velocity, focusing solely on the controlled conditions of temperature, pressure, gas flow, and time for catalyst preparation.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reactor temperature is critical for syngas activation, enabling the dissociation and hydrogenation of CO, which improves the carburization of the iron phase and facilitates CO conversion.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic evaluation for CO₂-FTS was conducted at a fixed temperature of 320 °C.
- **Observations:** At 320 °C, the catalyst demonstrated the highest lower olefins selectivity along with stable CO₂ conversion. Notably, increasing the temperature to 340 °C led to an increase in CH₄ and heavier hydrocarbon (C₅⁺) generation, indicating reduced catalyst stability under higher thermal conditions.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the concentration of syngas and the competitive adsorption of CO, thereby affecting product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The CO₂-FTS reaction was performed at a steady pressure of 1 MPa.
- **Observations:** Elevated pressure was found to enhance CO₂ conversion while reducing CO selectivity. This pressure effect is attributed to the volume contraction in the reaction system, which promotes CO transformation and shifts selectivity towards heavier hydrocarbon products.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO₂ Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO₂ ratio determines the extent of hydrogenation during the reaction, directly impacting product distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A H₂/CO₂ ratio of 3 was consistently applied during the catalytic tests.
- **Observations:** An increase in the feed ratio led to better CO₂ conversion and an improvement in lower olefins selectivity. The enhanced hydrogenation process at this ratio minimizes CO formation and favors the generation of desired olefins.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) reflects the ratio of the feed gas flow rate to the catalyst mass, influencing reactant contact time and conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The system maintained a GHSV of 1200 mL/(g_cat·h).
- **Observations:** Lower GHSV values (600 and 1200 mL/(g_cat·h)) were associated with higher CO₂ conversions and improved catalyst stability, whereas higher GHSV values caused reduced contact time, leading to decreased conversion and elevated CO selectivity.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), complemented by Mössbauer spectroscopy for quantitative phase analysis. These techniques elucidate the active phases responsible for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS) performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of fresh catalysts revealed the presence of ZnFe₂O₄ (spinel) and a weak ZnO peak. Upon reduction, ZnFe₂O₄ was fully converted to metallic Fe⁰ and ZnO, with Fe⁰ exhibiting higher crystallinity. After CO₂-FTS, the main phases identified were Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂, with ZnO persisting. In S-containing catalysts, Fe⁰, Fe₃C, and Fe₅C₂ were observed, but Fe₃O₄ was absent. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the spent 1.5Na–Fe₁Zn₁ catalyst as containing 32% Fe₃O₄ and 68% χ-Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The co-existence of χ-Fe₅C₂ and Fe₃O₄ is favorable for CO₂-FTS, enhancing activity and olefin selectivity. S addition promotes Fe₃C formation, which is less active for FTS, and suppresses Fe₃O₄, impacting the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) activity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to assess particle morphology and size distribution, providing insight into catalyst dispersion and stability.
- **Findings:** SEM images of fresh 1.5Na–Fe₁Zn₁ showed block-like particles with dense surface protrusions, attributed to ZnFe₂O₄ spinel and ZnO nanoparticles, respectively. TEM of reduced catalysts revealed large Fe⁰ nanoparticles (260–290 nm) with smaller ZnO nanoparticles (60–70 nm) attached to their surfaces, confirming a Fe⁰@ZnO structure. After long-term reaction, Fe nanoparticles in spent 1.5Na–Fe₁Zn₁ were encapsulated by thick carbon shells (up to 28 nm), while S-containing catalysts (e.g., 1.5Na0.1S–Fe₁Zn₁) showed negligible carbon deposition.
- **Observations:** Zn addition enhances Fe dispersion and inhibits aggregation, while Na₂S addition effectively suppresses carbon shell formation, improving catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was conducted to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which are critical for reactant accessibility and catalytic efficiency.
- **Findings:** Zn incorporation increased the catalyst surface area from 37 m²/g to 47 m²/g, indicating improved dispersion. S addition did not significantly alter these textural properties. Detailed values for pore volume and size were referenced in supplementary data.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface area due to Zn promotes greater active site exposure, facilitating improved FTS performance.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility and Surface Chemistry
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD, CO-TPD) were used to probe reducibility and surface adsorption properties, which influence catalyst activation and reactivity.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR revealed three reduction peaks: ZnFe₂O₄ to Fe₃O₄ (~335 °C), Fe₃O₄ to FeO (~425 °C), and FeO to Fe⁰ (~610 °C). Na₂S addition shifted these peaks to lower temperatures, indicating enhanced reducibility. CO₂-TPD showed that Na addition introduced strong high-temperature desorption peaks, while Na₂S suppressed both CO₂ and CO adsorption, as evidenced by decreased peak areas and lower desorption temperatures.
- **Observations:** Na₂S modifies the electronic environment, facilitating reduction and suppressing excessive CO₂/CO adsorption, which correlates with reduced carbon deposition and improved selectivity.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Deposition Analysis
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy and O₂ temperature-programmed oxidation (O₂-TPO) were utilized to assess carbon deposition, a key factor in catalyst deactivation.
- **Findings:** Raman spectra of spent 1.5Na–Fe₁Zn₁ displayed strong D (1313 cm⁻¹) and G (1600 cm⁻¹) bands, with a dominant D band indicating highly defective carbon. S-containing catalysts exhibited much weaker D/G bands, signifying minimal carbon accumulation. O₂-TPO confirmed reduced carbon oxidation peaks with Na₂S addition.
- **Observations:** Na₂S effectively suppresses carbon buildup, contributing to prolonged catalyst life and stable performance.

#### 6.1.6 Elemental and Phase Quantification
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) and Mössbauer spectroscopy provided quantitative analysis of elemental composition and iron phase distribution.
- **Findings:** ICP-OES confirmed the intended Fe, Zn, Na, and S loadings, with minimal loss after reaction. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified iron phases, supporting XRD findings regarding the prevalence of χ-Fe₅C₂ and Fe₃O₄ in spent catalysts.
- **Observations:** Accurate control and retention of promoter elements underpin the observed catalytic trends and stability.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO and CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** Conversion is defined as the percentage of CO (or CO₂) molecules transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO (or CO}_2\text{) Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{in}} - F_{\text{out}}}{F_{\text{in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{in}} \) and \( F_{\text{out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO (or CO₂) at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - For CO₂–FTS over 1.5Na–Fe₁Zn₁ at 320 °C, 1 MPa, H₂/CO₂ = 3, and GHSV = 1200 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹, CO₂ conversion increases with temperature, ranging from 25.2% to 42.8%.  
  - In CO–FTS, Na₂S-modified catalysts (e.g., 1.5Na0.1S–Fe₁Zn₁) achieve high CO conversion, up to approximately 96%.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - The optimal Fe/Zn ratio (1:1) maximizes conversion and stability.  
  - Na₂S addition in CO–FTS maintains high conversion and suppresses deactivation, while excessive Na₂S in CO₂–FTS reduces activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO Selectivity
- **Description:** CO selectivity refers to the fraction of converted CO₂ that is reduced to CO (via the reverse water–gas shift reaction), calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Increasing Na₂S loading (from 0.1 to 0.2 wt%) in CO₂–FTS sharply increases CO selectivity, indicating a shift toward the RWGS pathway.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - Higher Na₂S content suppresses olefin formation and favors CO production.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the proportion of converted CO (or CO₂) forming methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Methane selectivity is minimized in Na₂S-modified catalysts during CO–FTS, remaining low and stable.  
  - In CO₂–FTS, excessive Na₂S increases CH₄ and alkane selectivity at the expense of olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - Na₂S addition effectively suppresses methane formation in CO–FTS, but can increase it in CO₂–FTS if overdosed.

#### 6.2.4 C₄⁺ Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** C₄⁺ selectivity quantifies the fraction of converted CO forming long-chain (C₄ and above) α-olefins:
\[
\text{C}_4^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\sum F_{\text{C}_4^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - In CO–FTS, Na₂S-modified catalysts achieve C₄⁺ olefin selectivity up to 65.4%.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - Na₂S addition promotes long-chain α-olefin formation and enhances catalyst stability.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, reflects the probability of chain propagation:
\[
W_n = (1 - \alpha)^2 \alpha^{n-1}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Product distributions fit the ASF model, with α values increasing upon Na and Zn addition, indicating enhanced chain growth.  
  - S addition further tunes α, favoring lower olefins in CO₂–FTS and long-chain olefins in CO–FTS.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - The Fe⁰@ZnO structure stabilizes α, supporting sustained selectivity for target olefins.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate is the decline in conversion per hour:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate (h}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time (h)}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - S-free catalysts show rapid deactivation in CO–FTS, with conversion and olefin selectivity dropping sharply.  
  - Na₂S-modified catalysts maintain high conversion and selectivity over extended periods (up to 1000 h in CO₂–FTS), with minimal deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - Na₂S addition suppresses carbon deposition (confirmed by TG, O₂-TPO, and Raman), directly correlating with improved long-term stability.

#### 6.2.7 Space–Time Yield (STY) of Lower Olefins
- **Description:** STY measures the mass of lower olefins produced per gram of catalyst per hour:
\[
\text{STY (mg·g}_{\text{cat}}^{-1}\text{·h}^{-1}) = \frac{\text{Mass of lower olefins}}{\text{Catalyst mass} \times \text{Time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - The optimum STY for lower olefins is 20.4 mg·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹ at 320 °C for 1.5Na–Fe₁Zn₁.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - This high STY is achieved under conditions balancing conversion, selectivity, and stability.

**Summary:**  
The Na–Fe–Zn catalyst system, especially with controlled Na₂S addition, demonstrates high CO and CO₂ conversion, tunable selectivity for light and long-chain α-olefins, suppressed methane formation, and exceptional long-term stability. The interplay between catalyst composition, phase structure, and promoter content is critical for optimizing performance metrics across both CO₂–FTS and CO–FTS processes.
