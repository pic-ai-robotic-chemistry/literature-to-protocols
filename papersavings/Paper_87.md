## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis is executed in two integrated stages: support synthesis via co‑precipitation and subsequent catalyst preparation by incipient wetness impregnation. Each step is controlled to ensure reproducibility and uniformity.

#### Co‑precipitation and Support Formation
1. • Precursor Mixing: Zinc nitrate and aluminum nitrate are dissolved in deionized water to form the metal source solution, which is simultaneously dropped with a sodium bicarbonate solution acting as the precipitant. The dropwise addition under vigorous stirring at 40°C ensures optimal reaction conditions, leading to the formation and observation of a zinc aluminate precipitate with a visible color change.
2. • Washing and pH Verification: The precipitated filter cake is thoroughly washed with water until the wash water reaches a neutral pH, confirming the complete removal of residual sodium bicarbonate.
3. • Partial Drying and Extrusion: The washed filter cake is partially dried at 80°C, mixed with 5 wt % sesbania gum powder (which aids in extrusion), extruded to form shaped materials, and further dried at room temperature followed by drying at 110°C.
4. • High‑Temperature Calcination: The extrudates undergo calcination at 1200°C for 24 hours in a horizontal tubular furnace. This high‑temperature treatment facilitates the formation of ZnAl₂O₄·xAl₂O₃ phases (where x varies from 0 to ∞), enhancing crystallinity and stability.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation and Catalyst Formation
1. • Impregnation – Step 1: The support is first impregnated with a K₂CO₃ solution, ensuring even promoter distribution. Subsequent desiccation at 110°C for 2 hours removes any excess moisture.
2. • Impregnation – Step 2: The K₂CO₃-treated support is then impregnated with an ammonium iron citrate solution. A similar drying step follows to ensure uniform deposition of the iron precursor.
3. • Secondary Calcination: A final calcination is conducted at 350°C for 4 hours in air to stabilize the active components.
4. • Reduction: Fresh catalysts are reduced in situ at 550°C for 6 hours under a gas mixture of 50% H₂/50% Ar at 0.1 MPa, activating the iron phase and finalizing the catalyst structure.

This multi‐step synthesis, with precise temperature, time, and reagent control, ensures reproducible catalyst performance as confirmed by the experimental observations and QA findings.

The catalyst synthesis employs a standardized protocol for all four formulations—REF0 (x = 0), SP1/N1 (x = 1), SP5/N5 (x = 5), and REF∞ (x = ∞)—ensuring reproducibility by adhering to preset reagent weight ratios and transfer conditions.

##### 3.1.1.1 REF0

- **Aluminum nitrate (Al(NO₃)₃)**: Weighed similarly to meet the formulation design.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

Liquid reagents are transferred to ensure homogeneity and proper reaction conditions during both the support synthesis and impregnation phases.

##### 3.1.2.1 REF0  
- **Deionized water**: Poured in the required volume (based on the 1:5 ratio) using a graduated cylinder to dissolve the metal nitrates.  
- **Sodium bicarbonate solution**: Transferred dropwise into the reactor maintained at 40°C to promote coprecipitation.  
- **K₂CO₃ solution**: Delivered via incipient wetness using a micropipette to evenly saturate the support.

Liquid transfer for SP1/N1, SP5/N5, and REF∞ follows the same precise techniques.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 REF0

Mixing conditions for SP1/N1, SP5/N5, and REF∞ are maintained identically to guarantee reproducible catalyst properties.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 15%Fe/2%K₂O/83%(ZnAl₂O₄·xAl₂O₃)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Zinc nitrate and aluminum nitrate are dissolved together in deionized water (metal sources:water weight ratio of 1:5) to form the mixed nitrate solution.  
  • Sodium bicarbonate is prepared separately in deionized water at a weight ratio of 1:13.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • The solutions are maintained at 40°C under vigorous stirring.  
  • pH is controlled during the addition to ensure rapid precipitation, followed by washing to neutrality.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve the zinc and aluminum nitrates completely to yield a homogeneous precursor solution.  
  2. Simultaneously drop the sodium bicarbonate solution into the nitrate solution at 40°C, stirring continuously for 1 hour.  
  3. Filter the resulting precipitate and wash with deionized water until achieving a neutral pH.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Maintain constant temperature and stirring to ensure uniform nucleation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Precursor supports from Step 1, sesbania gum powder (5 wt % as extrusion aid), K₂CO₃ solution, and ammonium iron citrate solution.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Extrusion and careful mixing after partial drying maintains material uniformity.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Mix the half-dried precipitate with 5 wt % sesbania gum powder and extrude the mixture.  
  2. Air-dry at room temperature followed by drying at 110°C for 2 hours.  
  3. Impregnate the dried supports first with K₂CO₃ solution (ensuring full pore filling), then dry at 110°C for 2 hours, and subsequently impregnate with ammonium iron citrate solution.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  1. After impregnation, a further drying step at 110°C ensures complete moisture removal.  
  2. Calcinate the material at 1200°C for 24 hours in a tubular furnace to develop the desired ZnAl₂O₄·xAl₂O₃ or Al₂O₃ phases.

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • Perform an in situ reduction by heating the catalyst to 550°C for 6 hours under a gas flow of 50% H₂/50% Ar (0.1 MPa) ensuring full reduction of iron species.

**Key Considerations**:  
• Monitor pH during precipitation and maintain precise temperature control.  
• Ensure uniform impregnation by following sequential drying and impregnation steps to avoid agglomeration and guarantee reproducibility.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is designed to convert the oxidic precursors into the active metallic (or carbide) phases essential for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The activation conditions were directly extracted from the experimental protocol to ensure reproducibility and optimal performance.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for driving the reduction reaction, ensuring that iron oxide species are transformed into their active forms.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is heated to 550 °C during the in situ reduction step.
- **Observations:** Maintaining 550 °C has been shown to effectively expose active sites while balancing the risk of sintering, thereby facilitating the formation of a uniformly active catalyst.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The activation pressure influences the kinetics of the reduction process by affecting gas diffusion and catalyst-gas contact.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction is performed at an activation pressure of 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at 0.1 MPa ensures controlled gas flow conditions that enhance reduction efficiency without promoting rapid or uneven reduction, which could compromise catalyst stability.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The choice of activation gas mixture determines the reduction environment, with hydrogen serving as the reducing agent and argon acting as an inert diluent.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A gas mixture consisting of 50% H₂ and 50% Ar is used during activation.
- **Observations:** The balanced gas mixture facilitates effective reduction while moderating the reduction rate to prevent over-reduction or excessive sintering. This controlled environment is crucial for developing the desired active phase for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step ensures that the reduction process is complete, achieving uniform exposure of all catalyst components.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated for 6 hours.
- **Observations:** The 6-hour period is essential to allow sufficient time for the hydrogen to penetrate the catalyst structure thoroughly, leading to homogeneously reduced catalytic sites and optimal performance in subsequent reaction testing.

Collectively, these parameters—550 °C, 0.1 MPa, a 50% H₂/50% Ar gas mixture, and a 6-hour duration—are tailored to achieve the precise reduction needed for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, ensuring both efficient activation and reproducible results across different catalyst compositions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter that influences the activation of syngas and the formation of hydrocarbon products during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. In the described study, the reactor was maintained at 340 °C. Under these conditions, the catalysts demonstrated optimal performance, achieving high light olefin selectivity and a CO conversion of approximately 38.3%. At this temperature, the desorption rate of primary products is balanced to promote sufficient C–C coupling, enhancing light olefin production while limiting unwanted methane formation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
The overall reaction system was operated at a total pressure of 2.0 MPa (approximately 20 bar). This elevated pressure increases the syngas concentration within the reactor, thereby improving the probability of productive collisions between reactant molecules. The study notes that the increased pressure favors higher CO conversion and augments the selectivity towards C₂–C₄ hydrocarbons, as it enhances C–C coupling sufficient for the formation of heavier hydrocarbon chains.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The Fischer-Tropsch reactions were carried out using a syngas mixture with a composition of 45% H₂, 45% CO, and 10% Ar. This translates to an exact H₂/CO molar ratio of 1:1, which is crucial for balancing the hydrogenation reactions; a balanced ratio is essential for promoting light olefin production over undesired methane generation. The inclusion of argon serves as an inert internal standard during gas chromatographic analysis, thereby ensuring accurate quantification of reaction products.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The space-time velocity in the fixed-bed reactor was maintained at 1500 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹. This parameter, which quantifies the syngas flow rate per unit mass of catalyst, directly controls the residence time of the reactants over the catalytic surface. Although a higher space velocity can reduce CO conversion due to shorter contact times, the catalyst stability is preserved, thereby maintaining an efficient reaction profile for optimized light olefin production.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structural Evolution
- **Description:** The crystalline phases of the catalysts and supports were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Panalytical X'Pert Pro diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation. XRD analysis enables identification of phase composition and transformations during synthesis, reduction, and catalytic operation, which are critical for understanding catalyst activity and stability.
- **Findings:** The phase composition varies systematically with the Al₂O₃/ZnAl₂O₄ ratio (x value) in the support. For x = 0 (REF0), the fresh catalyst contains Fe⁰ (major) and ZnAl₂O₄ (major); after use, Fe₅C₂ (minor) and ZnAl₂O₄ (major) are present. For x = 1 (N1), fresh samples show Fe⁰ (minor), FeO (major), ZnAl₂O₄ (major), and θ-Al₂O₃ (minor); after use, Fe₅C₂ (minor), ZnAl₂O₄ (major), and θ-Al₂O₃ (minor) are observed. For x = 5 (N5), the fresh catalyst contains Fe⁰ (major), FeO (minor), α-Al₂O₃ (major), β-Al₂O₃ (minor), and ZnAl₂O₄ (minor); after use, Fe₅C₂ (minor), α-Al₂O₃ (major), β-Al₂O₃ (minor), and ZnAl₂O₄ (minor) are present. For x = ∞ (REF∞), Fe⁰ (major), β-Al₂O₃ (minor), and α-Al₂O₃ (few) are found in the fresh state, while β-Al₂O₃ (major) and α-Al₂O₃ (minor) dominate after use.
- **Observations:** The formation of Fe₅C₂ (Hägg carbide) after reaction indicates successful carburization of iron, which is associated with high Fischer–Tropsch activity. The support composition directly influences the distribution of spinel and alumina phases, impacting catalyst performance.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen physisorption (BET analysis) was performed using a Quantachrome NOVA1000 analyzer to determine specific surface area, pore size distribution, and pore volume. These parameters affect reactant diffusion and active site accessibility.
- **Findings:** The BET surface areas (m²/g) for REF0, N1, N5, and REF∞ are 1.1, 10.7, 19.6, and 4.7, respectively. Major pore sizes (nm) are 3.9, 3.7, 1.6, and 3.3; total pore volumes (cm³/g) are 0.013, 0.168, 0.121, and 0.066; average pore diameters (4V/A, nm) are 48.9, 62.9, 24.7, and 56.0, respectively. All samples exhibit type IV isotherms with H3 hysteresis loops, indicative of mesoporosity and slit-shaped pores.
- **Observations:** Binary phase supports (N1, N5) display higher surface areas and pore volumes than single-phase supports, suggesting improved textural properties that can enhance catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Basicity
- **Description:** CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) was used to assess surface basicity, with desorption peaks corresponding to weakly and strongly adsorbed CO₂. Basicity influences hydrogenation suppression and olefin selectivity.
- **Findings:** All catalysts exhibit two main CO₂ desorption peaks: a low-temperature peak (152–186°C) for weakly adsorbed CO₂ and a high-temperature peak (680–732°C) for strongly adsorbed CO₂. Catalysts with higher alumina content (N5, N1) show greater total CO₂-TPD peak areas and higher high-temperature peak intensities.
- **Observations:** Enhanced basicity in N1 and N5 correlates with higher C₂–C₄ olefin/paraffin ratios and improved light olefin selectivity, indicating that surface basicity is a key factor in tuning product distribution.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, JSM-7001F) was employed to observe particle morphology and phase distribution. Morphological features can affect catalyst dispersion and accessibility.
- **Findings:** FESEM images reveal that REF0 consists of tiny, irregular-shaped particles (~30 nm), while REF∞ displays α-Al₂O₃ cubic particles (100–300 nm) and β-Al₂O₃ flakes (thickness ~80 nm, edge length 200–1500 nm). N1 exhibits cube/cuboid-shaped particles with edge sizes of 400–800 nm.
- **Observations:** The presence of mixed-phase supports leads to more uniform and larger particle morphologies, which may contribute to improved catalyst stability and performance.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Agilent 725) was used to quantify elemental contents, including residual sodium from the precipitant.
- **Findings:** Sodium content in the supports (SP0, SP1, SP5, SP∞) was measured and reported in Table S1, reflecting the influence of sodium bicarbonate used during synthesis.
- **Observations:** Residual sodium may affect the formation of β-Al₂O₃ and influence catalyst basicity and phase composition.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch conditions (2.0 MPa, 340 °C, H₂/CO/Ar = 45/45/10, SV = 1500 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹), the catalyst with x = 1 (N1) achieved a CO conversion of 38.3%. Comparative performance follows the order: N1 (x = 1) > N5 (x = 5) > REF0 (x = 0) > REF∞ (x = ∞).
- **Observations and Trends:** The N1 catalyst demonstrates the highest activity, attributed to the optimized balance of ZnAl₂O₄ and Al₂O₃ in the support, which enhances iron dispersion and active phase formation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift and oxidation reactions:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity values are consistently reported for all catalysts. N1 exhibits moderate CO₂ selectivity, balancing olefin production and CO₂ formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing alumina content (higher x) tends to increase CO₂ selectivity, correlating with enhanced basicity and water-gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, a key indicator of hydrogenation activity:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For N1, CH₄ selectivity is 7.7% at 38.3% CO conversion and remains below 9% across tested conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** N1’s methane selectivity is significantly lower than reference Fe-based FTO catalysts (e.g., 19% for S/Na-promoted Fe), indicating effective suppression of excessive hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–C₄ Olefin Selectivity and Olefin/Paraffin Ratio
- **Description:** C₂–C₄ olefin selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming light olefins (C₂–C₄), while the olefin/paraffin (O/P) ratio indicates the preference for unsaturated over saturated hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** N1 achieves a C₂–C₄ hydrocarbon selectivity of 52.1%, with olefin fractions of ethylene (90.7%), propylene (94.7%), and butylene (92.9%). The O/P ratio for C₂–C₄ is notably high, reflecting strong olefin selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of ZnAl₂O₄ in the support (lower x) enhances olefin selectivity and O/P ratio, while higher alumina content increases paraffin formation.

#### 6.2.5 C₅⁺ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity quantifies the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms.
- **Experimental Data:** Catalysts with higher x values (more Al₂O₃) show increased C₅⁺ selectivity at lower temperatures, but this decreases as temperature rises.
- **Observations and Trends:** Mixed ZnAl₂O₄–Al₂O₃ supports (N1, N5) display a non-monotonic trend: C₅⁺ selectivity first decreases then increases with CO conversion, indicating a balance between chain growth and product desorption.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate (h}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** All catalysts exhibit stable performance with minimal deactivation over the tested period. N1 maintains high CO conversion and selectivity, indicating robust stability.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimized support composition in N1 contributes to its superior resistance to deactivation compared to other formulations.

**Summary:**  
The 15%Fe/2%K₂O/83%(ZnAl₂O₄·1Al₂O₃) catalyst (N1) demonstrates outstanding Fischer–Tropsch performance, combining high CO conversion, ultra-low methane selectivity, and exceptional light olefin selectivity and O/P ratio. Systematic variation of the support composition reveals that moderate alumina content optimizes both activity and selectivity, while also ensuring catalyst stability under industrially relevant conditions.
