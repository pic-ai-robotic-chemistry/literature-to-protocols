## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis procedures for the Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ catalyst and the pretreatment of ZSM‑5, ensuring reproducibility and precision.

#### • Co‑impregnation Method for Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ Catalyst
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation:**
   - Dissolve 10.90 g ferric citrate and 0.12 g sodium citrate in 50 mL deionized water.
   - Add 5 g ZrO₂ to the solution and stir vigorously to ensure homogeneous mixing.
   - *Reagents:* Ferric citrate, sodium citrate, ZrO₂.
   - *Observations:* Uniform solution before water removal.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition:**  
   - Although a specific precipitation–deposition step is not reported, the co‑impregnation process implicitly relies on intimate contact between the dissolved species and the support.
  
3. **Drying:**
   - Evaporate excess water at 50 °C.
   - Dry the resultant mixture at 80 °C overnight.
   - *Equipment:* Drying oven.

4. **Calcination:**
   - Calcine the dried sample in air at 400 °C for 5 hours.
   - *Equipment:* Controlled furnace.
   - *Observation:* Transformation of precursor to the active catalyst phase is achieved during calcination.

#### • Pretreatment of ZSM‑5 Component
1. **Calcination Under NH₃ Flow:**
   - Pre‑treat 3.0 g ZSM‑5 by calcination under an NH₃ flow (50 mL min⁻¹) for 12 hours.
   - Treatment is performed at various temperatures (300, 400, 500, or 700 °C) to tailor the acidic properties.
2. **Post‑Treatment:**
   - After calcination, purge the sample with an Ar flow for 1 hour at the same treatment temperature.
   - *Significance:* Modification of both Lewis and Brønsted acid sites is achieved, influencing subsequent catalytic performance.

#### • Preparation of Composite Tandem Catalyst
- Physically mix the pelletized Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ (particle size: 250–380 μm) with similarly sized ZSM‑5 in a mass ratio of 2:1.
- *Advantage:* This approach leverages the synergetic catalysis of CO hydrogenation on the Fe‑based phase and olefin aromatization over the zeolite component.

### Comparative Analysis
- **Co‑impregnation Method:**  
  • Advantages: Simplicity, uniform distribution of components.  
  • Limitations: Scalability challenges due to precise control requirements.
- **NH₃ Pretreatment of ZSM‑5:**  
  • Advantages: Tailorable acidity and enhanced catalytic coupling.  
  • Limitations: Additional processing steps and energy input.

The detailed numerical values and procedural sequencing provided ensure that the reported synthesis can be faithfully replicated in any well‐equipped laboratory.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

##### 3.1.1.1 Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ Catalyst
- **Ferric Citrate (Fe Citrate)**: Weighed 10.90 g.
- **Sodium Citrate**: Weighed 0.12 g.
- **Zirconium Dioxide (ZrO₂)**: Weighed 5 g.

##### 3.1.1.2 Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂/ZSM‑5 Tandem Catalyst
- **ZSM‑5**: Weighed to achieve a mass ratio of 1 relative to Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂; the final composite is formulated by mixing Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ with ZSM‑5 in a 2:1 mass ratio.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ Catalyst
- **Deionized Water**: Poured 50 mL into the beaker to dissolve the ferric citrate and sodium citrate, ensuring complete dissolution of the metal precursors before the addition of ZrO₂.

##### 3.1.2.2 Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂/ZSM‑5 Tandem Catalyst
- No additional liquid transfer is specified for the tandem catalyst; the physical mixing step follows the separate preparation of each component.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ Catalyst
- **Metal Salt Solution**: Stirred vigorously to ensure full dissolution of ferric citrate and sodium citrate. Following this, 5 g of ZrO₂ is added with continued stirring. The resulting mixture is subjected to evaporation at 50 °C, then dried overnight at 80 °C prior to calcination, ensuring homogeneous catalyst precursor formation.

##### 3.1.3.2 Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂/ZSM‑5 Tandem Catalyst
- **Composite Catalyst Preparation**: After separately preparing the Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ component, the pelletized material (with particle sizes ranging from 250–380 μm) is physically mixed with an appropriately weighed ZSM‑5. This manual mixing, conducted under ambient conditions, achieves uniform distribution and the targeted 2:1 mass ratio essential for the tandem catalyst functionality.

The outlined procedures emphasize precise weighing of solid reagents and controlled liquid transfer to ensure reproducibility. Vigorous and methodical mixing – first to prepare the catalyst precursor solution and subsequently to form the composite catalyst – plays a critical role in achieving high catalyst performance in the Fischer–Tropsch experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 10.90 g ferric citrate, 0.12 g sodium citrate, and 50 mL deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Ambient temperature for dissolution with continuous stirring.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve 10.90 g of ferric citrate and 0.12 g of sodium citrate in 50 mL deionized water. Vigorous stirring ensures complete dissolution and a homogeneous solution.
  - Add 5 g of zirconium dioxide gradually into the precursor solution, maintaining continuous agitation to ensure even dispersion.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure that dissolving and stirring are performed thoroughly to avoid any inhomogeneity prior to adding ZrO₂.

##### Step 2: Assembly, Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Catalyst Assembly**: The assembled precursor undergoes coimpregnation wherein zirconium dioxide serves as the support material. Vigorous stirring during the addition is crucial for uniform distribution.
- **Purification**: No additional purification such as centrifugation or washing is performed, as reagents are used as received.
- **Drying & Calcination**:
  - **Drying**: The homogeneous mixture is first evaporated at 50 °C to remove excess water, then dried overnight at 80 °C.
  - **Calcination**: The dried precursor is calcined in an air atmosphere at 400 °C for 5 hours. The controlled heating ensures proper formation of the Fe-based active species.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂/ZSM‑5 Tandem Catalyst

##### Step 1: ZSM‑5 Pretreatment
- **Reagents**: ZSM‑5 zeolite (Si/Al = 18).
- **Conditions**: Calcination under an NH₃ flow of 50 mL/min for 12 hours at 300, 400, 500, or 700 °C.
- **Process**:
  - Calcine the ZSM‑5 zeolite at the designated temperature to modify its acidic properties.
  - Purge the zeolite with Ar at 50 mL/min for 1 hour post-treatment.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform treatment ensures the controlled adjustment of Brønsted and Lewis acid sites.

##### Step 2: Physical Mixing
- **Reagents**: Pelletized Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ (sized 250–380 μm) and similarly sized pretreated ZSM‑5.
- **Conditions**: Mass ratio of 2:1 (Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂:ZSM‑5).
- **Process**:
  - Mechanically mix the two components to obtain the tandem catalyst, ensuring homogeneity to enable effective synergy between CO hydrogenation and olefin aromatization.

This clear, sequential approach—from precursor preparation through calcination and physical mixing—ensures reproducibility and precise control over the catalyst synthesis process.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for the Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ component is carried out in two distinct steps that prepare the precursor materials for Fischer–Tropsch conversion.

#### 5.1.1 Calcination in Air  
- **Activation Temperature:** The catalyst precursor is calcined at 400 °C. This temperature is critical for decomposing the ferric and sodium citrate compounds and removing residual water, thereby forming a well‐dispersed oxide phase on the ZrO₂ support.  
- **Activation Gas:** An oxygen‐rich air atmosphere is used during calcination. This environment ensures complete oxidation of organic residues and proper precursor transformation without introducing reducing conditions.  
- **Activation Time:** The calcination step is maintained for 5 hours, ensuring uniform decomposition and stabilization of the Fe₂O₃ phase prior to any reduction treatments.  
- **Observations:** This controlled thermal treatment promotes the formation of a reactive iron oxide phase that, under subsequent reduction conditions, is predisposed for efficient in situ carburization during CO hydrogenation.

#### 5.1.2 Pre‐Reaction Reduction  
- **Activation Temperature:** Following calcination, the catalyst is reduced at 350 °C. This moderate temperature is optimized to initiate the conversion of the oxide phase into the active species needed for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis without inducing sintering.  
- **Activation Pressure:** Reduction is performed at atmospheric pressure, a condition that minimizes catalyst agglomeration while providing adequate exposure to the reducing gas.  
- **Activation Gas:** The reduction utilizes a hydrogen‐rich atmosphere (pure H₂ or a controlled H₂/Ar mixture) delivered at a flow rate of 20 mL/min. This gas environment is essential for reducing the Fe species and setting the stage for subsequent carburization into active iron carbide phases during reaction conditions.  
- **Activation Time:** A 6‐hour reduction period ensures complete transformation of the precursor while balancing the risk of over-reduction and sintering.  
- **Observations:** The precise control of temperature, gas composition, and duration during this step is confirmed by improved CO hydrogenation kinetics and enhanced formation of iron carbides, which are directly linked to higher catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

These activation steps, executed in continuous-flow fixed-bed reactors with accurate temperature monitoring (e.g., via a central K-type thermocouple), ensure reproducibility and a well-prepared catalyst for reliable syngas conversion.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase evolution of the catalysts were investigated using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases and monitoring in situ transformations during reaction.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the fresh Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ catalyst revealed the presence of Fe₂O₃ as the primary iron phase. After 10 hours of CO hydrogenation, the Fe₂O₃ peaks disappeared, and new peaks emerged at 44.2°, corresponding to Hagg iron carbide (Fe₅C₂), alongside peaks for Fe₃O₄ (magnetite). The tandem Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂/ZSM‑5 catalyst exhibited a higher fraction of Fe₅C₂ (88.4%) compared to Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ alone (51.5%), as confirmed by Mössbauer spectroscopy.
- **Observations:** The enhanced formation of Fe₅C₂ in the presence of ZSM‑5 indicates improved carburization, which is closely linked to increased CO hydrogenation activity and olefin selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Dispersion and Morphology
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping were employed to assess catalyst morphology and the spatial distribution of active species.
- **Findings:** SEM images showed that both Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ and NH₃-treated ZSM‑5 maintained their pristine crystal structures, with aggregated small particles and uniform morphology. EDX mapping confirmed that Fe species were evenly dispersed on ZrO₂ microspheres, with carbon and iron elements co-located.
- **Observations:** The uniform dispersion of Fe species, attributed to the coimpregnation synthesis method, supports efficient catalytic performance by maximizing active site accessibility.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ adsorption–desorption (BET) analysis was conducted to determine surface area and pore structure, which influence reactant diffusion and catalyst accessibility.
- **Findings:** Both parent and NH₃-treated ZSM‑5 samples retained their typical microporous structure, with only minor changes in mesoporous pore area. Samples were degassed under vacuum at 300 °C for 5 hours prior to measurement, ensuring accurate assessment.
- **Observations:** The preservation of microporosity in ZSM‑5 after NH₃ treatment ensures the zeolite’s functionality in tandem catalysis.

#### 6.1.4 Acidic Properties of ZSM‑5
- **Description:** NH₃ temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) and pyridine adsorption FTIR (Py-IR) were used to quantify Brønsted and Lewis acid sites, which are critical for olefin aromatization.
- **Findings:** NH₃-TPD revealed a significant decline in total acid amount with increasing NH₃ pretreatment temperature. Py-IR showed that moderate NH₃ treatments (300–400 °C) reduced Lewis acid sites, while Brønsted acid sites remained stable; severe treatment at 700 °C led to a marked decrease in Brønsted acidity.
- **Observations:** The tunable acidity of ZSM‑5 directly impacts product selectivity, with higher Brønsted acidity favoring aromatic formation.

#### 6.1.5 Iron Species Differentiation
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy provided quantitative differentiation of Fe species, crucial for correlating phase composition with catalytic behavior.
- **Findings:** The spectra identified two sextets (magnetic hyperfine fields: 50.7 and 48.8 T) corresponding to large-particle Fe₂O₃, and a doublet (IS: 0.34 mm s⁻¹, QS: 0.77 mm s⁻¹) attributed to superparamagnetic Fe³⁺ in small Fe₂O₃ particles. After reaction, a higher fraction of Fe₅C₂ was observed in the tandem catalyst.
- **Observations:** The increased proportion of iron carbide in the presence of ZSM‑5 is associated with enhanced catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Kinetic Parameters
- **Description:** Steady-state isotopic transient kinetic analysis (SSITKA) measured surface residence times and concentrations of CO and CHₓ intermediates, providing insight into reaction mechanisms.
- **Findings:** The tandem catalyst exhibited increased surface residence time and higher CO adsorption, with significantly enhanced CO surface concentrations compared to Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ alone.
- **Observations:** These kinetic enhancements correlate with improved CO conversion rates and product selectivity in the tandem system.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100\%
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (280 °C, 3 MPa, 2400 mL g⁻¹ h⁻¹), Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ achieves a CO conversion rate of 0.42 μmol g⁻¹ s⁻¹, while the Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂/ZSM‑5 tandem catalyst reaches 0.52 μmol g⁻¹ s⁻¹. SSITKA experiments confirm these values and show increased CO surface residence time and concentration for the tandem catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of ZSM‑5 enhances CO conversion, attributed to improved CO adsorption and increased formation of reactive intermediates. The physical mixing ratio (2:1) of Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ to ZSM‑5 optimizes both CO activation and subsequent transformation steps.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift reaction. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100\%
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ formation rates are 0.19 mmol g⁻¹ min⁻¹ for Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ and 0.20 mmol g⁻¹ min⁻¹ for Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂/ZSM‑5.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity remains similar across catalysts, indicating that the presence of ZSM‑5 primarily affects hydrocarbon product distribution rather than the water-gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
\[
S_{\text{CH}_4} = \frac{n_{\text{CH}_4}}{\sum n_j} \times 100\%
\]
where \( n_{\text{CH}_4} \) is the carbon in CH₄ and the denominator sums all hydrocarbon products (excluding CO₂).
- **Experimental Data:** Methane formation rates are 0.04 mmol g⁻¹ min⁻¹ for Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ and 0.06 mmol g⁻¹ min⁻¹ for the tandem catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of ZSM‑5 slightly increases CH₄ selectivity, likely due to modified hydrogen transfer and reaction pathways.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–₄ and Aromatic Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity for light olefins (C₂–₄) and aromatics is determined as the fraction of these products among all hydrocarbons (excluding CO₂).
- **Experimental Data:** For Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂, C₂–₄ olefin selectivity is 32% and aromatic selectivity is 5%. Upon addition of ZSM‑5, C₂–₄ selectivity drops to 4%, while aromatic selectivity rises to 36%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The tandem catalyst efficiently converts light olefins into aromatics, demonstrating a strong bifunctional effect. Modification of ZSM‑5 acidity via NH₃ treatment further tunes the balance between olefin and aromatic selectivity.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of hydrocarbon chain growth, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution. Higher α values indicate a greater tendency to form longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** α is 0.758 for Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂ and increases to 0.831 for Na‑Fe‑ZrO₂/ZSM‑5.
- **Observations and Trends:** The increase in α with ZSM‑5 addition correlates with enhanced selectivity toward longer-chain hydrocarbons and aromatics, reflecting improved chain growth and coupling processes.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and product selectivity over time, with high carbon balance indicating minimal deactivation.
- **Experimental Data:** Both catalysts maintain a carbon balance exceeding 90% during extended operation, with no significant decline in CO conversion or aromatic selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The tandem system exhibits robust stability, attributed to the synergistic interaction between Fe-based and zeolite components, and the optimized reaction environment.
