## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the aqueous precipitation approach employed for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts based on iron oxides and illustrates two distinct thermal treatments leading to α‑Fe₂O₃ and γ‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods. The synthesis methods are reported with precise reactant concentrations, temperatures, durations, and equipment to ensure reproducibility and allow critical comparative analysis of the advantages and disadvantages in terms of synthesis complexity, scalability, cost, and equipment requirements.

#### Aqueous Precipitation Followed by Calcination (α‑Fe₂O₃ Nanorods)
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O in water with additives NaCl and PEG to initiate the formation of a β‑FeOOH precursor.  
   • Add a Na₂CO₃ aqueous solution under continuous stirring, allowing the precipitation–deposition of the precursor.
2. **Drying**  
   • Dry the β‑FeOOH precipitate at 50 °C for 6 h under vacuum to remove residual moisture.
3. **Calcination**  
   • Calcine the dried precursor at 500 °C for 5 h in a furnace, yielding the α‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods.  
4. **Additional Post‑processing**  
   • Post‐treatment includes pellet pressing at approximately 10 MPa and sieving (20–40 mesh) for reaction testing.

#### Aqueous Precipitation Followed by PEG Reflux (γ‑Fe₂O₃ Nanorods, with Optional Mn Promotion)
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • The initial steps mirror the α‑Fe₂O₃ procedure: dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O with NaCl and PEG, then add Na₂CO₃ under stirring to form the β‑FeOOH precipitate.
2. **Precipitation–Deposition and Reflux Heating**  
   • Instead of immediate calcination, subject the precursor to a reflux in PEG at 200 °C for 24 h under a nitrogen atmosphere to obtain γ‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods.
3. **Wet Impregnation for Mn Promotion (if applicable)**  
   • Impregnate using an ethylene glycol solution of Mn(NO₃)₂·4H₂O to achieve 0.5 wt% Mn promotion, followed by drying at 200 °C.
4. **Post‑processing**  
   • Final steps include pellet pressing and sieving, ensuring uniform particle size for catalytic evaluation.

This organized approach—with clearly delineated steps and controlled reaction parameters—ensures that researchers can replicate the procedures and compare the functional benefits of each synthesis route.

- **Precursor mixture**: Mixed under a nitrogen atmosphere at 120 °C while stirring steadily throughout the reagent addition to achieve a uniform suspension.

This structured approach—grouping operations by catalyst type and process step—ensures clarity and reproducibility in the experimental setup, fulfilling the detailed requirements for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis catalyst preparation.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: α‑Fe₂O₃ Nanorods
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 5.38 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 11.60 g NaCl, and 10 mL PEG dissolved in 190 mL deionized water; Na₂CO₃ is used as the precipitating agent.
- **Conditions**: The mixture is heated to 120 °C with continuous stirring (at a controlled addition rate of 5.5 mL/min for the Na₂CO₃ solution) to promote homogeneous reaction conditions.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O, NaCl, and PEG in water until a clear solution is achieved.
  - Gradually add the Na₂CO₃ solution to precipitate β‑FeOOH, ensuring even pH adjustment for complete precursor formation.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain a uniform reaction mixture and controlled pH throughout the precipitation stage.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Process**:
  - Wash the formed β‑FeOOH precipitate thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual soluble impurities.
  - Dry the washed material under vacuum at 50 °C for 6 hours.
  
##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Confirm complete removal of solvents during vacuum drying.
- **Calcination**: Calcine the dried precursor at 500 °C for 5 hours in air, ensuring a controlled temperature ramp to convert β‑FeOOH into well-defined α‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: γ‑Fe₂O₃ Nanorods (with/without Mn Promotion)
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents & Process**: Follow the same meticulous precursor preparation as outlined for α‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods.
  
##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly via PEG Reflux
- **Conditions**: Reflux the β‑FeOOH precursor in PEG at 200 °C for 24 hours under a continuous nitrogen stream, which provides an inert atmosphere that facilitates the controlled formation of γ‑Fe₂O₃.
- **Process**:
  - Transfer the precursor into a reflux system.
  - Maintain strict N₂ flushing to prevent oxidation during the prolonged heating period.

##### Step 3: Mn Promotion (if applicable)
- **Reagents**: Use an ethylene glycol solution of Mn(NO₃)₂·4H₂O.
- **Process**:
  - Impregnate the cooled γ‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods with the Mn-containing solution.
  - Subsequent drying is carried out at 200 °C under vacuum to achieve uniform promoter dispersion.

##### Step 4: Final Assembly
- **Process**:
  - Press the final catalyst materials into pellets at approximately 10 MPa.
  - Sieve the pellets to obtain a particle size range of 20–40 mesh, ensuring reproducible conditions for Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis involves an in situ carburization step designed to convert the pre-synthesized iron oxide nanorods into active iron carbide phases. This activation is crucial for exposing and stabilizing active sites conducive to high CO conversion and light olefin selectivity.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is the key variable for facilitating carburization, enabling the conversion of iron oxides (α‑Fe₂O₃ or γ‑Fe₂O₃) to the active carbide phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are treated at 340 °C during the carburization step.
- **Observations:** This moderate temperature is optimized to achieve sufficient carbide formation while preventing over-sintering of the nanorods.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the kinetics of reduction and carburization by controlling the gas–solid interactions during treatment.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The carburization is conducted under a pressure of 0.1 MPa, approximately corresponding to atmospheric conditions.
- **Observations:** Operating at this pressure ensures an effective transformation of the precursor oxides without promoting catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation provides both the reducing environment and the carbon source necessary for carbide formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with a H₂/CO ratio of 1 is used during the activation step.
- **Observations:** This balanced gas mixture supports both reduction (via H₂) and carburization (via CO), leading to the formation of target carbide phases verified by ex situ analyses.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity (STV) indicates the flow rate of the activation gas relative to the catalyst weight, influencing the exposure of catalyst surfaces.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The process is carried out at a weight hourly space velocity (W/F) of 2.5 g_cat·h·mol⁻¹.
- **Observations:** This controlled gas flow ensures uniform activation while minimizing the risk of local overheating or incomplete reaction.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The activation duration is critical for ensuring complete carburization of the catalyst without excessive exposure that might lead to sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are activated for 10 hours.
- **Observations:** The 10-hour treatment provides an optimal balance, resulting in a stable and reproducible active phase that enhances Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis reactions were carried out under precise operating conditions to ensure reproducibility and accurate catalytic evaluation. Detailed reaction parameters are reported below.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature plays a crucial role in both the reduction of iron oxides and the formation of active carbide phases. In the experimental setup, the reaction was conducted at 320 °C. The temperature control involved a two-step process: initially, the catalyst was heated from room temperature to 340 °C at a controlled rate of 2 °C·min⁻¹, followed by a cooling phase to 80 °C in a nitrogen atmosphere, and finally, reheated to 320 °C at 10 °C·min⁻¹. This protocol facilitated steady syngas activation and contributed to the stabilization of CO conversion over time.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Operating under a total system pressure of 1.0 MPa, the experimental conditions ensured an enhanced syngas concentration within the fixed-bed reactor. The chosen pressure not only contributed to improved CO conversion but also favored the formation of active iron carbide phases, which are essential for selective hydrocarbon synthesis.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The molar ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed gas was maintained at 1. This ratio is critical to the carburization process and helps balance the hydrogenation capacity during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, thereby influencing hydrocarbon distribution and selectivity. Maintaining a constant H₂/CO ratio ensured a consistent reaction environment and minimized variations in product outcomes.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Weight Hourly Space Velocity
A weight hourly space velocity (W/F) of 5 g_cat·h·mol⁻¹ was applied, which defines the reactor’s reactant exposure time relative to the catalyst mass. This parameter was instrumental in controlling the contact time between the reactants and the catalysts, directly affecting CO conversion and the evolution of active carbide phases. The chosen W/F contributed to the steady-state performance observed after 20 hours of continuous operation, with CO conversion stabilizing at 52.7% for α‑Fe₂O₃ and 57.1% for γ‑Fe₂O₃ catalysts.

Overall, these well-defined reaction conditions enable the reproducible execution of Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments, offering insights into the kinetics and stability of the catalysts under study.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Identification and Evolution
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase evolution of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman spectroscopy. These techniques are essential for distinguishing between α‑Fe₂O₃ (hematite), γ‑Fe₂O₃ (maghemite), and their transformation products during carburization and reaction.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirmed the successful synthesis of pure-phase α‑Fe₂O₃ and γ‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods, with α‑Fe₂O₃ matching JCPDS 33-0664 and γ‑Fe₂O₃ verified by both XRD and the presence of broad Raman features at 353, 491, 665, and 720 cm⁻¹. After carburization in syngas, γ‑Fe₂O₃ predominantly transformed into Hägg carbide (χ‑Fe₅C₂, JCPDS 36-1248), while α‑Fe₂O₃ formed cementite (θ‑Fe₃C, JCPDS 76-1877), sometimes coexisting with χ‑Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The initial iron oxide phase dictates the nature of the resulting iron carbide, which is directly linked to catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) were employed to assess catalyst morphology and particle dimensions, which influence surface area and active site accessibility.
- **Findings:** Both α‑Fe₂O₃ and γ‑Fe₂O₃ samples exhibited uniform nanorod morphology with an average diameter of 40 nm and length of 500 nm. HRTEM revealed interplanar spacings of 0.250 nm for the (110) plane of α‑Fe₂O₃ and 0.295 nm for the (220) plane of γ‑Fe₂O₃.
- **Observations:** The use of NaCl and PEG during synthesis ensured consistent nanorod dimensions, enabling direct comparison of phase effects.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms (BET analysis) were used to determine surface area and porosity, which affect catalyst dispersion and reactant diffusion.
- **Findings:** Samples were degassed at 180 °C for 6 h prior to measurement. Nitrogen adsorption was performed at −196 °C using a Micrometrics ASAP 2020 instrument. While specific surface area values are not detailed in the provided context, the methodology ensures reliable assessment of textural properties.
- **Observations:** Uniform nanorod morphology and controlled synthesis conditions contribute to comparable surface areas across samples.

#### 6.1.4 Electronic Structure and Oxidation States
- **Description:** In situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analyses were conducted to probe the oxidation states and local environments of Fe and Mn species.
- **Findings:** XANES at the Fe K-edge, analyzed with Demeter software, revealed phase compositions and coordination changes upon carburization. Mössbauer spectroscopy at room temperature further identified the presence and distribution of χ‑Fe₅C₂ and θ‑Fe₃C phases, as well as superparamagnetic Fe species. In situ XPS confirmed the evolution of Fe oxidation states under reaction conditions.
- **Observations:** The formation of specific iron carbide phases and the modulation of Fe–Mn interactions by Mn promotion are critical for tuning catalytic selectivity and activity.

#### 6.1.5 Reducibility and CO Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂‑TPR) and CO temperature-programmed desorption (CO‑TPD) were used to evaluate catalyst reducibility and CO adsorption characteristics.
- **Findings:** H₂‑TPR profiles indicated distinct reduction behaviors for α‑Fe₂O₃ and γ‑Fe₂O₃, reflecting their different phase stabilities. CO‑TPD experiments, conducted after reduction and CO adsorption at 50 °C, revealed differences in CO adsorption capacity and desorption temperatures, indicative of varied active site distributions.
- **Observations:** The reducibility and CO adsorption properties are closely linked to the initial crystal structure and the presence of Mn, influencing the formation of active carbide phases and overall catalytic performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (320 °C, 1.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1, W/F = 5 g_cat·h·mol⁻¹), the α‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods catalyst exhibits an initial CO conversion of 56.1%, stabilizing at 52.7% after 20 hours. The γ‑Fe₂O₃ nanorods catalyst shows a higher initial conversion of 82.5%, stabilizing at 57.1% after 20 hours. Mn promotion influences these values: for 0.5 wt% Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃, the stabilized CO conversion is 54.3%.
- **Observations and Trends:** γ‑Fe₂O₃-based catalysts display higher initial activity, while Mn addition slightly decreases CO conversion but enhances selectivity. Both catalyst types maintain stable conversion over extended operation, indicating good reaction stability.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At stabilized conversion, CO₂ selectivity values are: γ‑Fe₂O₃ (34.1%), 0.3 wt% Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃ (30.4%), 0.5 wt% Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃ (31.7%), 1 wt% Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃ (33.9%), α‑Fe₂O₃ (28.0%), 0.3 wt% Mn/α‑Fe₂O₃ (22.5%), 0.5 wt% Mn/α‑Fe₂O₃ (19.1%), and 1 wt% Mn/α‑Fe₂O₃ (20.2%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Mn promotion reduces CO₂ selectivity, particularly on α‑Fe₂O₃, indicating suppressed water–gas shift activity and improved carbon efficiency.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming methane, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Stabilized CH₄ selectivity: γ‑Fe₂O₃ (23.5%), 0.3 wt% Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃ (15.2%), 0.5 wt% Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃ (11.7%), 1 wt% Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃ (14%), α‑Fe₂O₃ (19.2%), 0.3 wt% Mn/α‑Fe₂O₃ (17.1%), 0.5 wt% Mn/α‑Fe₂O₃ (18.3%), 1 wt% Mn/α‑Fe₂O₃ (17.5%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Mn addition significantly suppresses methane formation on γ‑Fe₂O₃, favoring the production of higher hydrocarbons and olefins.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ and Light Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of carbon in hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, while light olefin selectivity (C₂–C₄) indicates the proportion of short-chain olefins. C₅⁺ selectivity is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\text{C atoms in C}_5^+}{\text{Total C atoms in hydrocarbons (CO}_2\text{-free)}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For 0.5 wt% Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃, C₅⁺ selectivity is 18.2% and light olefin selectivity reaches 61.2%. Non-promoted γ‑Fe₂O₃ shows C₅⁺ selectivity of 27.7% and light olefin selectivity of 25.4%. For α‑Fe₂O₃, light olefin selectivity is 44.5% (non-promoted) and 51.6% (0.5 wt% Mn).
- **Observations and Trends:** Mn promotion on γ‑Fe₂O₃ dramatically increases light olefin selectivity while reducing C₅⁺ selectivity, indicating a shift toward shorter-chain products.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over time on stream.
- **Experimental Data:** Both α‑Fe₂O₃ and γ‑Fe₂O₃ catalysts, especially with optimal Mn loading (0.5 wt%), maintain stable CO conversion and product selectivity over 20–50 hours, with minimal decline.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Mn/γ‑Fe₂O₃ catalyst demonstrates high operational stability, supporting its suitability for prolonged Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
