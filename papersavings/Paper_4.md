## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis of Fe₃O₄@χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalysts via a surface reconstruction route from well‐defined Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals. Two distinct catalyst geometries—nanocubes and octahedra—are prepared following an identical overall approach that emphasizes reproducibility and precise control of reaction parameters.

#### Catalyst Synthesis via Surface Reconstruction

• Step 1: Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
 1. Dissolve Fe(acac)₃ in a solvent mixture (for nanocubes, BE and OA; for octahedra, BE, OA, and OAm).  
 2. Degas the mixture at 120 °C, then heat to 290 °C (nanocubes) or to sequentially 220 °C and 300 °C (octahedra) to promote nucleation and growth of Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals.  
 3. A precipitation–deposition process occurs as the reaction advances, evidenced by color change and precipitate formation, allowing for efficient separation of the nanocrystals before subsequent processing.

• Step 2: Reduction  
 1. Load the pre‑synthesized Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals into a fixed‑bed reactor equipped with a quartz tube (inner diameter: 9 mm).  
 2. Reduce the nanocrystals in H₂ at 270 °C for 10 h under a controlled gas flow of 100 mL min⁻¹ at 1 bar.  
 • Observations include removal of surface organic residues and transformation toward a metallic state.

• Step 3: Syngas Treatment (Surface Reconstruction)  
 1. Subject the reduced particles to syngas treatment in the same fixed‑bed reactor.  
 2. Operate under 20 bar at 270 °C for 20 h using syngas composed of 64 vol% CO, 32 vol% H₂, and 4 vol% Ar with a space velocity of 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹.  
 • This step facilitates the formation of a χ‑Fe₅C₂ shell with facet‑dependent properties ({202} for nanocubes and {112} for octahedra).

• Step 4: Catalyst Support Incorporation  
 1. Finally, mix the surface‑reconstructed nanocrystals with SiC to yield the supported catalysts (Fe₃O₄@χ‑Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC and octahedra/SiC).  
 2. This support step ensures optimal dispersion and improved stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This structured procedure, with its explicit numerical values and controlled equipment specifications, provides a reproducible workflow for synthesizing advanced Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ Nanocubes/SiC  
- **Nanocube precursor solution**: Mixed by stirring at 300 rpm for 30 minutes under a nitrogen atmosphere while the system is maintained at 120 °C during the degassing step. This controlled environment prevents oxidation and assures homogeneity of the precursor solution.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ Nanocubes/SiC

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Synthesis of Fe₃O₄ Nanocubes  
- **Reagents**: 1.4 g Fe(acac)₃, 1.0 g 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid, 20.0 mL benzyl ether (BE), 2.5 mL oleic acid (OA).  
- **Conditions**: Degas at 120 °C for 30 minutes; heat to 290 °C at 10 °C min⁻¹ and maintain for 30 minutes.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve all reagents in the solvent mixture to form a homogeneous solution.  
  - Degas the solution to remove oxygen and moisture.  
  - Heat the mixture under controlled ramping conditions to produce well‐defined nanocubes.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete precursor dissolution and controlled heating to guide uniform crystal formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly – Reduction and Support Integration  
- **Reagents/Materials**: Pre-synthesized Fe₃O₄ nanocubes and SiC support.  
- **Conditions**: Reduction in H₂ at 270 °C for 10 hours using a fixed-bed reactor (inner diameter 9 mm) with a 100 mL min⁻¹ gas-flow; subsequent syngas treatment (64 vol% H₂, 32 vol% CO, 4 vol% Ar) at 20 bar, 270 °C, space velocity 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹ for 20 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Reduce the nanocubes to metallic Fe, removing surface organic residues.
  - Follow with syngas exposure in the reactor to carburize the metallic core into a χ-Fe₅C₂ shell that predominantly exposes {202} facets.  
  - Integrate the treated particles with SiC to form the final composite.
- **Key Steps**: Careful monitoring of temperature, gas-flow, and reaction time is essential for reproducibility.

##### Step 3: Purification & Post-Treatment  
- **Process**:  
  - After reduction and syngas treatment, wash the particles using ethanol and hexane to remove any unreacted species.  
  - Dry the samples under controlled conditions before catalytic testing.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ Octahedra/SiC

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Synthesis of Fe₃O₄ Octahedra  
- **Reagents**: 1.0 g Fe(acac)₃, 20.0 mL BE, 2.3 mL oleylamine (OAm), 1.6 mL OA.  
- **Conditions**: Degas at 120 °C for 30 minutes; heat first to 220 °C at 20 °C min⁻¹ for 1 hour, then to 300 °C for 2 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Form a homogeneous solution and degas; apply sequential heating steps to induce octahedral growth.  
  - Precipitate the product with ethanol followed by washing with hexane.
  
##### Steps 2 & 3: Catalyst Assembly and Purification  
- Follow similar reduction, syngas treatment, and support integration steps as described for the nanocubes. Notably, the carburization yields a χ-Fe₅C₂ shell predominantly exposing {112} facets, influencing different catalytic characteristics.  
- **Key Points**: Monitor reactor parameters closely and ensure consistent purification to achieve reproducible catalyst performance.

These detailed, sequential steps from raw precursor preparation to catalyst assembly and post-processing provide a replicable workflow for both nanocubes and octahedra materials in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is executed in two distinct stages—an initial H₂ reduction followed by a syngas treatment—to develop the active core–shell structure essential for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 H₂ Reduction

In the first stage, pre‐synthesized Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals are reduced at 270 °C under 1 bar H₂. This step, lasting 10 hours with a controlled flow rate of 100 mL/min, is critical for converting Fe₃O₄ to metallic Fe and for the removal of surface organic residues. The precise temperature ensures uniform reduction, while the steady gas flow guarantees sufficient reduction without catalyst sintering. Researchers noted that these conditions enable complete transformation and a clean surface, facilitating subsequent carburization.

#### 5.1.2 Syngas Treatment

Subsequent to the H₂ reduction, the catalyst undergoes syngas treatment at the same temperature (270 °C) but under a higher pressure of 20 bar. This step lasts for 20 hours and employs a syngas mixture composed of 64 vol% CO, 32 vol% H₂, and 4 vol% Ar. The elevated pressure and specific gas composition are essential for promoting carburization, where carbon from CO transforms the metallic Fe into an iron carbide (χ-Fe₅C₂) shell. The process is controlled by a space–time velocity of 2400 mL/(h·g_cat), ensuring that the reactants adequately interact with the catalyst surface. This controlled exposure is fundamental in producing the desired core–shell architecture and facet exposure, which in turn affect CO dissociation kinetics and overall catalyst performance.

Together, these activation parameters—a consistent temperature of 270 °C, well-defined pressure conditions (1 bar for H₂ reduction and 20 bar for syngas treatment), targeted gas compositions, controlled gas flow, and designated reaction times—are tailored to achieve complete catalyst transformation while maintaining structural integrity. The methodical separation of the reduction and carburization stages also ensures that activation steps remain distinct from any subsequent characterization procedures.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is a critical parameter that influences the activation of syngas and the formation of the active iron carbide phase, which in turn governs the catalyst performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction is conducted at a constant temperature of 270 °C.  
- **Observations:** Maintaining 270 °C promotes effective CO dissociation and stable phase formation, as evidenced by enhanced hydrocarbon chain growth and catalyst longevity. However, long-term tests revealed that, particularly for the octahedral catalysts, prolonged exposure to this temperature could lead to active site blockage due to carbon deposition.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure controls the concentration of syngas (H₂ and CO) in the reactor, thus directly impacting the reaction kinetics and product distribution.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch reaction is carried out under a pressure of 20 bar.  
- **Observations:** This elevated pressure increases the partial pressures of the reactants, thereby accelerating syngas conversion and favoring the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons. It also plays a role in stabilizing the desired iron carbide phase during the catalyst activation and reaction processes.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is essential in dictating the hydrocarbon selectivity and chain growth probability during synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The feed gas comprises 64 vol% H₂, 32 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar, which corresponds to an effective H₂/CO ratio of 2:1.  
- **Observations:** This ratio optimizes the balance between light and heavy hydrocarbon formation, with a higher ratio tending to increase methane selectivity while lower values better promote olefin production.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, expressed as the volumetric flow rate of syngas per unit catalyst mass, determines the exposure time between reactants and catalyst, influencing both conversion efficiency and selectivity.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reported gas hourly space velocity is 2400 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹.  
- **Observations:** This specific value ensures adequate contact time for high CO conversion rates and reproducible catalyst performance, as demonstrated by consistent conversion and selectivity trends in repeated experiments.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are essential for identifying the presence and proportion of active phases, which directly influence Fischer–Tropsch performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed the coexistence of Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂ phases in both nanocube and octahedra catalysts after syngas treatment. Quantitative analysis showed that the χ-Fe₅C₂ content in nanocubes increased after reaction (from ~29% to ~33%), while Fe₃O₄ remained the dominant core phase (~63–72%). Mössbauer spectroscopy corroborated these results, identifying distinct spectral components for Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂ and confirming their proportions. The small lattice mismatch (4.65% for nanocubes, 2.06% for octahedra) and epitaxial orientation between the Fe₃O₄ core and χ-Fe₅C₂ shell were supported by both XRD and high-resolution electron microscopy.
- **Observations:** The epitaxial relationship and increased χ-Fe₅C₂ content after reaction are associated with enhanced catalytic activity, particularly in nanocubes.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology, Facet Exposure, and Shell Thickness
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM), and fast Fourier transform (FFT) analyses were employed to assess particle morphology, facet exposure, and shell thickness. These parameters are critical for understanding structure–activity relationships.
- **Findings:** TEM and HAADF-STEM images confirmed the uniform core–shell architecture for both morphologies. Nanocubes exhibited predominantly {202} facet exposure, while octahedra displayed {112} facets, as verified by FFT analysis. The average particle sizes were 40.5 ± 3.9 nm for nanocubes and 44.8 ± 3.3 nm for octahedra, with high morphological purity (>95%). Shell thickness increased upon reaction: for nanocubes, from 2.0 nm to 2.6 nm; for octahedra, from 1.7 nm to 3.5 nm after 100 h on stream.
- **Observations:** The facet-dependent shell growth and preservation of core–shell structure underpin the distinct catalytic behaviors observed for each morphology.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was used to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which affect reactant accessibility and catalyst stability.
- **Findings:** Both catalysts initially exhibited nonporous surface layers. After 100 h on stream, the octahedra developed mesopores with an average diameter of 16.0 nm, attributed to structural collapse and void formation, while nanocubes retained a nonporous surface.
- **Observations:** The emergence of mesoporosity in octahedra is linked to the Kirkendall effect and may impact long-term catalyst performance.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Chemistry and CO Dissociation Pathways
- **Description:** In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was conducted to probe surface reaction intermediates and CO dissociation mechanisms, which are central to Fischer–Tropsch selectivity.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS spectra at 270 °C revealed a characteristic peak at 1741 cm⁻¹ (C=O stretch in CHO*) for nanocubes, indicating a hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway. This peak was absent in octahedra, which exhibited only direct CO dissociation.
- **Observations:** The facet-dependent CO activation mechanism—hydrogen-assisted on {202} facets (nanocubes) versus direct on {112} facets (octahedra)—explains the observed differences in product selectivity and catalyst stability.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (270 °C, 20 bar, syngas: 64 vol% H₂, 32 vol% CO, 4 vol% Ar, space velocity 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹), Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a CO conversion of **45.4%**, while Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ octahedra/SiC reached **21.2%**.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes, with {202} facet exposure, exhibit more than double the CO conversion of the octahedra ({112} facets), highlighting the critical role of surface structure in catalytic activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The article reports that both catalysts maintain low CO₂ selectivity under standard conditions, with the nanocubes producing less CO₂ relative to the octahedra.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower CO₂ selectivity in nanocubes indicates suppressed water–gas shift activity, favoring hydrocarbon formation over CO₂ production.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The octahedra catalyst produces a higher proportion of methane compared to the nanocubes under identical conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The increased methane selectivity for octahedra correlates with their lower chain growth probability, while nanocubes favor longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the percentage of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator for liquid fuel production.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieve a **C₅⁺ selectivity of 44.8 C%**, whereas octahedra/SiC yield **27.6 C%** under standard conditions. When octahedra are operated at similar conversion (42.6%), their C₅⁺ selectivity increases to **39.6 C%**.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes consistently outperform octahedra in C₅⁺ selectivity, though the gap narrows at matched conversion levels.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from Anderson–Schulz–Flory statistics, quantifies the probability of chain propagation:
  - Values closer to 1 indicate a higher likelihood of forming longer hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values are **0.66** for nanocubes/SiC, **0.62** for octahedra/SiC, and **0.63** for χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC. At similar conversion, octahedra reach **0.64**.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values for nanocubes align with their superior C₅⁺ selectivity and lower methane formation, confirming enhanced chain growth on {202} facets.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time; the deactivation rate is the decline in conversion per hour:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes maintain stable CO conversion (~45.4%) over **100 hours**, with minimal deactivation and lower carbon deposition (6.2 wt% by TGA). Octahedra show a decrease from **20.1% to 11.7%** over the same period.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes demonstrate superior long-term stability, attributed to their facet-dependent reaction mechanism and resistance to carbon buildup.

#### 6.2.7 Olefin-to-Paraffin Ratio (C₂–C₄)
- **Description:** This ratio indicates the selectivity for olefins versus paraffins among C₂–C₄ hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes exhibit a ratio of **1.1**, while octahedra show **0.6**.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher ratio for nanocubes reflects a preference for olefin production, which is industrially desirable for downstream chemical synthesis.

---
**Summary:** The Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC catalyst, characterized by {202} facet exposure, delivers higher CO conversion, greater C₅⁺ selectivity, a higher α factor, and superior stability compared to the octahedra/SiC ({112} facets). These results underscore the critical influence of surface structure on Fischer–Tropsch catalyst performance.
