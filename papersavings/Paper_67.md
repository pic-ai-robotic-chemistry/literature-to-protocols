## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis methods used to prepare Fischer–Tropsch catalysts via incipient wetness impregnation. The reported method employs ammonium iron citrate as the precursor in a 1.4M aqueous solution, ensuring homogeneous deposition of Fe nanoparticles on various supports.

#### • Incipient Wetness Impregnation Procedure

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - The catalyst support (e.g., CNF, α‑Al₂O₃, β‑SiC, SiO₂) is measured and prepared for impregnation.  
   - An aqueous solution of ammonium iron citrate (1.4M) is added gradually until the complete pore volume of the support is filled.  
   - This controlled addition promotes uniform distribution of the iron precursor, minimizing clustering.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - After impregnation, a precipitation–deposition step is performed if required to further promote adherence of the precursor to the support.  
   - In some formulations, careful adjustment of the pH (typically via the dropwise addition of a basic solution) facilitates the formation of a fine precipitate on the support surface.

3. Drying  
   - The impregnated material is first dried at room temperature under a controlled pressure of 60 mbar for 2 hours.  
   - This step is critical to remove excess solvent while preserving the distribution of the precursor.

4. Calcination  
   - Oxide-supported catalysts are calcined at 500°C for 2 hours in air to convert the precursor into a well-dispersed iron oxide phase.  
   - For carbon-supported catalysts (e.g., CNF), a milder calcination at 355°C for 2 hours under a nitrogen flow is applied to prevent degradation of the support.

5. Additional Post‑treatments  
   - Following calcination, no further treatments are required, although subsequent reduction or passivation may be performed based on performance needs.

### Comparative Analysis

The incipient wetness impregnation method offers advantages such as enhanced Fe nanoparticle dispersion and reproducibility. However, the synthesis complexity increases when dealing with precipitation–deposition steps and tailored drying conditions. While oxide supports benefit from high-temperature calcination in air, carbon supports require milder treatment under nitrogen to preserve structural integrity. This systematic approach allows reproducible synthesis and performance optimization of catalysts for sustainable lower olefin production.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

The following procedure details the step‐by‐step synthesis of supported iron-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts. Each catalyst preparation begins with complete documentation of primary precursors, ensuring reproducibility.

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/α-Al₂O₃

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • 2.7 mL of a 1.4 M ammonium iron citrate aqueous solution  
  • 2 g of α-Al₂O₃ support  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Ambient temperature for precursor dissolution  
  • Vigorous stirring during impregnation  
- **Process**:
  1. Dissolve ammonium iron citrate in water to create a homogeneous 1.4 M solution.
  2. Impregnate the α-Al₂O₃ support by adding the solution dropwise until its pore volume is fully filled.
  3. Stir continuously to ensure uniform distribution.
- **Key Steps**: Confirm complete dissolution and even dispersion of the precursor.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**:  
  • The precursor-impregnated α-Al₂O₃  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Repeat impregnation if necessary to achieve target iron loading (approximately 10 wt%).
- **Process**:
  1. If sequential addition is required, allow a preliminary drying phase between impregnations.
  2. Maintain a uniform mixing to secure homogeneity.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor added volume closely and adjust for precise iron loading.

##### Step [3]: Purification
- **Process**:
  1. Wash the impregnated support with boiling water to remove residual sodium and bring the pH to neutral.
  2. Employ filtration to separate the precipitate, repeating washing as needed.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure pH neutrality through iterative washing.

##### Step [4]: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:
  • Dry under 60 mbar at room temperature for 2 hours.
- **Calcination**:
  • Ramp at 10 K/min to 500°C and hold for 2 hours under air flow.

- **Process**:
  1. Reduce the catalyst in situ by exposing it to a H₂/Ar mixture (33% v/v) at 350°C for 2 hours (60 mL/min flow).
- **Key Steps**: Monitor the reduction process to ensure full conversion of iron oxide to the active phase.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for in situ reduction of the iron precursor, transforming iron oxide (Fe₂O₃) into the active phase required for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is performed at 350 °C during the pre‐reaction treatment, ensuring that the catalyst is efficiently reduced without causing thermal sintering.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 350 °C provides the necessary energy for reduction while minimizing detrimental particle agglomeration.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the reduction kinetics by controlling the gas–catalyst interaction during the in situ treatment.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Two pressure regimes are reported: a low-pressure activation conducted at 1 bar and a medium-pressure activation at 20 bar.  
- **Observations:** The higher pressure (20 bar) appears to enhance reduction kinetics compared to atmospheric conditions, contributing to differences in catalyst performance during subsequent synthesis.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation governs the reduction environment. An appropriate reducing atmosphere is essential for converting the iron oxide into the catalytically active phase.  
- **Experimental Conditions:**  
 • For low-pressure tests, the activation gas consists of a H₂/Ar mixture containing 33% H₂, delivered at a flow rate of 60 mL/min.  
 • For medium-pressure tests, pure H₂ is employed at a flow rate of 22 mL/min.  
- **Observations:** The reducing atmosphere ensures efficient conversion of Fe₂O₃ into active iron carbide, thereby promoting high activity and selectivity toward lower olefins.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step must be controlled to allow comprehensive in situ reduction without overexposure that may lead to excessive sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A 2-hour activation period is applied under both low and medium pressure conditions.  
- **Observations:** This treatment time is sufficient to achieve a balanced reduction, ensuring catalyst stability and optimal performance.

#### 5.1.5 Support-Dependent Activation  
- **Definition:** Adjustments in the activation procedure are made based on the catalyst support to preserve support integrity and control metal dispersion.  
- **Experimental Conditions:**  
 • For CNF-supported catalysts, a treatment at approximately 355 °C under an inert nitrogen atmosphere is used to avoid oxidation.  
 • Oxide supports, such as α-Al₂O₃, are treated at 500 °C under air conditions.  
- **Observations:** Tailoring the activation treatment to the support characteristics ensures well-dispersed active sites and minimizes unwanted secondary interactions, thereby enhancing selective catalytic performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is a critical parameter that influences syngas activation, hydrocarbon chain growth, and overall catalyst performance.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch tests were conducted under two temperature regimes. For experiments at 1 bar pressure, the reactor temperature was maintained at 350 °C, while tests at 20 bar were performed at 340 °C.
- **Observations:** The slightly lower temperature at 20 bar is chosen to optimize product selectivity towards lower olefins and to minimize side reactions. At 1 bar, although 350 °C supports activity, the CO conversions remained low (0.5–1%), which may indicate kinetic limitations or catalyst deactivation trends over time.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure affects the concentration of feed gases and thus the kinetics of the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Two pressure regimes were applied: 1 bar for low-pressure tests and 20 bar for elevated pressure experiments.
- **Observations:** The 20 bar conditions promote better reaction kinetics leading to improved hydrocarbon selectivity, particularly favoring the production of lower olefins over methane. In contrast, the 1 bar experiments serve as a baseline with limited CO conversion, which is useful for comparative catalyst evaluation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The hydrogen-to-carbon monoxide ratio is essential for controlling the reduction of the iron oxide precursor to catalytically active iron carbide and steering the product distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** For experiments performed at 20 bar, a fixed H₂/CO ratio of 1 was maintained.
- **Observations:** This equimolar ratio is selected to balance the syngas reactivity, promoting the formation of lower olefins while minimizing excessive methane production. The controlled ratio ensures reproducibility in hydrocarbon product profiles across different catalyst formulations.

#### 5.2.4 Reactor Setup Considerations
- **Details:** The plug flow reactor was configured with a dilution setup using 20 mg of catalyst blended with 200 mg of SiC (with particle sizes of 0.2–0.4 mm for the catalyst and 0.2 mm for SiC) to maintain isothermal conditions.
- **Observations:** This dilution strategy enhances heat transfer and minimizes temperature gradients, contributing to consistent catalytic performance and effective kinetic evaluations.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the iron-based catalysts were primarily assessed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques elucidate the nature and transformation of iron species, which are critical for Fischer–Tropsch (FT) activity.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis of fresh catalysts revealed that iron is present as Fe₂O₃ (hematite) across all samples. For Fe/SiO₂, Fe/γ-Al₂O₃, and Fe/β-SiC, the absence of characteristic iron oxide diffraction lines indicates either amorphous Fe₂O₃ or crystallites smaller than 4 nm. Crystallite sizes determined by XRD include: Fe/CNF – 7 nm, Fe/α-Al₂O₃ – 21 nm, Fe-Ti-Zn-K (bulk) – 50 nm, Fe-Cu-K-SiO₂ – 40 nm, and Bulk Fe – 30 nm. After reaction, the formation of catalytically active iron carbide (FexCy) is confirmed by Mössbauer spectroscopy, which detects Fe₅C₂ in spent catalysts with moderate to high activity.
- **Observations:** The use of inert or weakly interactive supports (e.g., CNF, α-Al₂O₃) favors the formation of small, well-dispersed Fe₂O₃ particles, which are more readily converted to active carbide phases under FT conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Promoter Distribution
- **Description:** X-ray fluorescence (XRF) was employed to quantify iron loading and the presence of promoters such as sodium and sulfur, which influence catalytic selectivity and activity.
- **Findings:** Iron loadings (wt%) determined by XRF are: Fe/CNF (12), Fe/α-Al₂O₃ (13), Fe/β-SiC (8), Fe/SiO₂ (13), Fe/γ-Al₂O₃ (13), Fe-Ti-Zn-K (72), Fe-Cu-K-SiO₂ (32), and Bulk Fe (63). Promoters introduced via ammonium iron citrate, such as Na and S, are present in measurable quantities (e.g., Fe/α-Al₂O₃ contains 0.594 wt% Na and 0.132 wt% S).
- **Observations:** The homogeneous distribution of Fe and promoters is attributed to the incipient wetness impregnation method, which is essential for achieving uniform catalytic properties.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provided direct visualization of particle size and dispersion, critical for understanding catalyst performance.
- **Findings:** TEM images show that Fe/α-Al₂O₃ exhibits a homogeneous distribution of iron oxide nanoparticles with a size of 14 ± 5 nm (increasing to 17 ± 5 nm after reaction), while Fe/CNF displays highly dispersed particles of 5 ± 1 nm. Bulk promoted catalysts (Fe-Ti-Zn-K) consist of much larger Fe₂O₃ particles (average 400 nm) forming aggregates.
- **Observations:** Supports such as CNF and α-Al₂O₃ enable the formation of small, well-dispersed nanoparticles, which are beneficial for catalyst activation and stability. In contrast, bulk catalysts tend to aggregate, resulting in larger, less active particles.

#### 6.1.4 Iron Phase Evolution and Carburization
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was used to identify iron phases and monitor the transformation from Fe₂O₃ to active iron carbide (Fe₅C₂) during reaction.
- **Findings:** Fresh catalysts display signatures of hematite and superparamagnetic Fe₂O₃, with the latter prevalent in particles smaller than 13.5 nm. After FT reaction, Mössbauer spectra of active catalysts (e.g., Fe/CNF, Fe/α-Al₂O₃) reveal the presence of Fe₅C₂, while low-activity catalysts (e.g., Fe/SiO₂, Fe/γ-Al₂O₃) lack carbide phases.
- **Observations:** The formation of Fe₅C₂ correlates with high FT activity and selectivity for lower olefins, underscoring the importance of support selection and iron dispersion in promoting carburization.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, typically calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet molar flow rates of CO, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under Fischer–Tropsch conditions (1 bar, 350°C, H₂/CO = 1), CO conversions for supported catalysts (Fe/CNF, Fe/α-Al₂O₃) are moderate (0.5–1%) due to intentional low-conversion operation, while at 20 bar and 340°C, conversions increase substantially (up to 77–88% for Fe/CNF and Fe/α-Al₂O₃; 97% for bulk Fe).
- **Observations and Trends:** Inert or weakly interactive supports (CNF, α-Al₂O₃) enable higher CO conversion at elevated pressure, while strongly interactive supports (γ-Al₂O₃, SiO₂) result in lower conversions due to suppressed formation of active iron carbide phases.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** Methane selectivity is defined as the percentage of carbon atoms in CH₄ relative to the total carbon in all hydrocarbons produced:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\text{C atoms in CH}_4}{\text{Total C atoms in hydrocarbons}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 1 bar, 350°C, and H₂/CO = 1:
  - Fe/CNF: 23%
  - Fe/α-Al₂O₃: 22%
  - Fe/β-SiC: 31%
  - Fe/SiO₂: 38%
  - Fe/γ-Al₂O₃: 54%
  - Fe-Ti-Zn-K: 83%
  - Fe-Cu-K-SiO₂: 43%
  - Bulk Fe: 76%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe/CNF and Fe/α-Al₂O₃ exhibit the lowest methane selectivity, favoring olefin production, while bulk and promoted bulk catalysts show significantly higher methane yields.

#### 6.2.3 Lower Olefin (C₂–C₄) Selectivity
- **Description:** Lower olefin selectivity is the fraction of carbon in C₂–C₄ olefins relative to total hydrocarbon carbon:
\[
\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4 \text{ Olefin Selectivity} = \frac{\text{C atoms in C}_2\text{–C}_4 \text{ olefins}}{\text{Total C atoms in hydrocarbons}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 1 bar, 350°C, H₂/CO = 1:
  - Fe/CNF: 61%
  - Fe/α-Al₂O₃: 61%
  - Fe/β-SiC: 58%
  - Fe/SiO₂: 56%
  - Fe/γ-Al₂O₃: 44%
  - Fe-Ti-Zn-K: 16%
  - Fe-Cu-K-SiO₂: 46%
  - Bulk Fe: 21%
- **Observations and Trends:** Weakly interactive supports (CNF, α-Al₂O₃) maximize lower olefin selectivity, while bulk and promoted catalysts are less selective.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of carbon in hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\text{C atoms in C}_5^+ \text{ hydrocarbons}}{\text{Total C atoms in hydrocarbons}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe/CNF at 1 bar, C₅⁺ selectivity is 12%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Supported catalysts favor lower olefins with moderate C₅⁺ production, while bulk catalysts yield fewer long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory model, indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination:
\[
\text{ASF:} \quad W_n = (1-\alpha)\alpha^{n-1}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** Maximum C₂–C₄ selectivity (~50 wt%) is achieved at α ≈ 0.4–0.5.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts with α in this range (e.g., Fe/CNF, Fe/α-Al₂O₃) show optimal lower olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Stability is assessed by monitoring the decline in activity (iron time yield, FTY) over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{FTY}_{\text{initial}} - \text{FTY}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe/CNF shows continuous activity loss over 15 h (e.g., 50% loss after 17 h for similar carbon-supported catalysts), while Fe/α-Al₂O₃ maintains stable activity over the same period.
- **Observations and Trends:** Weakly interactive supports (α-Al₂O₃) enhance catalyst stability, whereas carbon supports (CNF) exhibit higher initial activity but faster deactivation.

#### 6.2.7 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY measures the intrinsic activity as moles of CO converted per gram of Fe per second:
\[
\text{FTY} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted}}{\text{g Fe} \cdot \text{s}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** FTY ranges from 0.07 (Fe/γ-Al₂O₃) to 6.5 (Fe/CNF) × 10⁻² molCO/gFe·s, with Fe/CNF and Fe/α-Al₂O₃ showing the highest values.
- **Observations and Trends:** High FTY correlates with well-dispersed Fe nanoparticles on inert supports, while bulk and strongly interactive supports yield lower FTY.
