## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis methods employed for Iron–Ceria catalysts (15R and 15P), based on a coprecipitation combined with hydrothermal treatment process. Two synthesis protocols are used to control nanoshapes through the concentration of aqueous NaOH, providing a comparative analysis in terms of synthesis complexity, scalability, and cost.

#### Coprecipitation and Hydrothermal Treatment Method

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Reagents: Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O (total concentration 0.4 mol/L) in deionized water.  
   • Conditions: Stir the solution at room temperature for 2 hours.  
   • Observation: Formation of a homogeneous precursor solution.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • Action: Add aqueous NaOH dropwise to the precursor solution.  
   • Specifics: For nanorods (15R), use a higher concentration (6 mol/L); for nanopolyhedra (15P), use a lower concentration (0.2 mol/L).  
   • Conditions: Stir the resulting brown slurry for 30 minutes.
   • Equipment: Mixing is performed using standard laboratory glassware, with pH monitored using a calibrated pH meter.

3. **Hydrothermal Treatment**  
   • Procedure: Seal the mixture in a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave.
   • Conditions: Maintain at 100 °C for 24 hours to complete precipitation–deposition.
   • Observation: Formation of stable precipitates suitable for further processing.

4. **Drying**  
   • Process: Filter and wash the precipitates with deionized water until neutral pH is achieved.
   • Conditions: Dry in vacuum at 60 °C for 24 hours.
   • Equipment: Vacuum drying apparatus ensuring controlled moisture removal.

5. **Calcination**  
   • Parameters: Calcine the dried precursor at 400 °C for 4 hours with a ramping rate of 2 °C/min.
   • Equipment: Furnace capable of precise temperature control.
   • Outcome: Final catalyst structure with subnanometer iron oxide clusters on CeO₂ support is obtained.

This structured sequence, with emphasis on numerical precision and equipment detail, ensures reproducibility and allows for direct comparison between catalysts with differing nanomorphologies.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the reagent handling steps for the preparation of iron–ceria catalysts 15R (nanorods) and 15P (nanopolyhedra). The procedure is organized into three categories—Weighing, Liquid Transfer, and Mixing—to ensure full reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 15R  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed in the appropriate amount using an analytical balance.  
- **Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O**: Weighed in the suitable quantity using an analytical balance.

##### 3.1.1.2 15P  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed in a catalyst-specific proportion using an analytical balance.  
- **Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O**: Weighed with precision as required for catalyst synthesis.

*Note*: Although the exact masses are not explicitly stated, the procedure emphasizes the use of precise weighing to ensure batch-to-batch consistency in both catalyst formulations.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 15R  
- **Deionized Water**: Poured 5 mL into the reaction vessel to dissolve the solid precursors, ensuring complete wetting and dissolution.  
- **Aqueous NaOH**: Transferred 35 mL into a 50 mL Teflon bottle to initiate the coprecipitation step; the concentration is adjusted for formation of nanorod morphology.

##### 3.1.2.2 15P  
- **Deionized Water**: Poured 5 mL into the mixing container for precursor dissolution.  
- **Aqueous NaOH**: Added 35 mL into the designated Teflon vessel to promote precipitation, with concentration tailored for nanopolyhedron formation.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 15R  
- **Precursor Suspension**: Mixed by stirring the solution for 2 hours at room temperature within the Teflon-lined bottle, ensuring a homogeneous dispersion of the Fe and Ce species.

##### 3.1.3.2 15P  
- **Precursor Suspension**: Mixed thoroughly by stirring for 2 hours at room temperature in the Teflon container, maintaining uniformity for reproducible catalyst formation.

These detailed steps—organized by catalyst type and reagent handling category—link the initial preparation to subsequent hydrothermal treatment, ensuring that the protocol is executed consistently across different Fe doping levels and support morphologies.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Iron–Ceria Catalyst (15R/15P)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Ce(NO₃)₃·6H₂O in 5 mL of deionized water to obtain a total concentration of 0.4 mol/L. Fe/Ce molar ratios are adjusted to 5/95, 15/85, or 25/75.
- **Conditions**: Stir at room temperature for 2 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the metal nitrates thoroughly ensuring a homogeneous solution.
  - Confirm complete dissolution over the 2-hour period with consistent stirring.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure precise reactant ratios and complete solubilization of precursors.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: Precursor solution mixed with 35 mL aqueous NaOH.
- **Conditions**: Dropwise addition of NaOH at room temperature; use 6 mol/L NaOH for nanorod formation (15R) and 0.2 mol/L for nanopolyhedron formation (15P).
- **Process**:
  - Add NaOH slowly while stirring continuously for an additional 30 minutes to initiate coprecipitation.
  - Monitor pH to guide formation toward the desired nanoshape.
- **Key Steps**: Controlled dropwise addition and stirring to ensure appropriate morphology.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Immediately transfer the resulting brown slurry to a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave.
  - After hydrothermal treatment (100 °C for 24 h), filter the precipitate.
  - Wash repeatedly with deionized water until a neutral pH is reached.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain neutral pH to avoid residual ions.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Vacuum dry at 60 °C for 24 hours.
- **Calcination**: Heat at 400 °C for 4 hours with a ramping rate of 2 °C/min.
- **Process**:
  - Drying removes moisture without inducing particle agglomeration.
  - Calcination enhances crystallinity and stability.
- **Key Steps**: Strict control over temperature and duration to preserve nanostructure integrity.

##### Step 5: Activation
- **Process**:
  - Pre-treat the catalyst at 350 °C in air for 30 minutes.
  - Stabilize in pure N₂ at room temperature for 30 minutes.
  - Switch to a 10% H₂/Ar atmosphere and heat from room temperature to 900 °C at 5 °C/min.
- **Key Steps**: Controlled reduction is critical for generating active subnanometer iron oxide clusters.

This detailed protocol, incorporating precise reagent quantities, mixing conditions, and thermal treatment parameters, ensures repeatability and alignment with reported experimental reproducibility for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst synthesis.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD analysis was performed to determine the crystalline structure of the iron–ceria catalysts and monitor lattice variations due to doping and exsolution phenomena.
- **Instruments and Models**: Rigaku D/MAX 2550/PC Diffractometer with Ni‑filtered Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Operating at 40 kV and 40 mA; samples were heated from room temperature to 800 °C in 100 °C increments under a controlled 5% H₂/Ar atmosphere.
- **Test Parameters**: Approximately 100 mg of catalyst loaded in a ceramic holder (10 mm diameter, 1 mm depth); step size of 0.02° with two 20‑minute scans at each temperature increment (the second scan used for analysis).
- **Key Notes**: Care was taken to avoid re‑oxidation by rapid transfer of used samples in a N₂ atmosphere before analysis.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR was employed to evaluate the reducibility of the iron species within the catalyst, providing insights into Fe–O–Ce interactions.
- **Instruments and Models**: Builder PCSA‑1000 instrument equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Fresh catalysts were pretreated at 350 °C in air for 30 minutes, cooled and stabilized in pure N₂ for approximately 30 minutes.
- **Test Parameters**: Approximately 30 mg of catalyst was subjected to a flow of 10% H₂ in Ar; temperature ramped from room temperature to 900 °C at 5 °C/min.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and precise temperature control are critical for accurately capturing reduction profiles.

### 4.3 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & Aberration‑corrected STEM‑EELS
- **Operational Description**: TEM was used to directly observe ultrafine iron oxide clusters (<1 nm) and assess their spatial distribution on ceria supports.
- **Instruments and Models**: Philips Tecnai F20 TEM operating at 200 kV; JEOL ARM200F for aberration‑corrected STEM‑EELS.
- **Characterization Conditions**: High-resolution imaging under high acceleration voltage to ensure clarity of atomic-scale features.
- **Test Parameters**: Standard sample preparation via dispersion and deposition on TEM grids.
- **Key Notes**: Combined TEM/STEM-EELS analyses provide complementary crystallinity and elemental mapping information.

### 4.4 X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: XPS was conducted to evaluate surface chemical states by monitoring Ce 3d and C 1s peaks.
- **Instruments and Models**: Axis Ultra XPS spectrometer (Kratos, U.K.) using Al Kα radiation (225 W).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Calibration performed using the C 1s line at 284.8 eV.
- **Test Parameters**: Detailed spectral fitting of Ce 3d peaks to quantify the Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺ ratio.
- **Key Notes**: Overlap of the Fe 2p peak with the Ce MNN region limits detailed analysis of the iron chemical state.

### 4.5 X‑ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS)
- **Operational Description**: XAFS (including XANES and EXAFS) provided insights into the local coordination environment and oxidation state of iron in the catalysts.
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements were conducted at the BL14W1 beamline (SSRF) or at the 20‑ID‑B beamline (Advanced Photon Source).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Data collected in fluorescence mode with calibration using pure Fe foil.
- **Test Parameters**: Data analysis performed using Athena and Artemis for background subtraction, normalization, and fitting.
- **Key Notes**: XAFS effectively distinguishes between partially reduced and fully oxidized iron species, complementing observations from other techniques.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation procedure is designed to prepare the subnanometer iron oxide clusters on the CeO₂ support for optimal Fischer–Tropsch activity. This critical pre‐reaction treatment employs controlled reduction conditions to convert fully oxidized iron species into partially reduced active forms.

#### Activation Temperature
• Definition: The activation temperature is pivotal in driving the reduction of FeOx species. A properly chosen temperature ensures that active sites are exposed without causing undesirable phase transformations or sintering.  
• Experimental Conditions: The catalyst undergoes H₂ pretreatment at 350 °C.  
• Observations: At 350 °C, the reduction is sufficient to partially convert Fe³⁺ species, evident in the formation of some Fe–Fe bonds in the nanorod variant while maintaining a controlled degree of reduction.

#### Activation Pressure
• Definition: Pressure during activation affects the kinetics of the gas–catalyst interactions and ensures that the reducing atmosphere is maintained uniformly across the catalyst bed.  
• Experimental Conditions: The activation is performed under ambient pressure conditions.  
• Observations: Mild (atmospheric) pressure allows for a gradual and controlled reduction, thereby avoiding the overly aggressive conditions that might occur under elevated pressures.

#### Activation Gas
• Definition: The reducing gas mixture facilitates the removal of oxygen from the catalyst surface and promotes the formation of partially reduced iron sites.  
• Experimental Conditions: A 10% H₂ in Ar mixture is used during the pretreatment.  
• Observations: The diluted hydrogen atmosphere is key to achieving partial reduction. It prevents over‐reduction or excessive carburization, thereby preserving the ultrafine dispersion of the active iron clusters, particularly in the nanorod catalyst (15R) compared to the nanopolyhedra (15P).

#### Activation Time
• Definition: The duration of the activation step ensures that sufficient exposure to the reducing atmosphere occurs, permitting uniform transformation of the catalyst surface without inducing sintering or agglomeration.  
• Experimental Conditions: The activation step is maintained for 3 hours.  
• Observations: A 3-hour pretreatment provides an optimal balance between reduction efficiency and catalyst stability, setting the stage for the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction where performance metrics such as CO conversion and product selectivity are markedly enhanced.

This systematic approach to activation, with careful attention to temperature, pressure, gas composition, and time, establishes the desired surface properties that are crucial for high catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reactor temperature is a key factor in activating syngas and driving the kinetics of the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, directly influencing the reduction of iron species and the overall catalytic performance.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Catalytic tests were performed at two distinct reaction temperatures: 230 °C and 250 °C.  
- **Observations:** At 230 °C, the 15R catalyst exhibited a CO conversion of 22% while maintaining good stability. At 250 °C, the conversion increased to 31%, indicating enhanced kinetics; however, the rapid drop in conversion from 31% to about 21% within the first 10 hours suggests the onset of carbon deposition on the 15R catalyst. In contrast, the 15P catalyst consistently showed lower conversion values (6.6% at 230 °C and 8.4% at 250 °C) with minimal signs of deactivation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure governs the partial pressures of the reactant gases, affecting syngas concentration and thereby product selectivity and conversion rates.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** All reactions were conducted under a fixed pressure of 2.0 MPa in a fixed-bed flow reactor.  
- **Observations:** The defined pressure ensured sufficient syngas concentration, contributing to optimal catalyst performance. While the pressure was held constant, its role in maintaining stable reaction conditions was critical in achieving reproducible results.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide influences the hydrocarbon product distribution, affecting the selectivity between methane and heavier hydrocarbons.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A stoichiometric H₂/CO ratio of 2:1 was maintained during all catalytic tests, corresponding to a gas mixture containing 32% CO, 64% H₂, and 4% N₂.  
- **Observations:** This fixed ratio promoted a favorable balance in hydrocarbon formation, aligning with the catalyst’s tendency to yield higher C₅⁺ selectivity on the 15R catalyst.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space–time velocity, expressed as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), indicates the flow rate of reactants relative to the catalyst mass, determining the residence time for the reaction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was set at 720 cm³·h⁻¹·gcat⁻¹.  
- **Observations:** This parameter ensured adequate exposure of reactants to the active sites, facilitating reproducible conversion rates and product distributions across both catalysts.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of the iron–ceria catalysts were determined by ex situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku D/MAX 2550/PC Diffractometer with Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation. XRD analysis is essential for identifying the fluorite-type CeO₂ support, monitoring Fe incorporation, and detecting any secondary phases.
- **Findings:** All fresh samples (CeO₂–R, CeO₂–P, 5R, 5P, 15R, 15P, 25R, 25P) exhibited diffraction peaks corresponding to the fcc fluorite-type CeO₂ structure, with main reflections at the (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) planes. No additional peaks were observed except for minor α-Fe₂O₃ at the highest Fe loading. Lattice constant analysis revealed a decrease upon Fe³⁺ doping (due to the smaller ionic radius of Fe³⁺ compared to Ce⁴⁺), while post-reaction samples showed lattice constants returning to or exceeding those of pure CeO₂, indicating Fe migration from the lattice to the surface.
- **Observations:** The reversible change in lattice constants confirms Fe³⁺ exsolution during reaction, resulting in surface-dispersed iron oxide clusters, which are critical for catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Reducibility and Metal–Support Interactions
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was performed using a Builder PCSA-1000 instrument to probe the reducibility of iron species and Fe–O–Ce interactions. The position and number of reduction peaks provide insight into the nature of iron oxide clusters and their interaction with ceria.
- **Findings:** For 15R, five reduction peaks (A–E) were observed; 15P exhibited four. Peak A, starting at ~220 °C, is attributed to the reduction of oxygen at the Fe–O–Ce interface, occurring at much lower temperatures than for pure CeO₂ or α-Fe₂O₃. Peak B corresponds to the reduction of ultrafine FeOx clusters, while Peak C (around 500 °C) is assigned to surface CeO₂ reduction (present in 15R, absent in 15P). Peaks D and E, at higher temperatures, relate to bulk Fe₃O₄ and CeO₂ reduction, respectively.
- **Observations:** The lower temperature of Peak A in 15P indicates stronger Fe–O–Ce interactions, which correlate with less favorable catalytic performance compared to 15R.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Iron Oxide Cluster Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and aberration-corrected scanning TEM with electron energy loss spectroscopy (STEM-EELS) were used to examine catalyst morphology and the dispersion of iron oxide clusters.
- **Findings:** Both 15R and 15P samples displayed highly crystalline ceria nanorods and nanopolyhedra, respectively, with clear lattice fringes. STEM-EELS mapping revealed uniformly distributed Fe-rich microdomains with sizes ranging from 0.5 to 1.2 nm on the ceria surface.
- **Observations:** The subnanometer size and uniform dispersion of iron oxide clusters maximize the number of active sites and are directly linked to enhanced Fischer–Tropsch performance, especially in 15R.

#### 6.1.4 Iron Oxidation State and Local Coordination
- **Description:** X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS), including XANES and EXAFS, was employed to determine the oxidation state and local environment of Fe species.
- **Findings:** In 15R, XANES spectra after H₂ pretreatment and FTS revealed partially reduced Fe^δ+ species (δ ≈ 2.6–2.9), similar to Fe₃O₄, while 15P retained features of fully oxidized Fe³⁺. EXAFS analysis showed Fe–Fe metallic bonds in 15R after H₂ pretreatment, absent in 15P, which maintained Fe–O–Ce coordination.
- **Observations:** The presence of partially reduced Fe^δ+ and Fe–Fe bonds in 15R is associated with higher catalytic activity, whereas the persistence of Fe³⁺ in 15P correlates with lower activity.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Electronic Structure and Carbonate Formation
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted using an Axis Ultra XPS spectrometer to analyze Ce 3d and C 1s peaks, providing information on Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺ ratios and surface carbonate species.
- **Findings:** Both 15R and 15P exhibited increased Ce³⁺/Ce⁴⁺ ratios after FTS, indicating partial surface reduction. The C 1s spectra showed a peak at 288.9 eV, attributed to surface carbonates formed by CO₂ adsorption.
- **Observations:** Higher Ce³⁺ content suggests more oxygen vacancies, which facilitate reactant activation and may enhance catalytic performance.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2:1, GHSV = 720 cm³·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹), the 15R catalyst (ceria nanorods) achieved CO conversions of 22% at 230 °C and 31% at 250 °C. In contrast, the 15P catalyst (ceria nanopolyhedra) exhibited lower conversions of 6.6% at 230 °C and 8.4% at 250 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increases with temperature for both catalysts. The nanorod-supported catalyst (15R) consistently outperforms the nanopolyhedral variant (15P), highlighting the critical role of support morphology and Fe–O–Ce interactions in catalytic activity.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The 15R catalyst exhibited CH₄ selectivity of approximately 19–21%, while 15P showed higher CH₄ selectivity, particularly at lower CO conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** The lower CH₄ selectivity for 15R indicates a preference for longer-chain hydrocarbon formation, whereas 15P favors methane production, correlating with its lower overall activity.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of chain growth. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 230 °C, 15R achieved a C₅⁺ selectivity of 40%, while 15P reached only 29%. When CO conversion was fixed at ~10%, 15R’s C₅⁺ selectivity increased to 53%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanorod-supported catalyst (15R) demonstrates superior selectivity for higher hydrocarbons, underscoring the influence of Fe–O–Fe cluster structure on product distribution.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
  - α values range from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (maximum chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** 15R exhibited an α value of 0.64, while 15P showed a lower value of 0.57.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher α for 15R correlates with its enhanced C₅⁺ selectivity, indicating a greater tendency for long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time (h)}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For 15R, CO conversion dropped from 31% to 21% within the first 10 h (deactivation rate ≈ 1%/h), then stabilized at ~20% over the subsequent 38 h. 15P maintained a steady, low conversion (~10%) throughout 48 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The initial deactivation of 15R is attributed to carbon deposition at high activity, but the catalyst exhibits good long-term stability, with the active Fe–O–Fe clusters remaining structurally intact.

---
**Summary:**  
The iron–ceria nanorod catalyst (15R) demonstrates markedly higher CO conversion, lower CH₄ selectivity, greater C₅⁺ selectivity, and a higher α factor compared to the nanopolyhedral variant (15P). These results confirm that subnanometer Fe–O–Fe clusters on ceria nanorods are highly effective for FTS, promoting both activity and selectivity toward desirable long-chain hydrocarbons, with robust stability under reaction conditions.
