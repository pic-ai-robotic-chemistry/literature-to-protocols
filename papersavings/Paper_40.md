## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of CNF‐Supported Iron-Based Fischer–Tropsch Catalysts employs an incipient wetness impregnation approach, incorporating both unpromoted and promoted routes. The process follows a sequential protocol designed for reproducibility:

#### • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
1. Prepare a stock solution by dissolving 7.014 g of ammonium iron citrate in 25 mL of demineralized water.  
2. For promoted catalysts, sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate and iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate are dissolved alongside ammonium iron citrate to achieve the desired promoter incorporation.  
3. During mixing, a homogeneous solution is ensured before impregnation onto the CNF support.

#### • Precipitation–Deposition  
1. The precursor solution is applied dropwise to the carbon nanofibers under stirring, facilitating a precipitation–deposition phase that promotes uniform deposition.  
2. Homogeneous distribution is achieved by performing successive impregnation steps, which inherently integrate the precipitation–deposition step prior to drying.

#### • Drying  
1. After the first impregnation, the material is dried in a static air oven at 120 °C for 1 hour.  
2. A final drying step of 2 hours at 120 °C is applied after the last impregnation, ensuring complete removal of the solvent.

#### • Calcination / Heat Treatment  
1. The dried material undergoes heat treatment in a nitrogen atmosphere at 500 °C for 2 hours with a controlled heating rate of 5 °C/min.  
2. This step converts the impregnated iron species into active iron nanoparticles with particle sizes tailored (3, 4, 7, or 9 nm) by varying the iron loading.

#### • Post‑Treatment (Passivation)  
1. Following calcination, a controlled passivation process is executed by gradually increasing the oxygen concentration (stepwise increments of 2% v/v every 30 minutes until reaching 20% v/v) to oxidize the catalyst surface delicately. 

This synthesis method, chosen for its simplicity and scalability, ensures precise conditions—using equipment such as drying ovens and nitrogen-flow furnaces—that allow other researchers to replicate the procedure with exact temperature, time, and concentration parameters as reported.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: CNF‐Supported Iron‐Based Fischer–Tropsch Catalysts – Unpromoted (e.g., 3Fe)
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 7.014 g ammonium iron citrate dissolved in 25 mL demineralized water.
- **Conditions**: Stir at room temperature until complete dissolution.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve ammonium iron citrate in demineralized water to form a homogeneous stock solution.
  - Ensure the CNF support (purified via reflux in 1 M KOH and 65% HNO₃, washed to pH 7) is ready prior to impregnation.
- **Key Steps**: Confirm complete dissolution and support purity to promote uniform loading.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Impregnation
- **Reagents**: CNF support; precursor stock solution.
- **Conditions**: Incipient wetness impregnation.
- **Process**:
  - Impregnate the CNF with the prepared stock solution to achieve the target iron loading (e.g., 3Fe).
  - Dry the impregnated support under static air at 120 °C for 1 hour between successive impregnation cycles.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain uniform distribution of precursors to control iron nanoparticle sizes (approximately 3–9 nm).

##### Step 3: Drying, Calcination, and Passivation
- **Drying & Calcination Conditions**: Dry under static air at 120 °C for 2 hours after the final impregnation; calcine at 500 °C for 2 hours under nitrogen (ramp rate 5 °C/min).
- **Process**:
  - After thermal treatment, perform controlled passivation by gradually introducing oxygen (increase by 2% v/v every 30 minutes until reaching 20% v/v).
- **Key Steps**: Ensure slow oxidation to minimize sintering and preserve catalyst structure.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Process**:
  - Activate the catalyst by reduction under a H₂/N₂ mixture at 700 °C (2.8 bar) for 2 hours.
  - Monitor reduction and formation of Fe carbide species using in situ Mössbauer spectroscopy.
- **Key Steps**: Precisely control temperature and gas flow to obtain the active phase.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: CNF‐Supported Iron‐Based Fischer–Tropsch Catalysts – Promoted (e.g., 3FeP)
- **Precursor Preparation**: Prepare a similar stock solution including additional sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate and iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate along with ammonium iron citrate.
- **Assembly & Subsequent Steps**: Follow the same impregnation, drying, calcination, passivation, and activation protocols as for the unpromoted catalysts.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure homogeneous incorporation of promoters to enhance initial activity and modify product selectivity while monitoring iron particle size and catalyst stability throughout the process.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) with HAADF  
• **Operational Description**: TEM was employed to determine the nanoparticle size distribution and spatial placement of iron on the CNF support. High-resolution imaging, combined with EDS mapping and HAADF detection, confirmed the elemental composition and core–shell structure of the catalysts.  
• **Instruments and Models**: Philips Tecnai-20 FEG microscope (operating at 200 kV) with integrated EDS and HAADF detectors.  
• **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were prepared ex situ; catalysts were dried at 120 °C, heat-treated at 500 °C for 2 hours under nitrogen, and passivated via controlled oxidation prior to mounting on carbon grids.  
• **Test Parameters**: Imaging involved measuring at least 300 nanoparticles per catalyst, ensuring resolution adequate for nanometer-scale features.  
• **Key Notes**: Proper dispersion on grids and careful alignment are critical to minimize beam damage and to ensure accurate size distribution analysis.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)  
• **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR was utilized to assess the reduction behavior of the catalysts by monitoring hydrogen consumption as temperature increased.  
• **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics AutoChem II reactor system.  
• **Characterization Conditions**: The test was conducted in a flowing mixture of 5% H₂ in Ar at 100 mL/min. The catalyst underwent pre-treatment by reduction under a H₂/N₂ (at 2.8 bar) atmosphere at 700 °C for 2 hours, then a controlled ramp of 5 °C/min was applied from room temperature up to 350 °C.  
• **Test Parameters**: Careful temperature control and constant gas flow ensure reproducibility and accurate depiction of reduction profiles.  
• **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and precise pre-treatment are vital to minimize errors in reduction behavior analysis.

### 4.3 In‑situ ⁵⁷Fe Mössbauer Spectroscopy  
• **Operational Description**: This method monitored phase transformations during reaction conditions, notably tracking the formation of Fe carbide species and the presence of Fe²⁺ under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.  
• **Instruments and Models**: High-pressure Mössbauer in situ cell, calibrated using an α‑Fe foil.  
• **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed at 300 K and 4.2 K, with a total flow rate of 100 mL/min (equivalent to a GHSV of 12,000–24,000 h⁻¹) in a tubular reactor.  
• **Test Parameters**: Although specific scan settings are not detailed, strict control of temperature and gas flow ensured consistency in tracking phase evolution.  
• **Key Notes**: In situ characterization is essential for real‑time monitoring of active phase changes during catalytic operation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for the CNF‐Supported Iron-Based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is performed in two main steps: a heat treatment under an inert atmosphere followed by an in situ reduction. Both steps are carefully controlled to ensure the proper formation of active Fe carbide species essential for the Fischer–Tropsch process.

#### 5.1.1 Heat Treatment Temperature and Duration
• Definition: The heat treatment step is critical for decomposing the impregnated precursors, decomposing residual solvents, and forming the initial iron oxide phases while maintaining the structural integrity of the carbon support.  
• Experimental Conditions: The catalysts are subjected to a heat treatment at 500 °C for 2 hours under a continuous nitrogen flow.  
• Observations: This temperature and duration are optimized to prepare the catalyst with minimal sintering. Unpromoted catalysts, in particular, display limited sintering under these conditions.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas During Heat Treatment
• Definition: An inert gas atmosphere is necessary during heat treatment to prevent unwanted oxidation and maintain uniform precursor decomposition.  
• Experimental Conditions: Nitrogen is used to create an inert environment throughout the 500 °C heat treatment step.  
• Observations: The nitrogen flow effectively preserves the surface properties of both the carbon support and iron species.

#### 5.1.3 In Situ Reduction Parameters
• Definition: The in situ reduction step activates the catalyst by reducing iron oxide nanoparticles and initiating carburization to form active Fe carbide phases.  
• Experimental Conditions: Reduction is carried out at 340 °C with a temperature ramp of 5 °C/min, under a total pressure of 3 bar using a He/H₂ gas mixture (ratio 2:1) for 2 hours.  
• Observations: This controlled reduction ensures the formation of an active catalytic phase while balancing metal dispersion and limiting particle sintering.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity and Pressure
• Definition: Controlled gas flow and pressure during in situ reduction govern the reaction kinetics and gas–catalyst interaction.  
• Experimental Conditions: A gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 7200 h⁻¹ is maintained during the reduction phase, with an operating pressure of 3 bar.  
• Observations: These conditions guarantee complete activation, ensuring reproducible formation of active sites for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis while mitigating excessive sintering.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation was carried out under carefully controlled conditions to ensure reproducibility and relevance to industrial processes. The reaction parameters were selected to optimize syngas conversion while balancing catalyst activation and deactivation dynamics.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for activating the synthesis gas and facilitating the transformation of iron oxide species into active iron carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction temperature was maintained at 340 °C throughout the catalytic tests.
- **Observations:** Operating at 340 °C enables sufficient activation of the syngas while promoting the formation of the active phase. However, this elevated temperature also requires careful monitoring, especially for promoted catalysts that experienced more pronounced sintering and carbon deposition over extended reaction times.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure governs the concentration of syngas molecules in the reactor, thereby influencing hydrocarbon chain growth and product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The high throughput fixed-bed reactor was operated at a constant pressure of 20 bar.
- **Observations:** Maintaining a pressure of 20 bar enhances reaction kinetics and improves the conversion efficiency of synthesis gas into desired hydrocarbon products. This setting was found to favor the production of longer-chain hydrocarbons, although it also has implications for catalyst stability.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio in the feed gas is essential for determining the product distribution, impacting the selective formation of methane versus olefins.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The synthesis gas composition was H₂/CO/He = 45/45/10, yielding an H₂/CO ratio of 1.
- **Observations:** This balanced feed supports a diverse hydrocarbon spectrum. Variations in this ratio are known to shift product selectivity, a phenomenon observed in comparing promoted and unpromoted catalyst performances.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Represented by the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), this parameter indicates the volumetric flow rate of syngas per unit catalyst mass.
- **Experimental Conditions:** GHSV values were tested within a range of 7,200 to 54,000 h⁻¹.
- **Observations:** Lower GHSV levels tend to favor increased CO conversion, while higher GHSV values help minimize carbon deposition, ensuring catalyst stability under conditions simulating industrial operations.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** The morphology and particle size distribution of the CNF-supported iron catalysts were determined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with a Philips Tecnai-20 FEG microscope operated at 200 kV, equipped with EDX and HAADF detectors. At least 300 iron nanoparticles per catalyst were measured to obtain statistically significant average particle sizes.
- **Findings:** Both unpromoted (3Fe, 4Fe, 7Fe, 9Fe) and promoted (3FeP, 4FeP, 7FeP, 9FeP) catalysts exhibited well-dispersed iron nanoparticles on the carbon nanofiber (CNF) support, with average particle sizes closely matching the nominal designations (approximately 3, 4, 7, or 9 nm). After Fischer–Tropsch operation, promoted catalysts showed significant sintering, with Fe particles growing up to ~100 nm and developing a core–shell structure (Fe-rich core, Fe oxide shell), as confirmed by TEM-EDX mapping. In contrast, unpromoted catalysts maintained smaller particle sizes and exhibited limited sintering.
- **Observations:** The use of HAADF imaging provided clear Z-contrast, enabling precise visualization of Fe nanoparticle dispersion. The pronounced sintering and core–shell formation in promoted catalysts correlated with their deactivation, while the stability of unpromoted catalysts was attributed to their resistance to particle growth.

#### 6.1.2 Phase Composition and Transformation
- **Description:** In situ ^57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to monitor iron phase transformations during reduction and under Fischer–Tropsch conditions. Spectra were collected at 300 K and 4.2 K, with velocity calibration using an α-Fe foil.
- **Findings:** After reduction (350 °C, 2 bar, Ar/H₂), both promoted and unpromoted catalysts contained ~25 mol % Fe carbide species. Under Fischer–Tropsch conditions (340 °C, 20 bar, H₂/CO = 1, 20 h), promoted catalysts (e.g., 4FeP) were fully carburized, while unpromoted catalysts (e.g., 4Fe) retained only 23 mol % Fe carbide, with the remainder as Fe²⁺ species (similar to Wüstite, Fe₁₋ₓO). Mössbauer fitting identified Hagg carbide (χ-Fe₅C₂), ε′ carbide, and Fe²⁺ sites.
- **Observations:** Promoters (Na, S) accelerated Fe carbidization, resulting in higher initial activity but also increased sintering. The gradual phase transformation in unpromoted catalysts contributed to their stable performance over extended time-on-stream.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES) was used to determine the elemental composition and iron loading of the catalysts.
- **Findings:** The iron content corresponded to the targeted loadings for each catalyst series, confirming successful impregnation. Promoted catalysts contained additional sodium and sulfur, introduced via sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate and iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate, respectively.
- **Observations:** The presence of promoters was linked to enhanced Fe carbidization and altered sintering behavior, as observed in other characterization results.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was performed using a Micromeritics AutoChem II with a TCD detector to assess the reducibility of iron species.
- **Findings:** Both promoted and unpromoted catalysts exhibited similar TPR profiles under reduction conditions (350 °C, 5% H₂ in Ar, 2 h), indicating that promoter addition did not significantly affect the reduction step.
- **Observations:** The comparable reducibility suggests that differences in catalytic performance and phase transformation are primarily due to promoter effects during reaction, rather than during initial reduction.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Deposition
- **Description:** Carbon laydown during Fischer–Tropsch operation was quantified using a tapered element oscillating microbalance (TEOM), which monitored catalyst mass changes in real time.
- **Findings:** Promoted catalysts exhibited significant carbon accumulation within the first hours of reaction, while unpromoted catalysts showed lower rates of carbon deposition. The CNF support alone did not contribute to mass changes, confirming that carbon laydown occurred on the iron nanoparticles.
- **Observations:** The increased carbon deposition in promoted catalysts is associated with their higher initial activity and rapid deactivation, whereas the lower carbon laydown in unpromoted catalysts supports their long-term stability.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \left[1 - \left(\frac{C_{\text{CO,R}}}{C_{\text{He,R}}} \times \frac{C_{\text{He,blk}}}{C_{\text{CO,blk}}}\right)\right] \times 100
  \]
  where \(C_{\text{CO,R}}\) and \(C_{\text{He,R}}\) are the concentrations of CO and He at the reactor outlet, and \(C_{\text{CO,blk}}\) and \(C_{\text{He,blk}}\) are the corresponding concentrations from a blank reactor.
- **Experimental Data:** Under 340 °C, 20 bar, and H₂/CO/He = 45/45/10, unpromoted catalysts (3Fe, 4Fe, 7Fe, 9Fe) exhibited CO conversions of 3–18% (e.g., 3Fe: 4%, 4Fe: 3%, 7Fe: 4–13%, 9Fe: 4–18%). Promoted catalysts (3FeP, 4FeP, 7FeP, 9FeP) achieved higher or comparable conversions, ranging from 2–8% (e.g., 3FeP: 2–7%, 4FeP: 4%, 7FeP: 7%, 9FeP: 8%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoted catalysts consistently showed higher initial CO conversion, particularly at larger Fe particle sizes, attributed to rapid Fe carbidization. Unpromoted catalysts displayed gradual activity increase over time.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{Moles of CO}_2 \text{ produced}}{\text{Moles of CO converted}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Unpromoted catalysts exhibited CO₂ selectivities of 12–23%. Promoted catalysts showed higher values, ranging from 33–41%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Na and S promoters increased CO₂ selectivity, indicating enhanced water–gas shift activity alongside Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, determined on a carbon atom basis:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{Moles of C in CH}_4}{\text{Moles of CO converted (excluding CO}_2)} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Unpromoted catalysts showed high CH₄ selectivity (46–52%; e.g., 3Fe: 52%, 4Fe: 50%, 7Fe: 47%, 9Fe: 46%). Promoted catalysts exhibited much lower values (8–20%; e.g., 3FeP: 20%, 4FeP: 11%, 7FeP: 8%, 9FeP: 8%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition significantly suppressed methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–C₄ Olefin and C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity to C₂–C₄ olefins and C₅⁺ hydrocarbons is calculated as the percentage of carbon in these products relative to total converted CO (excluding CO₂).
- **Experimental Data:** Promoted catalysts (e.g., 9FeP) achieved C₂–C₄ olefin selectivities up to 60–66% and C₅⁺ selectivities of 27%. Unpromoted catalysts showed lower C₂–C₄ olefin selectivity (25–36%) and C₅⁺ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Larger Fe particle sizes and promoter presence enhanced selectivity toward valuable olefins and heavier hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth versus termination, typically derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution of hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not reported, product distributions indicate that promoted catalysts, with higher C₂–C₄ olefin and lower CH₄ selectivity, correspond to lower α values, favoring light olefins. Unpromoted catalysts, with higher CH₄ selectivity, are associated with higher α values.
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition and increased Fe particle size shift product distribution toward lower α, enhancing olefin yields.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion (or FTY) over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Total Time on Stream}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Promoted catalysts exhibited rapid deactivation, with significant activity loss over 100 h, primarily due to Fe particle sintering. Unpromoted catalysts showed minimal deactivation and stable performance, with limited sintering and negligible carbon deposition.
- **Observations and Trends:** The main cause of deactivation in promoted catalysts was Fe particle growth, while unpromoted catalysts maintained stability due to restricted sintering and gradual phase transformation.
