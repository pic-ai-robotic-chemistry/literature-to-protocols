## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe–Zn–K catalysts involves a multistep procedure that combines a microwave‐aided hydrothermal method with incipient wetness impregnation to introduce potassium. The following detailed procedure is extracted directly from the article and organized for reproducibility:

#### • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation
1. Dissolve measured amounts of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O in deionized water according to the desired Fe/Zn molar ratios (3:1, 1:1, 1:3, 1:5). The combined metal precursor concentration is maintained with a (Fe + Zn)/urea molar ratio of 1:2.
2. Add urea as the precipitator to the solution, ensuring a gradual hydrolysis which maintains the pH at approximately 11 as monitored throughout the mixing.  
   
#### • Precipitation–Deposition
1. After sufficient mixing, a slight deposition step is integrated where a support precursor (if required) is added dropwise. Continuous stirring for 1 hour ensures a homogeneous dispersion before proceeding.

#### • Drying and Calcination
1. The precipitate is dried at 120 °C overnight.
2. Calcination is then performed in air at 450 °C for 4 hours to develop the oxide phases, including the formation of ZnFe₂O₄ spinel and ZnO phases which are responsible for improved dispersion and catalytic behavior.

#### • Potassium Impregnation and Post‑treatment
1. An aqueous potassium carbonate solution is introduced via incipient wetness impregnation, maintaining an Fe/K molar ratio of 10:1. This dropwise addition ensures a uniform distribution of the promoter.
2. The impregnated catalyst is then dried at 120 °C for 8 hours.
3. The final step involves pelletizing the dried catalyst under 20 MPa and sieving to achieve a uniform particle size of 20–40 mesh, thus ensuring reproducibility in catalyst performance.

The procedure leverages a 100 mL Teflon‑lined stainless steel autoclave operating at a microwave frequency of 2450 MHz, 400 W, and 1.6 MPa for 90 minutes during the hydrothermal phase. This method not only facilitates uniform heating and improved particle dispersion but also provides advantages in synthesis complexity and scalability when compared with traditional methods.

### 3.2 Detailed Synthesis Process

#### Catalyst Preparation 1: Fe–Zn–K Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Dissolve appropriate amounts of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O in deionized water along with urea (maintaining a (Fe+Zn):urea molar ratio of 1:2). The Fe/Zn molar ratios vary (e.g., 3:1, 1:1, 1:3, 1:5) to tailor catalyst performance.
- **Conditions**: Maintain the mixture’s pH at ~11 via gradual urea hydrolysis. Use a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave in a microwave-aided hydrothermal process at a frequency of 2450 MHz, power of 400 W, and pressure of 1.6 MPa for 90 minutes.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the metal nitrates fully in deionized water.
  - Add urea and stir to achieve a homogeneous mixture.
  - Transfer the mixture into the autoclave and subject it to microwave heating.
  - Monitor the pH, ensuring it remains close to 11 during precipitation.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and uniform precipitation to avoid formation of unreducible ZnFe₂O₄ at higher Zn loads.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: Following precursor formation, an aqueous solution of potassium carbonate is used for impregnation, targeting an Fe/K molar ratio of 10:1.
- **Conditions & Process**:
  - Impregnate the dried precursor powder uniformly.
  - Dry the impregnated sample at 120 °C for 8 hours.
  
##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Filter the product and wash thoroughly with deionized water to remove residual salts.
  - Use standard centrifugation and filtration techniques; details of speed and time are controlled as per lab protocols.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the washed sample at 120 °C overnight using a laboratory oven.
- **Calcination**: Calcine the dried precursor in air at 450 °C for 4 hours. Although the ramp rate is not specified, temperature control is critical for phase formation.

##### Step 5: Final Shaping and Activation
- **Process**:
  - Pelletize the calcined catalyst under a pressure of 20 MPa and subsequently sieve to obtain particles within the 20–40 mesh range.
  - Pre-reduce the catalyst with syngas (H₂/CO = 2) in a fixed-bed reactor at 300 °C for 4 hours, ensuring a reproducible activation step.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was used to determine the crystalline phases of the Fe–Zn–K catalysts. The catalyst samples were ground into a fine powder and mounted on the sample holder to ensure proper alignment for phase identification.
- **Instruments and Models**: Rigaku D/MAX-2200PC X‑ray diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å).
- **Characterization Conditions**: An operating voltage of 40 kV, current at 30 mA, and a 2θ scanning range from 5° to 85°.
- **Test Parameters**: Scanning conducted at a rate of 8°/min with a step size ensuring sufficient resolution for detecting phases such as hematite, ZnFe₂O₄ spinel, ZnO, and Fe₃O₄.
- **Key Notes**: Accurate peak alignment and intensity analysis is critical for identifying low-concentration species like K, which may not yield distinct peaks.

### 4.2 H₂‑Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: This technique assesses the reduction behavior of the catalyst. A 50 mg sample is first pretreated in helium at 350 °C for 1 hour to remove adsorbed species before being exposed to a reducing atmosphere.
- **Instruments and Models**: TP‑5000 chemisorption analyzer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Reduction is performed with a 5% H₂/95% N₂ gas mixture at a flow rate of 25 mL/min. The temperature is ramped from room temperature to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Precise flow control and ramp rate are maintained to generate reliable reduction profiles.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and well-controlled temperature ensure accurate reduction behavior profiling.

### 4.3 BET N₂ Physisorption
- **Operational Description**: BET analysis determines the surface area, pore volume, and pore diameter. The sample undergoes vacuum degassing prior to measurement.
- **Instruments and Models**: JW‑BK132F N₂ physisorption equipment.
- **Characterization Conditions**: A 200 mg sample is degassed under vacuum at 300 °C for 2 hours; the measurement is conducted at 196 °C.
- **Test Parameters**: Analysis follows the single point BET method.
- **Key Notes**: Ensuring complete degassing is critical for reproducible adsorption data.

### 4.4 CO₂‑Temperature‑Programmed Desorption (CO₂‑TPD)
- **Operational Description**: CO₂‑TPD assesses the CO₂ adsorption behavior of the catalysts. A 200 mg sample is pretreated in a H₂/CO (2:1) mixture at 300 °C for 4 hours.
- **Instruments and Models**: TP‑5000 analyzer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Post-pretreatment, the sample is purged with He at 50 mL/min for 30 minutes, then CO₂ is adsorbed at 50 °C until saturation. Desorption is performed by heating from room temperature to 700 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Controlled gas flow rates and temperature ramp are essential for reliable desorption profiling.
- **Key Notes**: Adequate purging minimizes residual gases affecting adsorption-desorption dynamics.

### 4.5 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)
- **Operational Description**: SEM coupled with EDS is used to investigate particle morphology and elemental composition. Samples are coated using an auto fine coater to achieve a uniform surface.
- **Instruments and Models**: JEOL JSM‑6360LV SEM equipped with the JEOL JED‑2300 EDS system.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Standard SEM imaging conditions are maintained; coating improves imaging contrast.
- **Test Parameters**: EDS provides elemental analysis but specific mapping resolution is not detailed.
- **Key Notes**: Sample preparation is crucial for clear imaging and reliable elemental detection.

### 4.6 X‑Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

- **Instruments and Models**: Thermo Scientific Escalab 250 spectrometer.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pre‐reaction treatment designed to convert the as‐synthesized iron oxide precursors into their active metallic phases, essential for optimal performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This activation is strictly confined to catalyst synthesis and pre‐reaction preparation, excluding procedures employed solely for characterization.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is crucial for providing sufficient thermal energy to drive the reduction of iron oxides into the active iron species.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is pre‐reduced at 300 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature ensures that the iron oxide precursors are effectively transformed, facilitating a controlled reduction that enhances the catalyst’s activity while maintaining its structural integrity.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the rate and efficiency of the reduction reaction by affecting the interaction between the gaseous reducing agent and the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed under a pressure of 0.5 MPa in a stainless flow‐type pressurized fixed‐bed reactor, which has an 8 mm inner diameter and 400 mm length.
- **Observations:** Operating at 0.5 MPa supports a robust reduction process while keeping the reaction environment stable and reproducible.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
- **Definition:** The choice of reducing gas mixture is paramount in establishing an environment that is sufficiently reducing without causing over-reduction or sintering of the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with a H₂/CO molar ratio of 2 is employed during the activation step.
- **Observations:** The balanced reducing atmosphere provided by this syngas composition ensures effective reduction of the Fe oxides, promoting the formation of active iron sites while also aiding in the proper interaction between Fe and Zn species. This synergistic effect is particularly important in catalysts such as the Fe–Zn–K variants, where controlled reduction contributes to improved dispersion and stabilization of the metallic phases.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process is critical to achieve complete reduction without incurring adverse effects such as sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated for 4 hours.
- **Observations:** The 4-hour pre‐reduction period is optimized to ensure complete conversion of precursors while preserving a uniform particle size (~100 nm) and preventing agglomeration. This methodical design directly correlates with enhanced catalytic performance, evidenced by high CO₂ conversion and favorable selectivity toward light olefins.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the Fe–Zn–K catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å, 40 kV, 30 mA, 2θ = 5–85°, 8°/min). This analysis identifies the presence and transformation of crystalline phases, which are critical for catalytic performance and stability.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of Fe₂O₃ and K-promoted Fe₂O₃ samples displayed peaks characteristic of rhombohedral hematite (α-Fe₂O₃). With increasing Zn content, new peaks at 2θ = 30.5, 35.4, 36.9, 43.1, 53.4, 56.9, and 62.6 emerged, corresponding to the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel phase. ZnO peaks were also observed in all Zn-containing samples, with intensity increasing alongside Zn loading. In spent catalysts, Fe₃O₄ was detected in K-promoted Fe₂O₃, while Zn-containing samples predominantly exhibited ZnFe₂O₄. No iron carbide or potassium-related peaks were detected, likely due to low concentration or high dispersion.
- **Observations:** The formation of ZnFe₂O₄ and ZnO with increasing Zn content indicates strong Fe–Zn interaction and stabilization of iron species, which inhibits sintering and modifies catalytic properties.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were determined by N₂ physisorption (BET method) at −196 °C, providing surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter—parameters that influence active site accessibility and reactant diffusion.
- **Findings:** BET results for fresh catalysts showed a progressive increase in surface area and pore volume with higher Zn/Fe ratios:  
  - Fe₂O₃–K: 14.40 m²/g, 0.09 cm³/g, 25.85 nm  
  - 3Fe–1Zn–K: 16.35 m²/g, 0.11 cm³/g, 25.93 nm  
  - 1Fe–1Zn–K: 21.17 m²/g, 0.15 cm³/g, 27.76 nm  
  - 1Fe–3Zn–K: 31.47 m²/g, 0.16 cm³/g, 20.36 nm  
  - 1Fe–5Zn–K: 37.72 m²/g, 0.16 cm³/g, 16.94 nm  
- **Observations:** Moderate Zn addition (1Fe–1Zn–K) maximized pore diameter and balanced surface area, correlating with optimal catalytic performance. Excessive Zn led to smaller pores, likely due to ZnO cluster formation.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to assess particle morphology and size distribution, which affect catalyst dispersion and stability.
- **Findings:** SEM images revealed that K-promoted Fe₂O₃ samples formed regular, homogeneous spheres (~100 nm). Increasing Zn content resulted in more irregular and aggregated particles, attributed to ZnO cluster formation.
- **Observations:** The microwave-aided hydrothermal synthesis with urea ensured uniform particle size and dispersion at low to moderate Zn levels, while higher Zn loadings compromised uniformity.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Chemical States and Elemental Composition
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterized surface chemical states and elemental composition, providing insight into Fe, Zn, and K distribution and oxidation states.
- **Findings:** Fe 2p₃/₂ binding energy for Fe₂O₃–K was ~710.6 eV, with a satellite peak 8 eV higher. Increasing Zn shifted Fe 2p binding energies higher, indicating stronger Fe–Zn interaction and ZnFe₂O₄ formation. Surface atomic concentrations (e.g., 1Fe–1Zn–K: Fe 19.52%, Zn 5.68%, K 5.00%) showed Zn and K enrichment at the surface, with Fe/K ratios nearly constant except at highest Zn loading.
- **Observations:** The enrichment of Zn and K on the surface and the shift in Fe binding energies reflect altered electronic environments, which are linked to improved selectivity and stability.

#### 6.1.5 Reducibility and CO₂ Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) assessed catalyst reducibility and CO₂ adsorption, respectively.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR showed that Zn addition increased reduction temperatures due to ZnFe₂O₄ formation, while excessive Zn (ZnO clusters) shifted reduction peaks lower, indicating improved Fe dispersion. CO₂-TPD revealed that Fe₂O₃–K had the highest CO₂ uptake (1.501 mmol/g), which decreased with Zn addition (e.g., 0.692 mmol/g for 1Fe–1Zn–K), and desorption peaks shifted to lower temperatures.
- **Observations:** The formation of ZnFe₂O₄ stabilizes iron species and modifies reduction behavior, while changes in CO₂ adsorption suggest that the Fischer–Tropsch step, rather than CO₂ activation, is rate-limiting in these catalysts.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ in the feed that is transformed into products. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (H₂/CO₂ = 3, GHSV = 1000 h⁻¹, 320 °C, 0.5 MPa), the 1Fe–1Zn–K catalyst achieved a CO₂ conversion of 51.03%. Other Fe–Zn–K variants exhibited the following conversions: 3Fe–1Zn–K (52.33%), 1Fe–3Zn–K (50.48%), and 1Fe–5Zn–K (46.68%). The Fe₂O₃–K reference showed 53.94% conversion, while potassium-modified Fe/ZrO₂ reached 42%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal Fe/Zn ratio (1:1) balances high conversion and selectivity. Excessive Zn content (1Fe–5Zn–K) leads to reduced conversion, attributed to the formation of unreducible ZnFe₂O₄ phases.

#### 6.2.2 C₂–C₄ Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity toward C₂–C₄ olefins reflects the fraction of converted CO₂ forming light olefins, calculated as:
\[
\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4\ \text{Olefin Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4,\text{olefin}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 1Fe–1Zn–K catalyst exhibited a C₂–C₄ olefin selectivity of 53.58 wt.%. Other variants: 3Fe–1Zn–K (50.65%), 1Fe–3Zn–K (52.02%), 1Fe–5Zn–K (50.16%), Fe₂O₃–K (49.86%), and Fe/ZrO₂–K (46%).
- **Observations and Trends:** The 1Fe–1Zn–K catalyst maximizes light olefin production, with an olefin/paraffin ratio of 6.86 in the C₂–C₄ fraction, indicating strong suppression of paraffin and heavy hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO₂ yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity for 1Fe–1Zn–K is reported as 7.12 wt.%. For comparison, 3Fe–1Zn–K (7.45%), 1Fe–3Zn–K (7.68%), and 1Fe–5Zn–K (8.21%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane formation remains low across all Fe–Zn–K catalysts, with a slight increase at higher Zn loadings.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO₂ forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_5^+\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 1Fe–1Zn–K catalyst shows a C₅⁺ selectivity of 13.24 wt.%, with 3Fe–1Zn–K (14.32%), 1Fe–3Zn–K (12.85%), and 1Fe–5Zn–K (11.97%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Zn content suppresses C₅⁺ hydrocarbon formation, favoring light olefins.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO₂ conversion and selectivity over time-on-stream.
- **Experimental Data:** The 1Fe–1Zn–K catalyst maintained stable CO₂ conversion and olefin selectivity over 216 hours, with negligible deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The formation of ZnFe₂O₄ spinel phases enhances catalyst durability by preventing sintering and stabilizing active sites.

#### 6.2.6 Olefin/Paraffin Ratio (O/P)
- **Description:** The O/P ratio in the C₂–C₄ fraction reflects the selectivity for olefins over paraffins:
\[
\text{O/P Ratio} = \frac{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4\ \text{olefins}}{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4\ \text{paraffins}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 1Fe–1Zn–K catalyst achieved an O/P ratio of 6.86.
- **Observations and Trends:** This high ratio demonstrates the catalyst’s strong preference for light olefin production.

**Summary:** The Fe–Zn–K catalysts, particularly at a 1:1 Fe/Zn ratio, deliver high CO₂ conversion, exceptional selectivity toward light olefins, low methane and C₅⁺ formation, and outstanding long-term stability, making them highly effective for selective CO₂ hydrogenation.
