## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the xZr/Ru catalyst is performed via an incipient co‑impregnation method that ensures accurate loading of Ru (≈2 wt.%) and a controlled Zr/Ru molar ratio (varying from 0 to 1). The following step-by-step procedure describes the experimental process in detail, enabling reproducibility and clarity for additional researchers.

#### Incipient Co‑Impregnation Method: xZr/Ru Catalyst Synthesis

1. • Precursor Mixing and Co‑precipitation  
  – Prepare impregnating solutions of ruthenium and zirconium precursors in water at calculated concentrations to target 2 wt.% Ru and selected Zr/Ru molar ratios.  
  – Mix the solutions under continuous stirring to promote uniform interaction with the SiO₂ support.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Following the initial mixing, induce precipitation–deposition by gradually adding a deposition agent if required.  
  – This step facilitates a homogeneous distribution of metal species onto the support before subsequent drying.

3. • Drying  
  – Dry the impregnated sample at 60 °C for 4 hours.  
  – This controlled drying removes moisture while preserving the distribution of Ru and Zr precursors.

4. • Calcination  
  – Calcinate the dried sample in air at 400 °C for 3 hours using a ramping rate of 2 °C/min.  
  – This step converts the precursors into their respective oxides while maintaining the structural integrity of the catalyst.

5. • Reduction and Post‑treatment  
  – Reduce the RuO₂ precursor to metallic Ru by treating the calcined catalyst in pure H₂ at 450 °C for 2 hours.  
  – This hydrogen treatment is critical for achieving an active metallic phase, which is later confirmed through XRD and TEM analyses.  

The method’s design—comprising controlled precursor mixing, regulated drying, and precisely timed calcination and reduction steps—enhances catalyst homogeneity and performance. Additionally, the controlled incorporation of the ZrO₂ promoter, managed by adjusting the molar ratio, plays a pivotal role in engineering the Zr–O–Ru interface, ultimately boosting hydrogen spillover and catalytic activity during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation step is a critical pre-reaction treatment designed to convert the RuO₂ precursor into active metallic Ru necessary for efficient Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This process establishes the Zr–O–Ru interfacial structure on the SiO₂ support that is crucial for improving hydrogen spillover and CO dissociation.

- **Definition:** The activation temperature ensures that the thermal energy is sufficient for the H₂ reduction of RuO₂ to metallic Ru while preserving nanoparticle dispersion.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced under a pure hydrogen atmosphere at 450 °C.
- **Observations:** At 450 °C, hydrogen reacts readily with RuO₂, ensuring complete and uniform conversion to metallic Ru, which is essential for optimal catalytic performance.

- **Experimental Conditions:** No specific pressure parameters are provided; the process is conducted under conditions that imply atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Operating under these ambient conditions simplifies the procedure and has proven sufficient for effective catalyst reduction.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The choice of activation gas directly influences the reduction process by supplying the necessary reducing species.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A pure hydrogen (H₂) atmosphere is used for the activation step.
- **Observations:** Hydrogen acts as a reducing agent to convert RuO₂ to metallic Ru, thereby forming an active phase and promoting the formation of the Zr–O–Ru interfacial structure vital for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time and Heating Ramp
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process is critical for achieving complete reduction without inducing sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst undergoes H₂ treatment for durations ranging from 2 to 4 hours with a controlled heating rate of 2 °C/min.
- **Observations:** This controlled ramp minimizes sintering, and the selected time window ensures sufficient reduction while maintaining high dispersion of Ru nanoparticles. Post-activation, the catalyst demonstrates excellent stability during extended reaction periods.

Through these carefully defined activation parameters, the catalyst achieves an optimal balance of reduction efficiency and structural integrity, setting the stage for high performance in Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch experiments were performed within a temperature range of 260 °C to 450 °C. The reactor temperature was precisely controlled by first pressurizing with the H₂/CO feed gas and then increasing the temperature in a stepwise fashion at a ramping rate of 2 °C/min until the desired set-point was reached. This controlled temperature profile not only enhances syngas activation and promotes the formation of longer hydrocarbon chains but also plays a significant role in lowering the apparent activation energy—from 81.5 kJ/mol for the unpromoted catalyst to 44.6 kJ/mol for the optimized 0.5Zr/Ru system.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The reaction pressure conditions were maintained at discrete values of 4, 6, and 8 MPa. These elevated pressures help drive syngas concentration toward the active sites, thereby favoring the formation of long-chain olefins while suppressing undesired byproducts. The fixed-bed stainless steel reactor equipped with a quartz lining ensures stable pressure control throughout the catalytic tests.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
To optimize mass transfer and maintain a consistent CO conversion (reported between 7.2% and 10.7%), the catalyst testing was performed under various gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) conditions. Specifically, GHSV values of 1500, 3000, 4500, and 4000 mL/(gcat·h) were employed in different experiments. These values were carefully selected to balance the throughput and contact time between the syngas and catalyst.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The experiments utilized a syngas feed comprised of H₂ and CO. Although the exact H₂/CO ratio was not explicitly provided, the controlled composition is noted to be critical for effective CO dissociation and to influence product distribution favoring olefin formation.

#### 5.2.5 Reactor Configuration and Performance Observations  
A fixed-bed reactor fitted with online gas chromatography and mass spectrometry monitoring was used to validate reproducibility of the reaction conditions. Under these settings, the optimal 0.5Zr/Ru catalyst exhibited remarkable stability over 110 hours, with CO conversion averaging around 38.2% and maintained selectivities of 5.8–7.0% for CH₄ and below 0.5% for CO₂.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS/EXAFS). These techniques are essential for identifying the presence and transformation of active phases and for confirming the formation of interfacial structures critical to catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns, recorded on a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer (Cu Kα, 40 kV, 40 mA, 2°/min), confirmed the complete reduction of RuO₂ (JCPDS 43-1027) to metallic Ru (JCPDS 06-0663) after H₂ treatment. No diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline ZrO₂ were observed at low Zr loadings, indicating high dispersion or amorphous character. XANES and EXAFS at the Zr K-edge revealed that at low Zr/Ru ratios, Zr exists mainly in a tetragonal phase as atomically or monolayer-dispersed species, while higher Zr/Ru ratios lead to the emergence of monoclinic ZrO₂. EXAFS fitting identified a Zr–Ru scattering path at ~2.7 Å, distinct from bulk ZrO₂, confirming the formation of Zr–O–Ru interfacial structures.
- **Observations:** The formation and stability of the Zr–O–Ru interface are directly linked to enhanced catalytic activity, as these structures persist after reaction and facilitate hydrogen spillover.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Chemical States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and XAS were employed to determine the oxidation states and surface distribution of Ru and Zr, which are critical for understanding catalyst reactivity and interface formation.
- **Findings:** XPS spectra (ThermoFisher K-Alpha, Al Kα, calibrated to C1s at 284.8 eV) showed Ru 3d₅/₂ peaks at 279.9–280.2 eV and Ru 3d₃/₂ at 461.0–461.5 eV, consistent with metallic Ru. The Zr 3d₅/₂ peak confirmed Zr in the +4 oxidation state. Surface Zr/Ru ratios, calculated from XPS peak areas, were higher than bulk values (from XRF), indicating surface enrichment of Zr. Depth profiling via Ar-ion sputtering further confirmed Zr’s localization at the surface. XANES/EXAFS corroborated the intimate interaction between Zr and Ru at the interface.
- **Observations:** The surface enrichment and stable oxidation states of Ru and Zr are essential for the formation of active interfacial sites, which underpin the catalyst’s superior performance.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were used to assess particle morphology, size, and elemental dispersion.
- **Findings:** High-resolution TEM (JEOL ARM300F, 300 kV) and HAADF-STEM images revealed well-dispersed Ru nanoparticles on the SiO₂ support, with Zr species homogeneously distributed over both Ru and the support. No aggregation of Zr or Ru was observed before or after reaction. Lattice fringes with a spacing of 2.03 Å, characteristic of the (101) plane of hcp Ru, were consistently observed. The average Ru particle size remained uniform across samples, regardless of ZrO₂ loading.
- **Observations:** The high dispersion and intimate contact between Zr and Ru nanoparticles are crucial for maximizing interfacial active sites and ensuring catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Metal Dispersion
- **Description:** CO chemisorption was performed to quantify the exposed Ru surface area and assess the impact of ZrO₂ loading on metal dispersion.
- **Findings:** CO chemisorption (Micromeritics 2020) showed that increasing ZrO₂ content led to a gradual decrease in Ru metallic surface area, indicating partial coverage of Ru by ZrO₂. Despite this, the catalyst with a Zr/Ru ratio of 0.5 exhibited the highest intrinsic activity, suggesting that optimal ZrO₂ coverage enhances the formation of active interfacial sites.
- **Observations:** While excessive ZrO₂ can block Ru sites, moderate loading optimally balances surface accessibility and interfacial site density, directly correlating with improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Hydrogen Activation and Spillover
- **Description:** H₂-temperature programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and H₂-temperature programmed desorption (H₂-TPD) were used to evaluate hydrogen uptake and spillover, which are vital for Fischer–Tropsch catalysis.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR revealed that ZrO₂-promoted catalysts exhibited lower reduction temperatures than unpromoted samples, indicating facilitated reduction and enhanced hydrogen activation. Hydrogen spillover was further confirmed by color change in WO₃ mixed with the catalyst after H₂ treatment.
- **Observations:** Enhanced hydrogen activation and spillover, enabled by the Zr–O–Ru interface, are key contributors to the superior catalytic activity observed in the optimized catalyst.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions (260 °C, 5 bar, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 3000 mL·g⁻¹·h⁻¹), the unpromoted 0Zr/Ru catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 7.2%. Introduction of ZrO₂ as a promoter led to a volcano-like trend, with conversion peaking at 40.2% for the 0.5Zr/Ru catalyst. Further increases in Zr/Ru ratio (0.7, 1.0) resulted in decreased conversion, though still above the unpromoted level.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal Zr/Ru molar ratio (0.5) maximizes the formation of Zr–O–Ru interfacial sites, dramatically enhancing CO conversion. Excessive ZrO₂ coverage reduces exposed Ru surface area, diminishing activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For the 0.5Zr/Ru catalyst, CO₂ selectivity remained consistently below 0.5% over 110 hours of operation. Other formulations, such as Na/S/Mn-modified Co catalysts, exhibited higher CO₂ selectivity (up to 3%).
- **Observations and Trends:** The ZrO₂ promoter effectively suppresses CO₂ formation, with the 0.5Zr/Ru catalyst achieving the lowest CO₂ selectivity among tested samples.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 0.5Zr/Ru catalyst maintained CH₄ selectivity between 5.8% and 7.0% during extended testing at standard conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Low CH₄ selectivity is consistently observed for ZrO₂-promoted catalysts, supporting enhanced chain growth and olefin production.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C₅⁺ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C₅⁺,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 0.5Zr/Ru catalyst achieved a C₅⁺ selectivity of approximately 80% under optimal conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** High C₅⁺ selectivity is attributed to the Zr–O–Ru interface, which promotes C–C coupling and long-chain olefin formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution:
\[
\text{ASF:} \quad W_n = n(1-\alpha)^2\alpha^{n-1}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** The 0.5Zr/Ru catalyst exhibited an α value consistent with high long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity (exact value not numerically specified in the article, but inferred from product distribution).
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced α values correlate with increased Zr–O–Ru interfacial site density, favoring chain growth.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over time. Deactivation rate is defined as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 0.5Zr/Ru catalyst maintained stable CO conversion (~38.2%), Ru time yield (~0.914 molCO·gRu⁻¹·h⁻¹), CO₂ selectivity (<0.5%), CH₄ selectivity (5.8–7.0%), and C₅⁺ selectivity (~80%) over 110 hours, indicating negligible deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst demonstrates excellent long-term stability, with the apparent activation energy reduced from 81.5 kJ·mol⁻¹ (0Zr/Ru) to 44.6 kJ·mol⁻¹ (0.5Zr/Ru), reflecting more efficient active sites and sustained performance.

#### 6.2.7 Intrinsic Activity Metrics (RuTY and TOF)
- **Description:** Ru time yield (RuTY) and turnover frequency (TOF) quantify intrinsic activity:
\[
\text{RuTY} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted}}{\text{g Ru} \cdot \text{h}}
\]
\[
\text{TOF} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted}}{\text{mol surface Ru} \cdot \text{s}}
\]
