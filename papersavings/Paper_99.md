## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis for the Fe Fischer–Tropsch catalyst (100Fe/5Cu/4.2K) is performed via a controlled coprecipitation technique followed by binder incorporation and spray drying. The process is reproducible and carefully designed to ensure optimal catalyst performance.

#### • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
1. The synthesis begins by dissolving iron nitrate, copper nitrate, and tetraethylorthosilicate (for the silica component) in deionized water.  
2. Constant pH is maintained by the careful addition of aqueous ammonia until the desired pH is reached, ensuring uniform precipitation.  
3. Observed phenomena include the formation of a solid precipitate with characteristic color changes, indicating successful precursor formation.

#### • Precipitation–Deposition  
4. Following the initial coprecipitation, a precipitation–deposition step is implemented where a support precursor (binder silica) is added dropwise.  
5. This step, occurring immediately after precursor mixing and before washing, further deposits promoter species onto the precipitating particles.  
6. The mixture is stirred continuously for 1 hour to ensure complete deposition.

#### • Drying  
7. The precipitate is then thoroughly washed with deionized water using vacuum filtration, removing impurities before drying.  
8. Subsequent drying is performed in a bench‐scale Niro spray dryer operating at 270°C. The dryer dimensions are typically 1 m in diameter and 2 m tall, ensuring scalability up to 500 g batches.

#### • Calcination and Post‑treatment  
9. The dried catalyst precursor is calcined in a muffle furnace at 300°C for 5 hours to stabilize the active phases.  
10. Post‑calcination, the catalyst undergoes a reduction step under a hydrogen/argon gas mixture to activate the metallic phases.  
11. Finally, potassium bicarbonate is introduced to integrate the promoter, enhancing catalytic performance during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

This structured approach, which includes careful pH control, controlled binder addition (with silica levels ranging from 4 to 20 wt.%), and precise temperature and time settings, ensures both high reproducibility and optimal catalyst properties. Comparative analysis indicates that direct binder addition via spray drying simplifies the process, while additional binders like kaolin require extra handling and may affect attrition resistance.

### 5.2 Reaction

The FT reaction was conducted under rigorously controlled conditions to ensure reproducibility and reliable assessment of catalyst performance. Each key parameter is directly extracted from the experimental details provided in the article.

#### Reaction Temperature
The reaction was maintained at 270 °C, a temperature that critically influences both syngas activation and the conversion efficiency of the catalyst. #### Reaction Pressure
The experiment was carried out at an operating pressure of 1.48 MPa, a setting that promotes optimal interactions between syngas components and the catalyst. This pressure level was maintained throughout the reaction phase to maximize CO conversion and favor the production of C₅⁺ hydrocarbons. The established reaction pressure not only supports efficient gas-solid contact but also contributes to a balanced product distribution, aligning with the observed low methane output.

#### Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
A syngas mixture with an H₂/CO ratio of 0.67 was used, which is pivotal in dictating the hydrocarbon distribution during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This specific ratio aids in steering the reaction towards the production of higher molecular weight hydrocarbons, while also influencing selectivity and overall product yield.

#### Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The gas hourly space velocity was set at 2.0 NL/g-cat/h. This parameter defines the reactant flow relative to the catalyst mass and is crucial for determining the residence time of the reactants in the reactor. The chosen space-time velocity ensured sufficient contact time for the reaction to proceed effectively, contributing to the observed high conversion and selectivity trends.

These parameters, coupled with a high-pressure fixed-bed reactor constructed from a 1 cm i.d. stainless steel tube, create a well-defined setup that guarantees reproducibility and high performance in Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structural Evolution (XRD)
- **Description:** X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to identify the crystalline phases present in the catalysts at various stages. Measurements were performed using a Phillips PW1800 X-ray unit with Cu Kα radiation, scanning from 5° to 75° 2θ at 0.05° 2θ/s.
- **Findings:** Fresh catalysts predominantly exhibited α-Fe₂O₃ (hematite) phases. After CO pretreatment at 280°C for 16 hours, new peaks corresponding to Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) and χ-Fe₂.₅C (iron carbide) appeared. Following 100 hours of Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, the used catalyst was mainly composed of Fe₂.₅C.
- **Observations:** The sequential transformation from α-Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ and then to Fe₂.₅C under CO and FT conditions demonstrates the dynamic phase evolution critical for catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity (BET)
- **Description:** Nitrogen physisorption (BET) analysis was conducted using a Micromeritics Gemini 2360 system. Samples were degassed at 120°C for 1 hour prior to measurement. Surface area (m²/g) and pore volume (cm³/g) were determined for catalysts with varying binder silica content.
- **Findings:** Increasing binder silica from 4 to 16 wt.% led to a rise in BET surface area: Fe–bSi(4) at 80.3 m²/g, Fe–bSi(8) at 95.7 m²/g, Fe–bSi(12) at 121 m²/g, and Fe–bSi(16) at 151 m²/g (fresh samples). After reduction, surface areas decreased (e.g., Fe–bSi(12) to 68.7 m²/g). Pore volume remained stable (~0.56 cm³/g) up to 12 wt.% binder silica, increasing to 0.68 cm³/g at 16 wt.%.
- **Observations:** Optimal binder silica content (10–12 wt.%) maximizes attrition resistance and maintains high surface area, while higher levels further increase surface area but may compromise selectivity.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Metal Dispersion (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was performed using a Micromeritics 2705 TPR/TPD system. Samples were pretreated under Ar at 400°C, then reduced in H₂/Ar (5/95 vol%) from 25–900°C at 10°C/min.
- **Findings:** Two dominant reduction peaks were observed: one at 320°C (Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄) and another at 750°C (Fe₃O₄ → Fe). A shoulder at ~250°C was attributed to CuO reduction.
- **Observations:** The presence of distinct reduction steps confirms the sequential reduction of iron species and effective incorporation of copper as a promoter.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size (SEM)
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a Cambridge Stereoscan 100 provided insights into particle morphology and size distribution.
- **Findings:** Catalysts exhibited roughly spherical particles with diameters ranging from 30 to 90 μm, characteristic of spray-dried materials.
- **Observations:** The uniform, spherical morphology supports high attrition resistance and is well-suited for slurry bubble column reactor applications.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst. It is typically calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO entering and exiting the reactor, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch (FT) conditions (270°C, 1.48 MPa, 2 NL/g-cat/h, H₂/CO = 0.67), the Fe–bSi(12) catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 94.3%. Across the Fe–bSi series, conversion ranged from 94.1% to 95.5%. The Ruhrchemie benchmark catalyst, under identical conditions, exhibited a lower conversion of 86%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal CO conversion was observed at 10–12 wt.% binder silica. Increasing binder silica above this range or incorporating additional binders (precipitated silica or kaolin) led to reduced CO conversion, with kaolin addition causing a pronounced drop (down to 42.8% at 24 wt.% kaolin).

#### 6.2.2 Methane (CH₄) Selectivity
- **Description:** Methane selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe–bSi(12), methane selectivity was 6.8%. The Fe–bSi series showed methane selectivity increasing from 6.8% (12 wt.% binder silica) to 9.9% (20 wt.% binder silica). The Ruhrchemie catalyst had a higher methane selectivity of 8.3%. In scale-up tests (500 g batch, HPR-43), methane selectivity remained below 4% over 125 hours.
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane selectivity was minimized at 10–12 wt.% binder silica. Higher binder or additional silica/kaolin increased methane formation, indicating less favorable hydrocarbon distribution.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, reflecting the catalyst’s ability to promote chain growth.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe–bSi(12) exhibited a C₁₂⁺ selectivity of 60.8% and a C₅–C₁₁ selectivity of 12.8%, totaling approximately 73.6% for C₅⁺ products. The Ruhrchemie catalyst showed a C₅⁺ selectivity of 56.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** C₅⁺ selectivity was highest at optimal binder silica content (10–12 wt.%), remaining nearly constant in this range. Increasing binder or adding other binders shifted selectivity toward lighter hydrocarbons, reducing C₅⁺ output.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
\[
\text{ASF:} \quad \log(W_n/n) = n \log(\alpha) + \text{constant}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** Fe–bSi(12) had an α value of 0.89. Across the Fe–bSi series, α ranged from 0.88 to 0.92, with the highest values (0.91–0.92) at 4–8 wt.% binder silica.
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor was maximized at moderate binder silica levels, correlating with higher C₅⁺ selectivity. Excess binder or additional additives reduced α, favoring lighter products.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability was assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over extended operation (up to 125 hours).
- **Experimental Data:** The scaled-up Fe–bSi(12) catalyst (HPR-43) maintained 95% CO conversion and <4% methane selectivity over 125 hours, with no significant deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst demonstrated excellent stability and attrition resistance at optimal binder content. Additives such as kaolin or precipitated silica compromised both stability and selectivity.

---

**Summary:** The Fe–bSi(12) catalyst, with 12 wt.% binder silica, exhibited the best balance of high CO conversion, low methane selectivity, high C₅⁺ selectivity, and robust stability, outperforming the Ruhrchemie benchmark and other formulations. Optimal binder content is critical for maximizing FT performance and catalyst durability.
