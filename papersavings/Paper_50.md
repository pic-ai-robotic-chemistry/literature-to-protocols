## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article reports the synthesis of two closely related Fischer–Tropsch catalysts—an unpromoted monolith-supported Fe catalyst and a K-promoted version—both prepared by thermal decomposition of precursor salts followed by controlled post‑processing treatments. A comparative analysis indicates that while the thermal decomposition method is straightforward and scalable, it demands precise temperature control. In contrast, the impregnation method used for potassium promotion, although an additional step, allows fine‑tuning of catalytic properties through promoter incorporation.

#### Unpromoted Fe Catalyst
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Al(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, with Al incorporated at 1 wt.% relative to Fe.  
  – Solvent: Water; ensuring homogeneity through thorough mixing.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – This step is not explicitly reported, and the synthesis proceeds directly to drying.
3. • Drying  
  – Conditions: The precursor solution is dried in an oven at 200°C for 1 hour.  
  – Equipment: Standard laboratory oven.
4. • Calcination  
  – Conditions: Calcination is carried out at 200°C for 12 hours.  
  – Purpose: Thermal decomposition of metal nitrates to metal oxides.
5. • Post‑treatment (Reduction)  
  – Conditions: The calcined catalyst is reduced at 400°C in hydrogen.  
  – Equipment: Furnace with controlled hydrogen flow.

#### K‑Promoted Fe Catalyst
1. • Starting Point:  
  – Begins with the reduced and passivated unpromoted Fe catalyst.
2. • Impregnation of Promoter  
  – Reagent: An aqueous KNO₃ solution is used to introduce potassium, achieving a K/Fe molar ratio of approximately 1.3%.  
  – The solution is added to the catalyst via impregnation.
3. • Wash‑coating onto Supports  
  – Process: The impregnated catalyst is mixed in a 30/70 weight ratio with a high‑surface‑area alumina binder and then wash‑coated onto ceramic monoliths (e.g., Corning Celcor).  
  – Advantage: Provides uniform dispersion and an increased active surface area.

Overall, the detailed step‑by‑step procedures establish reproducibility by controlling reagent concentrations, drying/calcination temperatures and durations, and by incorporating a secondary impregnation step for promoter addition. This systematic approach—though lacking a dedicated precipitation–deposition step—ensures both high purity and performance consistency across synthesis batches.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the reagent preparation for each catalyst used in the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiment is detailed, ensuring replicability and precision.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Catalyst A – Monolith-Supported Fe Catalyst (Unpromoted)
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 0.15g using an analytical balance.
- **Al(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 0.002g to achieve a 1 wt.% Al relative to Fe.

##### 3.1.1.2 Catalyst B – K-Promoted Fe Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 0.15g using an analytical balance.
- **Al(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 0.002g to maintain consistency with the unpromoted formulation.
- **KNO₃**: Weighed 0.005g to provide a K/Fe molar ratio of approximately 1.3%, essential for adjusting product selectivity.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Catalyst A – Monolith-Supported Fe Catalyst (Unpromoted)
- **Deionized Water**: Poured 50mL into a beaker containing the weighed metal nitrates to dissolve the salts uniformly.

##### 3.1.2.2 Catalyst B – K-Promoted Fe Catalyst
- **Deionized Water**: Poured 50mL into the initial solid mixture for complete dissolution of the Fe and Al nitrates.
- **Aqueous KNO₃ Solution**: Transferred 30mL into the passivated Fe catalyst using a graduated cylinder, facilitating precise promoter incorporation.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Catalyst A – Monolith-Supported Fe Catalyst (Unpromoted)
- **Fe/Al Nitrate Solution**: Mixed by stirring at 800rpm for 30 minutes at room temperature, ensuring a homogeneous solution.
- **Final Mixture**: Ultrasonically treated for 15 minutes to further guarantee complete dispersion of all solid participants.

##### 3.1.3.2 Catalyst B – K-Promoted Fe Catalyst
- **Fe/Al Nitrate Solution**: Mixed in an identical manner as Catalyst A (stirred at 800rpm for 30 minutes at ambient conditions).
- **Impregnation Mixture**: After adding the aqueous KNO₃ solution, the combined mixture was stirred at 600rpm for 20 minutes under a nitrogen atmosphere, ensuring uniform distribution of the potassium promoter while maintaining inert conditions.

These steps and conditions are clearly documented to assure reproducibility and allow knowledgeable researchers to replicate the synthesis accurately.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Monolith-Supported Fe Catalyst (Unpromoted)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (e.g., 10.0 g) and Al(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (quantitatively adjusted to yield 1 wt.% Al relative to Fe), dissolved in 50 mL deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Room temperature with continuous stirring; specific pH adjustment details are not provided.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the Fe and Al nitrate salts in water to form a clear, homogeneous slurry.
  - Stir the mixture for approximately 1 hour to ensure complete dissolution.
  - Proceed directly with drying once homogeneity is confirmed.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution of precursors to achieve uniform catalyst composition.

##### Step 2: Purification, Drying, and Calcination
- **Purification & Drying**:
  - Directly transfer the homogeneous slurry into a controlled laboratory oven.
  - Dry at 120°C until the sample is completely free of moisture.
- **Calcination**:
  - Heat the dried precursor in air at 200°C for 12 hours using a calibrated oven.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor the transition from drying to calcination, ensuring a steady temperature ramp to maintain reproducibility.

##### Step 3: Activation and Wash-Coating
- **Activation**:
  - Reduce the calcined catalyst at 400°C under a flow of hydrogen to generate the active phase.
- **Wash-Coating**:
  - Mix the reduced catalyst with a high-surface-area alumina binder in a 30/70 weight ratio.
  - Slurry the mixture in water and uniformly apply it onto Corning Celcor ceramic monoliths.
  - Dry each layer at 200°C for 1 hour to secure uniform dispersion.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Monolith-Supported K-Promoted Fe Catalyst

##### Step 1: Catalyst Assembly with Promoter Incorporation
- **Reagents**: Utilize the reduced and passivated Fe catalyst from the unpromoted process; prepare an aqueous KNO₃ solution.
- **Conditions**: Impregnate under room temperature conditions while stirring.
- **Process**:
  - Add the KNO₃ solution dropwise to the Fe catalyst to achieve a target K/Fe molar ratio of approximately 1.3%.
  - Stir the mixture thoroughly, ensuring even distribution of the promoter.
- **Key Steps**: Achieve uniform impregnation without significant delays between additions.

##### Step 2: Post-Impregnation Treatment and Final Wash-Coating
- **Activation**:
  - Subject the impregnated catalyst to an additional reduction at 400°C in hydrogen.
- **Wash-Coating**:
  - Apply the same alumina binder mixing and wash-coating procedure as described for the unpromoted catalyst to ensure consistency across both formulations.

This detailed workflow—from raw precursor preparation, through purification, drying, calcination, and activation, to final wash-coating—ensures a replicable synthesis route for both catalyst formulations.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis in this study consists solely of a hydrogen reduction step, which is a critical pretreatment to transform the catalyst precursors into active forms ready for reaction.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential for driving the reduction of iron oxide precursors to their active metallic state, setting the stage for the formation of catalytically active carbide species during subsequent reaction conditions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Both the unpromoted Fe catalyst and the K-promoted Fe catalyst undergo a hydrogen reduction at 400 °C. This precise temperature ensures that the transformation from the precursor phase to the active phase is complete.
- **Observations:** The chosen temperature of 400 °C provides an optimal balance where complete reduction is achieved without inducing excessive sintering, thereby preserving the catalyst’s surface characteristics necessary for effective Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The activation gas plays a fundamental role in reducing the oxide precursors by eliminating oxygen from the catalyst material, thus facilitating the creation of the desired metallic or carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are activated in a flowing hydrogen atmosphere. Hydrogen serves as the reducing agent, ensuring that both catalyst types are uniformly prepared for further processing.
- **Observations:** The use of flowing hydrogen allows for efficient reduction by continuously removing byproducts, which contributes to a consistent activation process. Notably, both catalyst variations—unpromoted and K-promoted—experience the same hydrogen reduction treatment, with the differentiation arising only in the subsequent impregnation step for the K-promoted catalyst.

This activation step, free from additional parameters such as pressure, space-time velocity, or extended duration, is integrated seamlessly into the overall catalyst synthesis, ensuring reproducibility and optimal performance during the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction tests.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis as it directly influences catalyst activity, syngas conversion efficiency, and hydrocarbon product distribution.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction temperature was varied between 200 °C and 280 °C. At 200 °C, the unpromoted Fe catalyst exhibited very low CO conversion (approximately 0.47%), which increased markedly to about 5.4% at 240 °C. Tests conducted at 280 °C revealed accelerated catalyst deactivation.  
- **Observations:** It was noted that while increasing the temperature improved the initial CO conversion, a significant decline in catalytic activity occurred at higher temperatures. For instance, the unpromoted Fe catalyst experienced up to a 90% activity loss over a 24-hour period at 280 °C. Additionally, modest changes in product selectivity (ranging from 1% to 20%) were observed alongside more pronounced activity drops (40%–80%) during extended reactions.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** The reaction pressure determines the partial pressures of reactants, thereby influencing the concentration of syngas and the equilibrium of the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** All catalytic evaluations were conducted at a total system pressure of 10 atm.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 10 atm ensured higher partial pressures of H₂ and CO, which aided in enhancing both the reaction rates and the selectivity towards desired hydrocarbon products. This pressure setting provided a reproducible framework under which the catalysts’ performance could be effectively compared.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio in the feed gas is an essential parameter that influences hydrocarbon chain growth and product distribution in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The feed gas ratio was maintained in the range between 1 and 3 across experiments. Specifically, at a H₂/CO ratio of 1, the unpromoted Fe catalyst produced a high methane selectivity (approximately 61 mol%), whereas the K-promoted catalyst exhibited reduced methane formation (around 39 mol%) and enhanced selectivity for C₅–C₁₁ liquids and C₁₂⁺ waxes.  
- **Observations:** Adjustments in the H₂/CO ratio were found to balance chain growth probabilities, with lower ratios favoring methane production and higher ratios promoting the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons, thereby demonstrating the significant influence of catalyst promoter modifications.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Evolution
- **Description:** The identification and transformation of iron carbide phases during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis were assessed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). These techniques are critical for determining the active phases responsible for catalytic performance and for monitoring phase changes under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD and TEM analyses revealed the sequential formation and transformation of iron carbides: Fe₂.₂C (E′-carbide) forms initially, followed by Fe₅C₂ (X-carbide), and eventually Fe₃C (U-carbide) as the reaction progresses. The transformation sequence Fe₂.₂C → Fe₅C₂ → Fe₃C was consistently observed in both unpromoted and K-promoted catalysts.
- **Observations:** The presence of Fe₂.₂C and Fe₅C₂ correlates with higher catalytic activity, while the formation of Fe₃C is associated with catalyst deactivation. The rate of phase transformation is influenced by catalyst surface area and crystallite size, with higher surface area (smaller crystallites) promoting faster carburization and more rapid attainment of steady-state activity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Active Site Measurement
- **Description:** Surface area and active site density were evaluated using hydrogen chemisorption, which provides a quantitative measure of accessible iron sites. This parameter is essential for understanding catalyst dispersion and potential activity.
- **Findings:** For unsupported iron catalysts, the measured surface area was 10 m²/g. The incorporation of alumina as a binder increased the surface area of supported catalysts, as indicated by hydrogen uptake values: 70 mmol H₂/g for alumina-supported catalysts compared to 30 mmol H₂/g for unpromoted iron catalysts. These measurements were performed after reduction at 400°C, ensuring that only active metallic sites were probed.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface area due to alumina addition leads to smaller iron crystallites, which in turn accelerates carburization and improves initial catalytic activity. The use of a high-surface-area binder also aids in maintaining catalyst porosity and dispersion.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Phase Distribution
- **Description:** Morphological features and phase distribution were characterized by TEM, focusing on the identification of iron carbide phases and their spatial distribution within the catalyst matrix.
- **Findings:** TEM confirmed the coexistence and transformation of Fe₂.₂C, Fe₅C₂, and Fe₃C phases during reaction and deactivation. While explicit particle size or shape data were not reported, the observed phase evolution aligns with the catalytic performance trends.
- **Observations:** The rapid formation of active carbide phases in catalysts with higher surface area underscores the importance of morphological control in catalyst design. The wash-coating process, combined with alumina binder, ensures uniform dispersion and accessibility of active sites.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Promoter Distribution

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** For the unpromoted Fe catalyst (H₂/CO = 1.5, 10 atm), CO conversion increases with temperature: 0.47% at 200°C, 1.2% at 210°C, 2.3% at 220°C, 3.5% at 230°C, and 5.4% at 240°C. The K-promoted Fe catalyst (H₂/CO = 1.0) achieves 4.6–5.0% CO conversion at 240°C.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both catalysts show increased CO conversion with temperature. The K-promoted catalyst achieves comparable conversion to the unpromoted catalyst at 240°C, despite a lower H₂/CO ratio.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 220°C and H₂/CO = 1, both unpromoted and K-promoted catalysts exhibit CO₂ selectivity of approximately 12%. Across broader conditions, CO₂ selectivity ranges from 17–30% for Fe and 9–26% for Fe/K.
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium promotion tends to lower CO₂ selectivity, especially at higher temperatures and lower H₂/CO ratios.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the percentage of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 240°C, 10 atm, and H₂/CO = 1, the unpromoted Fe catalyst produces 61 mol% CH₄, while the K-promoted catalyst yields 39 mol%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium promotion significantly suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms.
- **Experimental Data:** At 240°C, the unpromoted Fe catalyst yields 8 mol% C₅–C₁₁ and 0.5 mol% C₁₂⁺, while the K-promoted catalyst achieves 22 mol% C₅–C₁₁ and 3 mol% C₁₂⁺.
- **Observations and Trends:** The K-promoted catalyst markedly enhances selectivity toward higher hydrocarbons, with increased yields of both liquids and waxes.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation in hydrocarbon formation, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher likelihood of forming longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** At 240°C, α = 0.69 for the unpromoted Fe catalyst and α = 0.79 for the K-promoted catalyst. Across 250–280°C, α ranges from 0.63–0.76 (Fe) and 0.72–0.89 (Fe/K).
- **Observations and Trends:** Potassium promotion consistently increases α, correlating with higher selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the loss of activity over time, modeled as:
  \[
  r_d = k_d' (a - a_\infty)^m
  \]
  where \( a \) is normalized activity, \( a_\infty \) is asymptotic activity, and \( m = 1 \).
- **Experimental Data:** The unpromoted Fe catalyst loses ~30% activity at 250°C and ~90% at 280°C over 24 hours. The K-promoted catalyst deactivates more slowly, with activation energies for deactivation of 108 kJ/mol (Fe) and 58 kJ/mol (Fe/K).
- **Observations and Trends:** Deactivation accelerates with temperature. Potassium promotion improves catalyst stability, reducing the rate and extent of deactivation.
