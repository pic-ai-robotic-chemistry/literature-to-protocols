## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalyst is synthesized via a continuous coprecipitation process that ensures uniform dispersion of iron within a silica matrix. This section outlines the sequential procedures and equipment used to produce a catalyst with a targeted iron loading of approximately 50.9 wt%, providing reproducible conditions for subsequent performance evaluation.

#### Continuous Coprecipitation Method

1. Catalyst Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Aqueous solutions of Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol are continuously fed into a mixing chamber where they are combined with a flowing ammonia solution.  
   • Reaction conditions are maintained at 80 ± 1 °C and controlled to reach a pH of 8.2–8.5, as monitored in real time using a pH meter.  
   • A continuous flow setup, incorporating pumps and mixing chambers, guarantees a homogeneous precursor mixture and consistent iron distribution.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   • Immediately following the mixing stage, precipitate formation occurs; the simultaneous deposition of iron and silica onto the support ensures uniform active phase distribution.  
   • During this step, observable formation of a fine precipitate confirms the progression of the reaction.

3. Drying  
   • The filtered precipitate is washed with distilled water to remove residual impurities and then dried at 120 °C for 12 h.  
   • This drying step is critical to remove moisture prior to thermal treatment.

4. Calcination  
   • The dried precursor is calcined at 500 °C for 5 h in air using a temperature-controlled furnace.  
   • Calcination facilitates the formation of iron oxide phases that act as precursors for iron carbide conversion.

5. Additional Post‑treatment (Carburization)  
   • Controlled carburization is performed in a fixed-bed reactor by treating the calcined catalyst with CO, H₂, or 2H₂/CO syngas at 300 °C for 20 h.  
   • Variations with CO exposure times of 5 h and 10 h are also examined to tune the formation of iron carbide phases (ε‑Fe₂C, Fe₇C₃, and χ‑Fe₅C₂).  

This detailed methodology not only supports reproducibility but also offers a comparative analysis with alternative methods in terms of scalability, cost, and equipment requirements.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD is used to identify the crystalline phases present in the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalyst and to estimate particle sizes via the Scherrer equation.
- **Instruments and Models**: Bruker D2 X‑ray diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å) at an operating voltage of 30 kV and current of 10 mA; samples are ground into powders and loaded in a sealed sample holder.
- **Test Parameters**: Continuous scanning mode from 10° to 90° 2θ at a rate of 0.04° per second; peak broadening is analyzed for crystallite size estimation.
- **Key Notes**: Precise alignment of the powdered sample is critical; the broad diffraction peaks indicate highly dispersed phases that may challenge discrimination between crystalline and amorphous components.

### 4.2 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & HAADF‑STEM with EDS
- **Operational Description**: TEM and HAADF‑STEM coupled with EDS are employed to examine catalyst morphology, particle dispersion, and elemental distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: JEM‑2100F (JEOL, Japan) and Talos 200A (FEI, America); HAADF‑STEM mode for high‐resolution imaging and elemental mapping.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Operating acceleration voltage of 200 kV; samples are prepared by dispersing a paraffin‐protected catalyst in ethanol, sonicated for 0.5 hours, and deposited onto copper grids with holey carbon films.
- **Test Parameters**: Magnification and exposure times are optimized to resolve particle sizes and phase boundaries.
- **Key Notes**: Careful removal of paraffin by sonication prevents oxidation while ensuring a well‐dispersed sample.

### 4.3 Mössbauer Spectroscopy (MES)
- **Operational Description**: MES quantifies phase compositions and distinguishes among various iron carbide phases by analyzing magnetic and electronic states.
- **Instruments and Models**: MR‑351 constant-acceleration Mössbauer spectrometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements conducted at 11 K to fully block magnetic relaxation; calibration is performed using α‑Fe foil.
- **Test Parameters**: Spectra are recorded using a triangular drive signal; absorption peaks are modeled using Lorentzian line shapes.
- **Key Notes**: Low temperature operation ensures accurate resolution of overlapping magnetic sextets and quadrupole doublets.

### 4.4 X‑Ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS)
- **Operational Description**: XAFS (comprising XANES and EXAFS) provides insights into the local coordination and electronic structure of iron.
- **Instruments and Models**: Experiments conducted at the 1W1B beamline at Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Transmission mode using a 2.5 GeV electron beam at 200 mA maximum current; energy resolution is <1–3×10⁻⁴ at 9 keV.
- **Test Parameters**: Spectra acquired over an energy range of 6900–7900 eV with an average acquisition time of 16 minutes.
- **Key Notes**: The combination of XANES and EXAFS allows detailed analysis of short-range order; an Fe foil reference standard ensures energy calibration.


## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
The catalyst precursor is activated via controlled carburization at 300 °C. This moderate temperature is critical because it is sufficiently high to enable the diffusion of carbon into the iron oxide particles and promote the formation of tailored iron carbide phases (including Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂) while suppressing the kinetically favored ε‑Fe₂C. Such conditions ensure a balance between effective carbo‐reduction and the prevention of undesirable sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
No specific activation pressure is delineated for the carburization process. The catalyst activation is performed in a fixed-bed reactor, where focus is placed on temperature, gas composition, and flow parameters, rather than on an explicitly controlled pressure during carburization.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
The carburization is executed using controlled gas atmospheres, namely pure CO, pure H₂, and a 2H₂/CO syngas mixture. Each gas composition plays a distinct role: a CO–rich atmosphere provides a high carbon chemical potential that strongly favors carbide formation (particularly Fe₇C₃), whereas H₂ promotes reduction with moderate carburization, and the syngas mixture influences the formation of χ‑Fe₅C₂. These choices directly impact the catalyst’s phase distribution and subsequent performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
A gas space velocity of 4000 mL/(g_cat·h) is employed during the carburization step. This parameter ensures consistent exposure of the catalyst to the reactive gases, facilitating uniform carburization across the catalyst bed, which is essential for reproducible formation of the targeted carbide phases.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
The activation procedure primarily involves a 20-hour carburization step. In addition, variations with 5-hour and 10-hour durations under CO exposure are explored to evaluate the effects of exposure time on carbide phase development. Extended activation times correlate with an increased formation of active phases, although careful optimization is necessary to avoid over‐carburization that may adversely affect catalyst stability.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature, a critical factor for syngas activation and carbide phase formation, was strictly maintained at 260 °C. This temperature is optimal for the formation of the active phase χ‑Fe₅C₂, which is associated with enhanced intrinsic activity and selectivity. The catalyst demonstrated good stability at 260 °C, as evidenced by consistent performance over the testing period and controlled carburization processes that prevented rapid deactivation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure

Operating at this pressure environment is instrumental in optimizing the water–gas shift reaction, directly impacting syngas concentration and product selectivity. This ratio is critical for balancing the conversion rates of hydrogen and carbon monoxide, thereby influencing both hydrocarbon chain length distribution and overall product selectivity. ## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural evolution of the Fe/SiO₂ catalyst were determined primarily by X-ray diffraction (XRD), with phase identification supported by Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES) and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS).
- **Findings:** XRD analysis revealed that the calcined catalyst exhibited broad peaks at 34.7° and 62.7°, corresponding to highly dispersed iron oxides (α‑Fe₂O₃ or γ‑Fe₂O₃). After controlled carburization, distinct iron carbide phases were observed depending on the gas atmosphere: χ‑Fe₅C₂ was predominant after syngas treatment, Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ (bimodal distribution) after CO treatment, and ε‑Fe₂C with χ‑Fe₅C₂ after H₂ treatment. For example, FeSi-CO displayed Fe₇C₃ peaks at 40.0°, 42.9°, 44.9°, 50.8°, and 53.4°, while FeSi-H₂ showed ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂ peaks at 37.7°, 41.4°, 43.2°, and 57.3°.
- **Observations:** The selective formation of ε‑Fe₂C, Fe₇C₃, or χ‑Fe₅C₂ is governed by the carburization atmosphere and duration, with phase stability confirmed under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) conditions by in situ XAFS and MES.

#### 6.1.2 Particle Size and Morphology
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM), and the Scherrer equation (applied to XRD data) were used to assess particle size, dispersion, and morphology.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed well-dispersed nanoparticles stabilized by the SiO₂ support. In the calcined catalyst, the average particle size was 5.5 nm. After carburization, χ‑Fe₅C₂ particles averaged 7.1–7.8 nm, Fe₇C₃ particles 27.5–28.2 nm, and ε‑Fe₂C particles 13.9–15.4 nm, confirming a bimodal size distribution in some samples. HAADF-STEM and EDX mapping further verified the spatial distribution and phase identity of the iron carbides.
- **Observations:** The silica support effectively prevents sintering and agglomeration, maintaining high dispersion and uniformity, which is critical for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Phase Quantification and Iron Valence States
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES) at 11 K and XAFS (XANES/EXAFS) were employed to quantify phase composition and elucidate iron oxidation states and local coordination.
- **Findings:** MES spectra identified and quantified the relative amounts of ε‑Fe₂C, Fe₇C₃, χ‑Fe₅C₂, and Fe₃O₄. For instance, FeSi-CO after 20 h CO treatment contained 26% χ‑Fe₅C₂, 16% Fe₇C₃, and the remainder as iron oxide. XANES spectra positioned between α-Fe and Fe₂O₃ references indicated mixed iron oxide and carbide states, with Fe–Fe bond lengths from EXAFS distinguishing between carbides: 2.17 Å (χ‑Fe₅C₂), 2.06 Å (Fe₇C₃), and 2.33 Å (ε‑Fe₂C).
- **Observations:** Higher iron carbide content, particularly Fe₇C₃, correlates with increased intrinsic catalytic activity. The reduction degree and specific carbide phase composition are directly linked to FTS performance.

#### 6.1.4 Phase Stability under Reaction Conditions
- **Description:** In situ MES and XAFS were used to monitor phase stability and transformation during FTS.
- **Findings:** Under typical FTS conditions (260 °C, 1–3 MPa), the phase composition remained stable, with only minor increases in carbide content over time. ε‑Fe₂C was found to transform into χ‑Fe₅C₂ at higher temperatures (>250 °C), while Fe₇C₃ remained stable under medium-temperature conditions.
- **Observations:** The active carbide phases are robust under reaction conditions, enabling reliable structure–performance correlations.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (260 °C, 3 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 4000 mL/(g_cat·h)), the Fe/SiO₂ catalysts exhibited the following CO conversions:
  - FeSi-CO: 50.8%
  - FeSi-H₂: 33.1%
  - FeSi-syn: 22.3%
  - Additional values include 44.8% for FeSi-H₂ and up to 49.2% for FeSi-syn under varied conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest CO conversion was achieved with the FeSi-CO catalyst, correlating with a higher content of Fe₇C₃ and χ-Fe₅C₂ phases. Increased carburization time (up to 20 h) further enhanced conversion, while the use of H₂ or syngas during pretreatment resulted in lower conversions due to differing phase compositions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** 
  - FeSi-CO: 9.7%
  - FeSi-H₂: 4.6%
  - FeSi-syn: 2.8%
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher CO₂ selectivity is associated with catalysts rich in Fe₇C₃ and χ-Fe₅C₂, indicating enhanced WGS activity. Lower CO₂ selectivity in FeSi-H₂ and FeSi-syn suggests suppressed WGS due to the presence of ε-Fe₂C.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - FeSi-CO: 19.2%
  - FeSi-H₂: 10.6% (as low as 9.3% under some conditions)
  - FeSi-syn: 12.9%
- **Observations and Trends:** The lowest CH₄ selectivity is observed for FeSi-H₂, which contains significant ε-Fe₂C, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon formation. Higher CH₄ selectivity in FeSi-CO is linked to Fe₇C₃ and χ-Fe₅C₂ phases.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the weight percentage of hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms among total hydrocarbons:
\[
\text{C₅⁺ Selectivity} = \frac{\text{mass of C₅⁺ hydrocarbons}}{\text{total mass of hydrocarbons}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - FeSi-CO: 50.0%
  - FeSi-H₂: 74.4% (up to 76.4% under some conditions)
  - FeSi-syn: 70.0%
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts with higher ε-Fe₂C content (FeSi-H₂) exhibit superior C₅⁺ selectivity, indicating a strong propensity for chain growth and long-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor denotes the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, with higher values favoring longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not directly reported, the high C₅⁺ selectivity (up to 76.4% for FeSi-H₂) implies a high α, especially for catalysts containing ε-Fe₂C.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced α is associated with ε-Fe₂C-rich catalysts, reflecting efficient C–C coupling and suppressed methane formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring the retention of CO conversion and selectivity over time, as well as the preservation of active phase composition.
- **Experimental Data:** No explicit deactivation rates are provided; however, the catalysts maintain stable performance under moderate carburization and FTS conditions, with phase analysis confirming the persistence of intended carbide phases.
- **Observations and Trends:** Stability is attributed to the use of silica support, controlled carburization, and optimal reaction temperature, which collectively prevent sintering and undesirable phase transitions.

#### 6.2.7 Turnover Frequency (TOF) and Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** TOF and FTY quantify the intrinsic activity of the catalyst:
  - TOF (s⁻¹): Number of CO molecules converted per surface iron atom per second.
  - FTY (μmolCO/gFe/s): Moles of CO converted per gram of iron per second.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - Fe₇C₃: TOF = 4.59 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ (highest among phases)
  - FeSi-CO: FTY = 16.5 μmolCO/gFe/s
- **Observations and Trends:** The FeSi-CO catalyst, rich in Fe₇C₃, demonstrates the highest intrinsic activity, while ε-Fe₂C and χ-Fe₅C₂ show lower TOF values.
