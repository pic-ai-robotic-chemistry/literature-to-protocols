## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines the catalyst synthesis methods as reported in the article for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts. Two primary approaches are described: a hydrothermal synthesis route with in situ silica encapsulation and, for comparison, a conventional incipient wetness impregnation method.

#### Hydrothermal Synthesis Followed by Silica Encapsulation

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - • Reagents: Cobalt acetate and TTAB are dissolved in ethanol with ammonia added to adjust the pH to 8.  
   - • Equipment: A 150 mL Teflon‑lined autoclave is utilized.  
   - • Observations: Formation of a homogeneous solution intended for nucleation.

2. Hydrothermal Preparation  
   - • Conditions: The sealed autoclave is heated at 150 °C.  
   - • Duration: Varied synthesis times (4 h for Cat‑4h, 8 h for Cat‑8h, and 12 h for Cat‑12h) control the cobalt crystallite size.  
   - • Outcome: TTAB‑capped Co₃O₄ nanocrystals with uniform size and cubic morphology are obtained, as confirmed by TEM and XRD.

3. Precipitation–Deposition (Silica Encapsulation)  
   - • Process: The suspension containing TTAB‑capped nanocrystals is mixed with 185 mL ethanol and 185 mL deionized water.  
   - • Addition: A TTAB solution containing TEOS is added dropwise.  
   - • Conditions: The mixture is maintained under vigorous stirring and refluxed at 60 °C for 15 h to facilitate the in situ formation of a silica‑TTAB layer.
   
4. Drying  
   - • Procedure: After centrifugation, the silica‑encapsulated composite is dried at 110 °C for 12 h, removing excess solvent.

5. Calcination  
   - • Details: The dried composite is calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 h (ramping at 5 °C min⁻¹), effectively removing TTAB and stabilizing the confined catalyst structure.

#### Conventional Incipient Wetness Impregnation

1. Precursor Mixing  
   - • Process: Mesoporous SiO₂ is impregnated with a cobalt nitrate solution.
   
2. Drying and Calcination  
   - • Conditions: The impregnated support is dried at 80 °C overnight and subsequently calcined at 400 °C for 3 h.  
   
• Comparative Analysis:  
   - The hydrothermal route offers precise control over cobalt crystallite size and enhanced dispersion due to the encapsulation step, whereas the incipient wetness method is simpler but may result in catalyst aggregation. This structured synthesis ensures reproducibility and improved catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Cat‑xh (Hydrothermal Route with Silica Encapsulation)

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 1.17 g cobalt acetate  
  • 4.26 g tetradecyltrimethylammonium bromide (TTAB)  
  • 100 mL ethanol  
  • Ammonia solution (to adjust pH to 8)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Stirring at 30 °C with vigorous agitation  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve cobalt acetate and TTAB in 100 mL ethanol at 30 °C.  
  2. Add ammonia dropwise until the pH reaches 8.  
  3. Ensure complete mixing to form a homogeneous precursor solution.  
- **Key Steps**: Control of pH and stirring to promote uniform reagent distribution.

##### Step [2]: Hydrothermal Synthesis  
- **Reagents**: Precursor solution from Step 1  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Transfer the mixture to a Teflon-lined autoclave  
  • Seal and heat at 150 °C for a controlled duration (4, 8, or 12 hours) to tune Co₃O₄ nanocrystal size  
- **Process**:  
  1. Introduce the solution into the autoclave and perform the hydrothermal reaction.  
  2. The reaction duration controls crystallite size: longer heating leads to larger particles.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain constant temperature, secure autoclave integrity, and enforce precise timing.

##### Step [3]: Silica Encapsulation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 20 mL tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS)  
  • A mixture of ethanol (185 mL) and deionized water (185 mL)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Reflux with continuous stirring at 60 °C  
- **Process**:  
  1. Add TEOS dropwise to the suspension of TTAB-capped Co₃O₄ nanocrystals.  
  2. Reflux the mixture at 60 °C for 15 hours to form a uniform in situ silica-TTAB layer.  
- **Key Steps**: Controlled dropwise addition and maintaining consistent reflux conditions ensure effective encapsulation.

##### Step [4]: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Purification**:  
  • Retrieve the nanocomposite by centrifugation.  
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry at 110 °C for 12 hours in a controlled oven.  
- **Calcination**:  
  • Ramp temperature at 5 °C/min to 400 °C, then hold for 4 hours in air to remove residual TTAB, finalizing the mesoporous support.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor temperature, time, and atmosphere to preserve catalyst morphology and prevent cobalt aggregation.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD analysis was performed to determine the crystalline phases (FCC and HCP) and estimate crystallite sizes using Scherrer’s equation on the most intense (311) peak.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Philips X’Pert Pro diffractometer with monochromatized Co Kα radiation (λ = 1.78897 Å).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Scanned over 20°–80° at a speed of 6°/min.  
- **Test Parameters**: Step size set to secure accurate peak resolution.  
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment is critical for precise phase determination.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: The reduction behavior of cobalt species was evaluated via a programmed temperature increase after sample pretreatment.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Micro fixed-bed reactor coupled with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Sample pretreated by reduction at 400 °C for 10 hours in H₂, evacuated at 400 °C for 1 hour in N₂; then, 8 vol.% H₂ in N₂ was introduced at 30 mL/min from room temperature to 900 °C with a ramp of 10 °C/min.  
- **Test Parameters**: Distinct peaks indicate surface, bulk, and deeply embedded Co species.  
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading is essential to differentiate reduction peaks.

### 4.3 H₂‑Chemisorption
- **Operational Description**: Used to quantify metal dispersion by measuring H₂ uptake.  
- **Procedure**: Catalyst reduced at 400 °C for 10 hours in H₂, evacuated, then titrated at 100 °C.  
- **Key Notes**: Evaluates both uptake and reduction degree for accurate dispersion determination.

### 4.4 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & HRTEM
- **Operational Description**: TEM (JEM-2100F) images were recorded to observe particle morphology and crystallite size distribution (200 counts per sample).  
- **Key Notes**: HRTEM confirms the uniform “water‑melon–like” embedding of nanocrystals in mesoporous silica.

### 4.5 Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS)
- **Operational Description**: DRIFTS examined CO adsorption features on the catalyst surface.  
- **Test Parameters**: Spectra acquired in a 650–4000 cm⁻¹ range, 2 cm⁻¹ resolution with 32 scans accumulated.  
- **Key Notes**: Monitors band shifts corresponding to cobalt oxidation state changes.

### 4.6 CO‑Temperature‑Programmed Desorption/Mass Spectrometry (CO‑TPD/MS)
- **Operational Description**: Evaluates CO* adsorption strength by tracking desorbed CO (m/z = 28).  
- **Procedure**: After reduction (400 °C in H₂ for 10 h) and He purging (30 mL/min, 1 h), the sample was heated from RT to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.

### 4.7 Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP)
- **Operational Description**: Determines the cobalt mass fraction (~15%).  
- **Key Notes**: Specific operating conditions were not detailed.

### 4.8 In‑situ X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
- **Operational Description**: Assesses cobalt oxidation states during thermal treatment in a 10 vol.% H₂/N₂ mixture.  
- **Instruments and Models**: PHI 5000 Versa Probe using an Al Kα source (1486.6 eV) at 25 W.  
- **Procedure**: Sample heated at 400 °C for 10 hours before cooling.

### 4.9 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms (Quantachrome Quadrasorb SI) were used for surface area and porosity measurements at −197 °C across a P/Po range of 0.05–0.30.  
- **Key Notes**: Total pore volume obtained at P/Po = 0.99.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is crucial for converting cobalt oxide precursors into metallic cobalt, which forms the active sites for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** All catalysts undergo pre‐reaction activation at 400 °C. This temperature is precisely maintained in a fixed-bed reactor during the reduction phase.
- **Observations:** Activation at 400 °C promotes uniform reduction and dispersion of cobalt, with the confinement effect from the silica support helping to control sintering. In contrast, catalysts prepared by incipient wetness impregnation exhibit cobalt migration and particle growth under similar conditions.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure can affect the kinetics of gas-catalyst interactions during reduction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** No explicit pressure settings are provided for the pre‐reaction activation beyond ambient conditions. Although the Fischer–Tropsch reaction is performed at 2 MPa, the activation step does not specify a particular pressure.
- **Observations:** The lack of detailed pressure control during activation suggests that the focus is on temperature and gas composition to drive the reduction process.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The reducing gas is essential to facilitate the transition of cobalt oxide to its metallic form.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen is used as the activation gas during the reduction process.
- **Observations:** Utilizing pure hydrogen ensures an efficient reduction of cobalt oxide into metallic cobalt without introducing effects such as carburization, thereby directly supporting catalyst performance in the subsequent reaction.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity influences the exposure and contact of catalyst particles with the activation gas.
- **Experimental Conditions:** No explicit value (e.g., in mL/(gcat·h)) has been provided for the catalyst activation step.
- **Observations:** As this parameter is more relevant to reaction conditions, the procedure emphasizes maintaining a pure hydrogen environment during activation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process is critical to achieve complete reduction while avoiding catalyst overexposure that may lead to sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation time is fixed at 10 hours for all catalyst samples.
- **Observations:** A uniform 10-hour hydrogen reduction treatment ensures reproducible activation across all variants (Cat‑4h, Cat‑8h, Cat‑12h), contributing to controlled particle size and enhanced catalytic stability.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is a key parameter that influences the activation of syngas by enhancing the adsorption and conversion of CO and H₂ on the active cobalt sites. It also plays a vital role in controlling the rate of hydrocarbon chain growth and the distribution of products.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch catalytic tests were conducted at a fixed temperature of 220 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 220 °C ensures effective catalyst activation while preventing excessive sintering of cobalt crystallites. This temperature supports a steady state during the reaction, maintaining the catalyst’s surface area and activity, thereby contributing to both high conversion rates and stability over extended operation times.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure is responsible for influencing the concentration of reactants in the gas phase, which in turn affects the kinetics of the reaction and the overall efficiency of the catalytic process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was carried out in a stainless steel fixed-bed reactor at a pressure of 2 MPa.
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure of 2 MPa enhances the partial pressures of CO and H₂, which not only accelerates the reaction rates but also promotes the formation and growth of heavier hydrocarbon chains. This condition is critical for achieving optimal catalytic performance and improving product selectivity, particularly toward longer chain hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio in the syngas feed determines the hydrogen availability relative to carbon monoxide, directly affecting the chain propagation and termination steps in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The feed gas composition was defined with a CO/H₂ ratio of 1/2, which corresponds to an H₂/CO ratio of 2.
- **Observations:** This specified ratio ensures an optimal balance in the hydrogenation steps, facilitating the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons while controlling the over-hydrogenation that may lead to increased methane formation. The chosen syngas composition has been demonstrated to provide reproducible results, ensuring both catalyst durability and consistent product distribution in future evaluations.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Crystallite Size
- **Description:** The crystal structure and average crystallite size of cobalt phases were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), with crystallite sizes calculated using the Scherrer equation based on the (311) diffraction peak of Co₃O₄ and corrected for metallic cobalt after reduction.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the reduced Cat-xh catalysts reveal the coexistence of strong face-centered cubic (FCC, JCPDS 15-0806) and weaker hexagonal close-packed (HCP, JCPDS 05-0727) metallic cobalt phases. The average crystallite sizes of metallic cobalt (d(Co⁰)ˣ) are 6.5 nm for Cat-4h and 10.2 nm for Cat-12h, with intermediate values for Cat-8h. The crystallite size increases with longer hydrothermal synthesis durations.
- **Observations:** The precise control of crystallite size via hydrothermal duration and silica encapsulation is critical for tuning catalytic properties, with smaller crystallites favoring diesel-range selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were used to examine the morphology, size distribution, and dispersion of cobalt nanocrystals within the silica matrix.
- **Findings:** TEM images show that cobalt nanocrystals are uniformly dispersed and embedded within the mesoporous silica, forming a distinctive "water-melon–like" structure. The silica encapsulation effectively prevents cobalt aggregation, maintaining a narrow particle size distribution before and after Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Observations:** The spatial confinement provided by mesoporous SiO₂ ensures high dispersion and stability of cobalt nanocrystals, in contrast to conventional impregnated catalysts (Cat-IM), which exhibit significant aggregation after reaction.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were employed to determine the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and porosity of the catalysts.
- **Findings:** The Cat-xh catalysts exhibit high surface areas of 320–331 m²/g and pore volumes of 0.22–0.23 cm³/g, characteristic of mesoporous materials.
- **Observations:** The large surface area and mesoporosity facilitate good dispersion and accessibility of active cobalt sites, contributing to enhanced catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** H₂-temperature programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was used to assess the reducibility and distribution of cobalt species.
- **Findings:** Multiple reduction peaks are observed: Peaks I and II (below 400 °C) correspond to surface and bulk Co₃O₄ reduction, while Peak III (higher temperature) is attributed to cobalt species deeply embedded in silica. The Cat-xh catalysts show a larger proportion of deeply embedded cobalt compared to Cat-IM.
- **Observations:** The embedment structure, confirmed by the prominence of Peak III, reflects the effectiveness of silica confinement in controlling cobalt accessibility and reduction behavior.

#### 6.1.5 Metal Dispersion and Particle Size by H₂-Chemisorption
- **Description:** H₂-chemisorption measurements were conducted to quantify cobalt metal dispersion and accessible particle size (d(Co⁰)ᴴ).
- **Findings:** The Cat-xh catalysts display higher metal dispersion and smaller accessible particle sizes compared to Cat-IM, consistent with the uniformity observed in TEM and XRD analyses.
- **Observations:** Enhanced dispersion and smaller particle size are directly linked to the spatial confinement effect of the silica matrix.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Composition and Oxidation State
- **Description:** In situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) were used to determine the oxidation state and coordination environment of cobalt.
- **Findings:** After reduction, cobalt exists predominantly in the metallic state in Cat-xh catalysts. EXAFS analysis indicates lower Co–Co coordination numbers than bulk Co, confirming high dispersion.
- **Observations:** The metallic state and high dispersion of cobalt are essential for optimal Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.7 CO Adsorption and Surface Reactivity
- **Description:** In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) and CO-temperature programmed desorption/mass spectrometry (CO-TPD/MS) were used to probe CO adsorption characteristics.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS reveals red shifts in CO adsorption bands as cobalt crystallite size decreases, indicating stronger CO–cobalt interactions. CO-TPD/MS shows higher desorption temperatures and quantities for smaller crystallites, reflecting stronger C* adsorption.
- **Observations:** Stronger CO and C* adsorption on smaller cobalt nanocrystals enhances chain growth probability, influencing product selectivity toward heavier hydrocarbons.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO in the feed and effluent, respectively. Nitrogen is used as an internal standard for accurate quantification.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (220 °C, 2 MPa, CO/H₂ = 1/2, W/F = 5.1 g_cat h mol⁻¹), the CO conversion values are:
  - Cat‑4h: 77.0%
  - Cat‑8h: 78.2%
  - Cat‑12h: 80.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing the hydrothermal synthesis duration (and thus cobalt crystallite size) leads to a modest rise in CO conversion. The confined catalysts maintain high conversion rates, with Cat‑12h achieving the highest value.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Reported CH₄ selectivity values are:
  - Cat‑4h: 8.0%
  - Cat‑8h: 10.4%
  - Cat‑12h: 8.7%
  - Cat‑IM (reference): 18.0%
- **Observations and Trends:** The confined Cat‑xh catalysts exhibit significantly lower CH₄ selectivity compared to the impregnated reference (Cat‑IM), indicating that spatial confinement and controlled crystallite size suppress methane formation.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity denotes the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of heavy hydrocarbon production.
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivity values are:
  - Cat‑4h: 84.2%
  - Cat‑8h: 80.7%
  - Cat‑12h: 74.6%
  - Cat‑IM: 67.3%
- **Observations and Trends:** Smaller, more deeply embedded cobalt crystallites (Cat‑4h) favor higher C₅⁺ selectivity, while larger crystallites (Cat‑12h) shift product distribution toward lighter hydrocarbons. The reference catalyst shows the lowest C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The chain growth probability (α) reflects the likelihood of hydrocarbon chain propagation versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model:
  \[
  W_n = n \cdot \alpha^{n-1} \cdot (1 - \alpha)^2
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** The α factor decreases with increasing cobalt crystallite size:
  - Cat‑4h: 0.85
  - Cat‑8h: 0.80
  - Cat‑12h: 0.73
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values in smaller, confined cobalt crystallites correspond to enhanced selectivity for heavier hydrocarbons, demonstrating a reversal of conventional ASF trends due to spatial confinement.

#### 6.2.5 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF measures the number of CO molecules converted per surface cobalt atom per second, indicating intrinsic catalytic activity.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - Cat‑4h: 0.039 s⁻¹
  - Cat‑12h: 0.064 s⁻¹
