## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis is achieved via a reverse precipitation method that emphasizes accurate pH control and sequential addition of reagents to ensure reproducibility and performance. Below is a step-by-step breakdown of the experimental procedure:

#### Reverse Precipitation Method Procedure

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₂·9H₂O (1 M) in 500 mL of deionized water.  
   • Immediately add the non-metal promoter solution (e.g., ammonium sulphate at 1.0 M, with promoter loadings ranging between 0.4–5 mmol) to ensure its incorporation into the catalyst structure.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • Begin pH adjustment by slowly introducing NH₄OH (25 wt.%) to reach a target pH of approximately 6; note the initial reagent temperature is around 16°C, which then increases to about 27°C during the process.  
   • Add Na₂CO₃ powder dropwise to slightly raise the pH to around 6.3. This sequential addition not only helps achieve the optimum Na:S mole ratio (at least 2:1 with sodium in the range of 8–26 mmol and sulphur between 3–4.5 mmol) but also promotes uniform precipitation as indicated by observable color changes and precipitate formation.

3. **Drying**  
   • Transfer the precipitate to an oven and dry at 150°C overnight to remove moisture and stabilize the structure.

4. **Calcination**  
   • Calcine the dried material at 350°C for 4 hours in a muffle oven. This process decomposes any residual precursors and develops the active catalyst sites.

5. **Post‑processing**  
   • Crush and sieve the calcined catalyst to a particle size between 38–150 µm to ensure proper bed packing during catalytic testing.  
   • Prior to catalytic evaluation, a reduction step is performed in situ at 420°C under 20 bar H₂ for 16 hours.

### Comparative Analysis  
The reverse precipitation method offers lower synthesis complexity and reduced equipment demands compared to conventional co‑precipitation methods. Its precise pH control and sequential promoter addition allow for effective incorporation of both non‑metal and alkali promoters, making it a cost-effective and scalable option for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst production.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed into products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (330 °C, 20 bar, H₂:CO = 4:1, GHSV = 12.9 L(n) g-cat⁻¹ h⁻¹), the reference iron catalyst promoted with sodium and sulphur achieved a CO conversion of approximately 40%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing sodium and sulphur promoter loadings enhances C₂–C₄ olefin selectivity but reduces CO conversion. Optimal performance is observed at a Na:S mole ratio of at least 2:1 (Na: 8–26 mmol, S: 3–4.5 mmol), balancing conversion and selectivity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water-gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity was determined via online GC analysis. While specific numerical values are not detailed, trends are reported.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher promoter loadings (sodium and sulphur) decrease CO₂ selectivity, indicating suppressed water-gas shift activity and more selective hydrocarbon formation. Lower promoter levels increase both CO conversion and CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The sodium and sulphur promoted catalyst exhibited a methane selectivity of approximately 13% under optimal conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane selectivity is minimized at a Na:S ratio of at least 2:1. Lower sodium or higher sulphur increases methane formation, while optimal ratios suppress it, deviating from the typical Schulz–Flory distribution.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–C₄ Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** C₂–C₄ olefin selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming C₂–C₄ olefins, a key performance indicator for light olefin production.
- **Experimental Data:** The optimized catalyst achieved up to 68% selectivity to C₂–C₄ olefins, with grouped C₂–C₄ selectivity around 55%.
- **Observations and Trends:** This selectivity exceeds the maximum predicted by the Schulz–Flory model, highlighting the catalyst’s unique ability to favor olefin production over methane and paraffins.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of hydrocarbon chain growth versus termination, derived from fitting product distributions to the Schulz–Flory model.
- **Experimental Data:** The catalyst’s product distribution deviates from the ideal Schulz–Flory correlation, with lower α values favoring C₂–C₄ olefins and reduced methane.
- **Observations and Trends:** Sodium and sulphur promotion at optimal ratios lowers α, enhancing light olefin selectivity and suppressing methane formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability refers to the maintenance of activity and selectivity over time. While a formal deactivation rate is not reported, qualitative stability is discussed.
- **Experimental Data:** The catalyst demonstrated promising activity and selectivity suitable for further optimization, with no significant deactivation observed during the reported evaluation period.
- **Observations and Trends:** The combination of sodium and sulphur not only tunes selectivity but also supports stable performance under the tested conditions.
