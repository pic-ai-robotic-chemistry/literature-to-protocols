## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details two synthesis methods applied for the FeMnK@H‐S‐1(x) catalyst: the primary “desilication–recrystallization” method and the conventional impregnation method for comparison. Both methods are reported with precise conditions to ensure reproducibility and controlled pore formation.

#### Desilication–Recrystallization Method
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Mix 4.5 g of the FeMnK/Silicalite-1 composite with 30 mL of a mixed alkaline solution comprising TPAOH and NaOH. Maintain a total OH⁻ concentration of 0.56 M, while varying NaOH fraction (x = 0.00, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50, 0.67, 0.80) to regulate mesopore formation.  
   • Equipment: Standard stirring apparatus and pH monitoring with deionized water washes ensure neutrality.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • Immediately following mixing, stir the slurry at 25 °C for 3 h to facilitate even dispersion and initial deposition of metal oxides.  
   • Transfer the mixture to an autoclave and heat at 170 °C for 24 h. This step allows controlled precipitation–deposition on the zeolite framework as observed by the transition to a brown solid.

3. **Drying and Calcination**  
   • Recover the product by centrifugation and wash until a natural pH is reached.  
   • Dry the washed sample overnight at 80 °C.  
   • Calcinate in static air at 450 °C for 6 h to solidify the structure and lock in the hierarchical porosity.

#### Impregnation Method (Reference Catalyst)
1. **Precursor Mixing**  
   • Impregnate the silicalite-1 support with aqueous solutions of iron, manganese, and potassium nitrates (using defined concentrations as reported).  
   • Stir at 80 °C to facilitate uniform distribution.

2. **Drying and Calcination**  
   • Dry the impregnated support overnight at 80 °C.  
   • Calcinate at 400 °C for 4 h, providing a contrast in thermal treatment compared to the desilication method.

Both methods report precise numerical values and conditions—temperatures in °C, durations in hours, and fixed reagent concentrations—to ensure reproducibility. The specific sequence, particularly the integration of the Precipitation–Deposition step prior to drying and calcination, is critical for preserving the zeolite structure while optimizing the formation of mesopores and the encapsulation of FeMnK oxides.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 FeMnK@H‐S‐1(x)

- **Final Mixture**: The homogeneous synthesis gel is then transferred for subsequent autoclave treatment, supporting the formation of the hierarchical zeolite structure.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: FeMnK@H‐S‐1(x)

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Iron nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O), manganese nitrate (Mn(NO₃)₂, 50 wt%), and potassium nitrate (KNO₃) impregnated onto silicalite-1. For example, ~1 g of the FeMnK/Silicalite-1 composite is combined with 2 mL of nitrate solution per precursor.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Disperse 4.5 g of the composite in 30 mL of a mixed alkaline solution composed of TPAOH and NaOH. The total OH⁻ concentration is maintained at 0.56 M, with the NaOH molar fraction (x) varied as required.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Thoroughly disperse the FeMnK/Silicalite-1 composite in the alkaline solution.  
  2. Stir the mixture at 25 °C for 3 hours to ensure complete dissolution of precursors and proper incorporation of alkaline media.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Strict control of the OH⁻ concentration and NaOH/TPAOH ratio to modulate pore formation.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly and Desilication  
- **Reagents**:  
  • The prepared precursor mixture.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Transfer the stirred dispersion to an autoclave.  
- **Process**:  
  1. Heat the autoclave at 170 °C for 24 hours, allowing desilication–recrystallization to occur and generating hierarchical pore structures.  
  2. Monitor the reaction to maintain temperature consistency.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensuring uniform heat distribution to maintain the integrity of the zeolite framework.

##### Step [3]: Purification  
- **Process**:  
  1. Recover the solid product by centrifugation at appropriate speeds.  
  2. Wash repeatedly with deionized water until a natural, neutral pH is achieved.

##### Step [4]: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry the product overnight at 80 °C using a convection oven.  
- **Calcination**:  
  • Calcine at 450 °C for 6 hours (or at 400 °C for 4 hours for comparative reference catalysts) to remove residuals and stabilize the structure.

##### Step [5]: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • Optionally reduce the catalyst under a 20% H₂/80% N₂ gas mixture at 400 °C for 6 hours to activate the metallic sites.

Throughout the workflow, key parameters such as pH, stirring duration, and temperature are meticulously controlled to ensure reproducibility and optimal meso- and micropore formation essential for enhanced catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to assess the structural integrity of the zeolite support and to identify crystalline phases of the encapsulated metal oxides. This analysis is crucial for confirming the preservation of the MFI (silicalite-1) framework and detecting the presence of active iron oxide phases.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns demonstrate that the characteristic peaks of the MFI structure are retained in FeMnK@H‐S‐1(x) catalysts after alkali treatment, indicating successful preservation of the zeolite framework. However, relative crystallinity decreases as the NaOH molar fraction (x) increases, reflecting partial framework degradation at higher x values. Diffraction peaks between 30° and 60° (2θ) correspond to Fe₂O₃ (JCPDS 25-1402), confirming the presence of iron oxide. No distinct peaks for manganese are observed, likely due to high dispersion or overlap with iron oxide signals.
- **Observations:** The maintenance of the MFI structure ensures shape selectivity, while the presence of Fe₂O₃ provides active sites for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. Increasing x leads to reduced crystallinity, which correlates with changes in catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Pore Structure
- **Description:** N₂ adsorption/desorption (BET and BJH analyses) was used to quantify surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, providing insight into the hierarchical porosity critical for mass transfer and product selectivity.
- **Findings:** BET measurements at −196 °C reveal that the total surface area (SBET), mesopore surface area (Smeso), and pore volume (Vtotal) increase with x up to 0.20, then decrease at higher x due to excessive desilication and macropore formation. Mesopores (12–17 nm) emerge at x = 0.10–0.50, while macropores (up to 130 nm) appear at x ≥ 0.67. The hierarchy factor, representing the balance between micropores and mesopores, peaks at x = 0.20.
- **Observations:** Optimal mesopore formation at x = 0.20 enhances mass transfer and suppresses secondary reactions, directly supporting the observed volcano-type trend in catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM) provided detailed visualization of catalyst morphology, shell thickness, and metal oxide nanoparticle distribution.
- **Findings:** SEM images show the evolution from microporous to mesoporous and macroporous shells as x increases. TEM analysis reveals a hollow silicalite-1 shell with thicknesses of 10–40 nm. Metal oxide nanoparticles are encapsulated within the shell, with mean diameters of 8.2 nm (x = 0.00), 9.7 nm (x = 0.20), and 10.0 nm (x = 0.80), indicating consistent particle size across samples.
- **Observations:** The encapsulation of nanoparticles within the zeolite shell is confirmed, and the controlled shell thickness and particle size distribution contribute to catalyst stability and selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Surface Analysis
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray fluorescence (XRF) were used to determine surface and bulk elemental composition, as well as oxidation states of active metals.
- **Findings:** XPS detects surface Fe at ~1.0 wt% (x < 0.50), much lower than the bulk Fe content (8.0 wt%, XRF), confirming encapsulation. Fe 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 peaks for Fe³⁺ (Fe₂O₃) are observed at 711.0 and 724.4 eV, respectively, with a shakeup satellite at 719.0 eV. The Fe 2p3/2 binding energy for FeMnK@H-S-1(0.00) is 711.2 eV. Mn 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 peaks are at 640.6–642.0 and 652.2–653.6 eV, respectively. Residual Na is detected and acts as an electronic promoter.
- **Observations:** The low surface Fe content supports the core–shell architecture, while the presence of residual Na influences hydrogen adsorption properties.

#### 6.1.5 Reducibility and Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** H₂-temperature programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and H₂/C₂H₄-TPD-MS pulse experiments were conducted to probe the reduction behavior and olefin adsorption/desorption characteristics.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR profiles show that reduction peaks shift to lower temperatures at x = 0.20, indicating easier reduction due to optimal mesopore formation. At higher x, peaks shift to higher temperatures, reflecting stronger metal-support interactions. H₂/C₂H₄-TPD-MS experiments reveal that catalysts with optimal mesopore structure (x = 0.20) exhibit lower ethylene desorption energy and suppressed secondary hydrogenation, as evidenced by a higher ethylene/ethane ratio in the exit gas.
- **Observations:** These results confirm that hierarchical porosity enhances reducibility and product desorption, minimizing undesired secondary reactions and supporting high light olefin selectivity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (280 °C, 0.5 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.9, 4000 h⁻¹), CO conversion for FeMnK@H‐S‐1(x) increases from approximately 7.5% at \( x = 0.00 \) to a maximum of 13.9% at \( x = 0.20 \), then decreases with further NaOH addition.
- **Observations and Trends:** The CO conversion exhibits a volcano-type dependence on the NaOH molar fraction (x), peaking at \( x = 0.20 \). This trend correlates with the optimal development of mesopores, which enhance mass transfer and accessibility to active sites.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity increases from 16.2% for FeMnK@H‐S‐1(0.00) to 38.6% for FeMnK@H‐S‐1(0.20), remaining relatively constant at higher x values.
- **Observations and Trends:** The rise in CO₂ selectivity with increasing x up to 0.20 suggests enhanced water–gas shift activity, likely due to improved accessibility of iron oxide sites within the hierarchical structure.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO resulting in methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity is minimized at \( x = 0.20 \), corresponding to the catalyst with optimal mesopore content.
- **Observations and Trends:** The suppression of CH₄ formation at \( x = 0.20 \) is attributed to moderate hydrogen activation, which favors olefin production over excessive hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The FeMnK@H‐S‐1(0.20) catalyst achieves the highest light olefins (C₂–C₄) selectivity at 50.6%, with a corresponding decrease in C₃⁺ selectivity at higher or lower x values.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal mesopore formation at \( x = 0.20 \) enhances light olefins selectivity and suppresses the formation of heavier hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model, indicates the probability of chain propagation versus termination. Higher α values favor longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the product distribution and selectivity trends imply that the α factor is tuned by the hierarchy factor, with lower α at \( x = 0.20 \) favoring light olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** The balance of micro- and mesoporosity at \( x = 0.20 \) modulates the α factor, maximizing light olefins yield.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time; the deactivation rate is defined as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Specific deactivation rates are not reported; however, the hierarchical structure at \( x = 0.20 \) is associated with sustained performance and minimal secondary reactions, as evidenced by pulse experiments.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal pore architecture at \( x = 0.20 \) not only enhances initial activity and selectivity but also contributes to improved catalyst stability by suppressing secondary hydrogenation and facilitating product desorption.
