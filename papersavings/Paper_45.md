## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

#### Hot Injection Synthesis via Chemie Douce Route

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: Octadecylamine (serving simultaneously as solvent and surfactant) and cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) are combined. CTAB acts as the bromide-inducing agent, critical for enhancing nanoparticle crystallinity and oxidation resistance.  
   - Equipment: A four-neck flask reactor equipped with temperature and inert gas (N₂ or Ar) control.  
   - Process: The mixture is thoroughly stirred and degassed for 1 hour to eliminate oxygen and moisture, ensuring an inert atmosphere.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Reagent Addition: Fe(CO)₅ is injected into the hot, degassed mixture under a continuous flow of inert gas.
   - Temperature Conditions: The reaction mixture is initially heated to a temperature range of 393–453 K. At approximately 453 K, Fe(CO)₅ decomposes, as indicated by a distinct color change from orange to black signifying nanoparticle nucleation.
   - Observations: The rapid formation of Fe nanoparticles occurs with visible precipitate formation. Proper dispersion is ensured by the dual function of octadecylamine.

3. • Post‑processing: Drying and Pyrolysis  
   - Temperature Ramp: Following deposition, the temperature is increased to 623 K for an additional 10 minutes. This step facilitates the catalytic dehydrogenation of octadecylamine and the pyrolysis of long‑chain organonitriles, which deposits carbon to transform the initially amorphous Fe particles into crystalline Fe₅C₂.  
   - Note: In the absence of CTAB, the reaction yields amorphous and oxidation‑prone Fe particles, underscoring the bromide’s role.
   - Subsequent Steps: The reaction mixture is cooled under inert conditions, then washed with ethanol and hexane to remove unreacted precursors and byproducts before drying.

### Comparative Analysis  
- Advantages: The chemie douce route offers lower reaction temperatures, simplified reactor setup, and cost‑efficiency compared to traditional high‑temperature synthesis methods.  
- Scalability: The one‑pot, hot injection strategy ensures high reproducibility and ease of scaling up.  
- Equipment & Operational Ease: Utilization of standard laboratory multi‑neck flasks with precise temperature and gas control minimizes oxidation and ensures controlled nanoparticle nucleation and growth.  

This structured procedure enables reproducibility and highlights the critical parameters required for synthesizing well‑crystallized Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles for Fischer–Tropsch applications.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the reagent preparation is described for each catalyst type, ensuring clear, reproducible steps tailored to the specific synthesis of Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles and their supported counterpart.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe₅C₂ Nanoparticles (Fe₅C₂ NPs)
- **Octadecylamine (C₁₈H₃₉N)**: Weighed 14.5g using an analytical balance.
- **Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, C₂₁H₃₈BrN)**: Weighed 0.113g using an analytical balance.

##### 3.1.1.2 Supported Fe₅C₂/SiO₂ Catalyst
- **Fe₅C₂ Nanoparticles**: Weighed 80mg for dispersion prior to impregnation onto the SiO₂ support.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe₅C₂ Nanoparticles (Fe₅C₂ NPs)
- **Fe(CO)₅**: Injected 0.5mL using a syringe under an inert N₂ atmosphere to ensure controlled delivery into the heated reaction mixture.
- **Octadecylamine (liquid phase component)**: Transferred by pouring the required volume into the four-neck flask, as part of the solvent–surfactant system.

##### 3.1.2.2 Supported Fe₅C₂/SiO₂ Catalyst
- **Ethanol**: Poured 10mL into a beaker to create a homogeneous medium for dispersing the pre-synthesized Fe₅C₂ NPs prior to the impregnation onto the SiO₂ support.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe₅C₂ Nanoparticles (Fe₅C₂ NPs)
- **Octadecylamine/CTAB Mixture**: Mixed by stirring the precursors at approximately 393K under an inert atmosphere to allow efficient degassing. This controlled stirring ensures the reagents are uniformly distributed prior to the Fe(CO)₅ injection.
- **Post-Injection Mixture**: Ultrasonically treated for a defined duration to achieve complete homogenization, facilitating consistent nanoparticle formation.

##### 3.1.3.2 Supported Fe₅C₂/SiO₂ Catalyst
- **Fe₅C₂ Dispersion in Ethanol**: Mixed by stirring and subsequently ultrasonicated to ensure thorough dispersion of the nanoparticles, which promotes consistent impregnation onto the SiO₂ support.

This catalyst-specific grouping of weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing steps under clearly defined conditions is critical for reproducibility and achieving the desired synthesis outcomes in Fischer–Tropsch experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₅C₂ Nanoparticles

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Fe(CO)₅ (iron carbonyl) – used as the primary iron precursor  
  • Octadecylamine – serving as both solvent and surfactant  
  • CTAB (0.113 g) – acting as the bromide-based inducing agent  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Inert atmosphere maintained using N₂ (or Ar where specified)  
  • Reaction vessel: multi-neck flask reactor with controlled heating  
- **Process**:  
  • Degas a mixture of octadecylamine and CTAB under a continuous N₂ flow while stirring.  
  • Heat the solution to between 393 K and 453 K; this temperature range initiates the decomposition of Fe(CO)₅ when injected.  
  • Inject Fe(CO)₅ rapidly into the degassed and preheated mixture, ensuring immediate contact under inert conditions to prevent oxidation.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Strict temperature control and inert atmosphere are critical during the injection and subsequent reaction phases.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:  
  • The freshly nucleated Fe nanoparticles  
  • Additional reagents, if required, for catalyst support modifications (e.g., SiO₂ for supported catalysts)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • After injection, ramp the temperature to 623 K at a rate of 10 K/min and maintain for 10 minutes.  
- **Process**:  
  • The elevated temperature facilitates the catalytic dehydrogenation of octadecylamine and pyrolysis of organonitriles, transforming Fe nanoparticles into crystalline Fe₅C₂.  
  • For supported catalysts, subsequently disperse the Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles in ethanol and impregnate onto a SiO₂ matrix.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  • Purify by washing with ethanol and hexane to remove unreacted reagents and residual surfactants.  
  • Dry the purified nanoparticles at 60°C for 6 hours, followed by overnight drying at 120°C.  
  • Calcine the dried precursor with a ramp rate of 5°C/min up to 300°C under air flow.

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • For supported catalysts, perform reduction in H₂ at 653 K under 0.1 MPa for 16 hours prior to catalytic testing.

**Key Notes**:  
• The initial hot injection of Fe(CO)₅ under inert conditions is essential to nucleate Fe nanoparticles reliably.  
• The presence of CTAB is pivotal to enhance crystallinity and antioxidation properties, ensuring reproducibility of the synthesis procedure.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is a critical pretreatment step applied to ensure that the catalyst precursor is transformed into the active phase. For conventional hematite-derived catalysts, a distinct hydrogen reduction procedure is adopted prior to the reaction.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential for driving the reduction of Fe₂O₃ into metallic iron and subsequently converting it to the catalytically active iron carbide phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The supported Fe₂O₃ catalyst is activated at 653 K in a controlled environment.
- **Observations:** This elevated temperature facilitates complete reduction, ensuring that the precursor is fully converted into the desired active phases, which is critical for achieving optimal catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the kinetics of reduction as well as the uniformity of gas–catalyst interactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** During the reduction pretreatment, a low pressure of 0.1 MPa is maintained under a hydrogen atmosphere.
- **Observations:** The controlled pressure environment supports effective reduction while preventing excessive agglomeration of active sites, thereby enhancing the catalyst’s stability during reaction.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition used during activation governs the reduction pathway and the formation of the active phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen is employed as the reducing agent.
- **Observations:** A hydrogen-rich atmosphere is indispensable for the sequential reduction of Fe₂O₃ to metallic iron and its subsequent carburization into iron carbide. The specific use of hydrogen, rather than a mixed gas, ensures a clean reduction process.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is critical to achieve complete reduction without inducing detrimental sintering effects.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The hydrogen reduction treatment is conducted for 16 hours.
- **Observations:** The extended activation period is necessary to fully transform the hematite precursor, ensuring that the active phase forms consistently. Notably, Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles synthesized via the bromide-induced wet chemistry route bypass this pretreatment, proceeding directly to reaction without an induction period.

These carefully controlled activation parameters underscore the importance of precise catalyst pre-treatment for achieving reproducible Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, as it directly affects syngas activation and catalyst performance. In this study, the reactor temperature is ramped from 303 K to 543 K at a controlled rate of 3 K/min before being maintained at 543 K throughout the catalytic evaluation. At 543 K, Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles exhibit immediate activity, ensuring prompt conversion of syngas. This temperature not only enhances the conversion efficiency but also helps inhibit excessive catalyst deactivation by reducing the duration of exposure to lower temperatures where undesirable side reactions may occur. However, extended runs indicate that, despite maintained morphology, the catalyst experiences partial oxidation due to interactions with water and CO₂ byproducts.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Reaction pressure is vital for maintaining the requisite syngas concentration. The experiments are carried out at a fixed pressure of 3 MPa, which supports consistent adsorption of CO and H₂ on the catalyst surface. The elevated pressure is also observed to favor the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺), thereby improving overall product selectivity.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The feed gas composition is detailed as 32% CO, 63% H₂, and 5% Ar, with the inert argon acting as an internal standard for calculating CO conversion. This composition leads to an implied H₂/CO ratio of approximately 1.97. A careful balance of these gases is crucial, as a higher hydrogen concentration generally favors the formation of light olefins over methane.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Gas Hourly Space Velocity (GHSV)
The reactor operates at a gas hourly space velocity of 15,000 cm³/(g_cat·h). This parameter determines the contact time between the reactants and the catalyst. A high GHSV ensures rapid mass transfer and minimizes the residence time of reactants, which is key to maintaining the catalyst's activity and controlling the product distribution.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phase and structural integrity of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation. This analysis is critical for confirming the formation of the active Fe₅C₂ phase, which is essential for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) activity.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the synthesized nanoparticles display reflections matching JCPDS no. 36-1248, confirming the presence of well-crystallized Fe₅C₂ as the dominant phase. Minor reflections at 33° and 62° indicate the presence of Fe₃O₄ as a surface oxidation product. In control experiments without bromide, the products were amorphous and easily oxidized, lacking the characteristic Fe₅C₂ peaks.
- **Observations:** The use of a bromide source (CTAB) during synthesis is crucial for achieving high crystallinity and phase purity of Fe₅C₂, which directly correlates with enhanced catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were employed to assess nanoparticle morphology, core–shell structure, and particle size distribution. These parameters influence catalyst dispersion and stability.
- **Findings:** TEM and HRTEM images reveal that the Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles possess a core–shell structure, with a well-crystallized Fe₅C₂ core and a thin amorphous or graphitic carbon shell. The average particle size is approximately 20 nm, as determined from the analysis of over 300 particles. The lattice spacing observed in HRTEM corresponds to the (510) plane of Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The presence of the carbon shell provides some protection against oxidation and sintering. Synthesis without bromide yields amorphous, less stable particles, underscoring the importance of controlled nucleation.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Composition and Chemical States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze surface elemental composition and oxidation states, which are critical for understanding catalyst reactivity and stability.
- **Findings:** XPS analysis shows a high surface carbon content (70.55%), oxygen (18.27%), and iron (9.66%). The Fe 2p spectrum exhibits peaks at ~710 and ~724 eV, characteristic of Fe₃O₄, and additional peaks at 707.0 and 719.9 eV, confirming the presence of Fe₅C₂. The C 1s peak at ~285 eV, along with Raman spectroscopy, indicates both amorphous and graphitic carbon on the surface. No bromide is detected on the catalyst surface.
- **Observations:** The surface carbon layer and minor Fe₃O₄ formation are consistent with the core–shell morphology and provide partial protection against deactivation. The absence of bromide on the surface confirms it does not act as a catalyst poison.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Carbon Structure
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy was utilized to characterize the nature of surface carbon, which can influence catalyst stability and resistance to sintering.
- **Findings:** Raman spectra display prominent D and G bands at 1328 cm⁻¹ and 1600 cm⁻¹, respectively, indicating the presence of both disordered (amorphous) and graphitic carbon.
- **Observations:** The graphitic carbon shell contributes to the antioxidation properties of the nanoparticles, supporting catalyst longevity under FTS conditions.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) conditions (543 K, 3 MPa, syngas: 32% CO, 63% H₂, 5% Ar; GHSV: 15,000 cm³ h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹), the Fe₅C₂/SiO₂ catalyst exhibited an initial CO conversion of 39%. This value decreased to 24% after 100 hours on stream.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe₅C₂ catalyst showed a rapid decline in CO conversion within the first 30 hours, followed by a more gradual decrease. In contrast, a conventional reduced-hematite catalyst displayed a lower and slightly increasing CO conversion (from 18% to 20%) over the same period. The Fe₅C₂ catalyst did not require an induction period and maintained higher intrinsic activity throughout the test.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** While specific numerical values are not detailed, product analysis confirmed that CO₂ selectivity was measured and used to assess catalyst performance.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe₅C₂ catalyst favored hydrocarbon formation over CO₂, as evidenced by its higher selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons and light olefins compared to the hematite catalyst.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4\ \text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe₅C₂ catalyst produced a low proportion of methane, as inferred from the product distribution.
- **Observations and Trends:** Low CH₄ selectivity is desirable in FTS, and the Fe₅C₂ catalyst’s product profile confirms a shift toward longer-chain hydrocarbons and light olefins.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ and Light Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms. Light olefin selectivity quantifies the proportion of C₂–C₄ olefins among all carbon products.
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe₅C₂ catalyst achieved a C₅⁺ selectivity of 39%, surpassing the 26% observed for the reduced-hematite catalyst. For light olefins (C₂–C₄), the Fe₅C₂ catalyst yielded 61% olefins in the C₂–C₄ range, corresponding to 19% of all carbon products, compared to 11% for hematite.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe₅C₂ catalyst demonstrates superior selectivity for both long-chain hydrocarbons and valuable light olefins, indicating its effectiveness for targeted FTS applications.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon growth, with values closer to 1 favoring longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe₅C₂ catalyst exhibited an α value of 0.68, indicative of a strong tendency toward long-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** This high α value aligns with the observed C₅⁺ selectivity, confirming the catalyst’s propensity for chain growth.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time}} \ (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe₅C₂, CO conversion dropped from 39% to 24% over 100 hours, corresponding to a deactivation rate of 0.15% h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The main cause of deactivation was partial oxidation of Fe₅C₂ and hydrocarbon encapsulation, as confirmed by post-reaction XRD, TEM, and XPS analyses. Despite this, the catalyst retained its core structure and outperformed the hematite reference in both activity and stability.
