## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of both In‐Fe/CNT and Out‐Fe/CNT catalysts was carried out using a standardized incipient wetness impregnation method. This section details the reproducible procedures along with a comparative analysis of the two approaches regarding synthesis complexity and catalyst performance.

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation for In‐Fe/CNT Catalyst

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   – Reagent: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O dissolved in deionised water.  
   – Concentration: The amount of iron is calculated to yield ≈12 wt% Fe.  
   – Volume: The solution volume equals the total pore volume of the acid‐treated CNT (0.62 cm³/g) to ensure capillary filling.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   – Although not separately performed, the dropwise introduction of the metal solution promotes uniform deposition inside the nanotube pores before drying.

3. • Drying  
   – Conditions: Drying at 120 °C (duration briefly applied to remove moisture).  
   – Equipment: Drying oven with controlled temperature.

4. • Calcination  
   – Conditions: Calcination at 400 °C for 3 hours.  
   – Observation: Transformation of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O into the hematite (Fe₂O₃) phase is achieved, which is crucial for subsequent catalytic reduction.

#### Modification for Out‐Fe/CNT Catalyst

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   – Initially, acid‐treated CNTs are pre‐wetted with pure deionised water to fill the inner pores.  
   – This step prevents the metal solution from entering the inner channels, ensuring that the Fe species deposit primarily on the external surface.

2. • Drying and Calcination  
   – The same drying (120 °C) and calcination (400 °C for 3 hours) conditions are used to convert the impregnated precursor to iron oxide.

### Comparative Analysis

• Advantages of the incipient wetness impregnation method include controlled reagent delivery and precise volume matching, which are essential for achieving the desired active phase dispersion.  
• For In‐Fe/CNT, capillary filling leads to ≈80% internal deposition, offering potential benefits in reduced sintering and enhanced stability.  
• In contrast, the pre‐wetting approach in Out‐Fe/CNT results in predominantly external deposition, with slightly different catalytic performance.  

This reproducible procedure, with clearly defined reagent concentrations, step‑wise operations, and numerical details, ensures that other researchers can replicate the synthesis with high fidelity.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the preparation of precursors for the In‑Fe/CNT and Out‑Fe/CNT catalysts via incipient wetness impregnation, with a focus on solid weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing to ensure full reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 In‑Fe/CNT Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed as the sole metal precursor for the catalyst. Although the exact mass is not specified, the reagent is measured using a high‑precision analytical balance to ensure reproducibility.

##### 3.1.1.2 Out‑Fe/CNT Catalyst
- No explicit solid reagent weighing details are provided for the Out‑Fe/CNT preparation; the procedure relies on a preliminary wetting step rather than the direct weighing of the metal precursor.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 In‑Fe/CNT Catalyst
- **Deionized water**: Poured in an amount equal to the total pore volume of the acid‑treated carbon nanotubes (0.62 cm³/g) into the container holding the weighed Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O. This specific volume enables capillary action to draw the solution into the inner pores of the CNTs.

##### 3.1.2.2 Out‑Fe/CNT Catalyst
- **Pure water**: Poured in to pre‐wet the acid‑treated CNTs, filling the inner pores. This step is critical to ensure that, upon subsequent addition of the metal salt solution, the precursor remains primarily on the external surface of the CNTs.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 In‑Fe/CNT Catalyst
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O solution**: Mixed gently after adding deionized water so that the impregnation occurs by capillary forces. The process is carried out at ambient temperature and immediately followed by drying at 120 °C and calcination at 400 °C for 3 hours, ensuring homogeneous distribution of the iron precursor within the CNT pores.

##### 3.1.3.2 Out‑Fe/CNT Catalyst
- **Metal salt solution over wetted CNTs**: After pre‑wetting, the metal salt solution is added and mixed by mild agitation to promote even deposition on the external surface. The sample is then subjected to the same drying and calcination protocol as the In‑Fe/CNT catalyst.

This structured procedure emphasizes reproducibility by controlling the reagent volumes—especially the deionized water volume and its role in directing the deposition of the iron precursor either inside or outside the CNT pores, thus directly impacting the catalyst active phase distribution and subsequent performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: In‐Fe/CNT Catalyst

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  Calculated amount of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate) and deionised water equal to the acid‐treated CNTs total pore volume (0.62 cm³/g).
- **Conditions**: Ambient room temperature; stirring until homogeneous; no pH adjustment beyond the inherent acidity from the acid treatment.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the predetermined amount of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in deionised water to match the CNT total pore volume.
  - Directly impregnate the acid‐treated CNTs with the solution, relying on capillary action to fill the inner pores.
  - Ensure a uniform distribution by gentle stirring during impregnation.
- **Key Steps**: The matching of solution volume to pore volume is critical to ensure the precursor is deposited inside the CNTs.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly, Purification, and Thermal Treatment
- **Assembly & Purification**:
  - Immediately transfer the impregnated CNTs to a drying vessel.
  - Minimal purification is involved since the process relies on in situ deposition; any unreacted species are removed during subsequent washing if necessary.
- **Drying & Calcination**:
  - Dry the sample in an oven at 120 °C to remove moisture.
  - Calcine the dried catalyst at 400 °C for 3 hours to convert the precursor to hematite (Fe₂O₃), using a controlled ramp if available.
- **Key Steps**: Strict temperature control during drying and calcination ensures reproducibility and proper formation of the iron oxide phase.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Out‐Fe/CNT Catalyst

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation & Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: Same as above with Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and deionised water.
- **Conditions**: Prior pre‐wetting of the acid‐treated CNTs with pure deionised water.
- **Process**:
  - Pre-wet the CNTs to fill internal pores.
  - Add the iron nitrate solution so that the metal salt is primarily deposited on the exterior surface.
- **Key Steps**: This pre-wetting step prevents capillary infiltration, ensuring deposition on the outer surfaces.

##### Step [2]: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- Follow identical drying (120 °C) and calcination (400 °C for 3 hours) protocols as for In‐Fe/CNT.
  
Both synthesis workflows maintain strict adherence to reagent quantities, conditions, and sequential processing—ensuring reproducibility and clear differentiation in iron distribution between inner and outer CNT surfaces.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation step is carried out in situ within a fixed-bed micro reactor equipped with a PID temperature controller and precise mass flow controllers, ensuring controlled reduction of the iron oxide precursor to the active metallic state for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for driving the reduction of the hematite (Fe₂O₃) phase to its active metallic form, which subsequently transforms into carbide and magnetite phases during the reaction.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is ramped from room temperature to 380 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min under a continuous flow of pure hydrogen. Once 380 °C is reached, this temperature is maintained for 14 hours.
- **Observations:** The controlled temperature profile ensures thorough reduction while preventing rapid temperature spikes that could lead to sintering, thereby preserving metal dispersion.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure can influence the kinetics of reduction by affecting gas–catalyst interactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Specific activation pressure parameters are not separately provided for the reduction step. The reduction is performed at conditions typical of atmospheric pressure in the fixed-bed reactor, while the reactor is later pressurized to 2 MPa for the synthesis reaction.
- **Observations:** The absence of a designated pressure during activation indicates that atmospheric conditions are sufficient to achieve the desired reduction in this setup.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation is fundamental in facilitating the reduction of the catalyst precursor.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A flow of pure hydrogen is employed at a controlled rate of 30 mL/min. This pure hydrogen atmosphere ensures that only in situ reduction occurs, without interference from other gases that could induce carburization.
- **Observations:** The use of pure hydrogen effectively converts the iron oxide phase to an active state while helping to maintain high metal dispersion, which is especially important for catalysts with active sites confined inside carbon nanotubes.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is essential for completing the reduction process and stabilizing the active sites.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained at 380 °C for 14 hours.
- **Observations:** This extended activation period ensures full reduction of the precursor, thereby contributing to the catalyst’s consistent activity and stability during prolonged Fischer–Tropsch synthesis runs.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for syngas activation and hydrogenation steps in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, directly affecting catalytic activity and product distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic evaluations were performed at a constant temperature of 270 °C.
- **Observations:** At 270 °C, the in‐Fe/CNT catalyst exhibited high CO conversion with minimal deactivation over 125 hours. This stable performance underscores the effective syngas activation and minimized catalyst sintering at the specified temperature.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the reactants’ density within the reactor, thereby affecting conversion rates and product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were conducted under a controlled pressure of 2 MPa in a fixed-bed micro reactor setup.
- **Observations:** Operating at 2 MPa enhanced the syngas concentration, which contributed to improved conversion and favored the formation of heavier hydrocarbons. The condition also played a role in maintaining catalyst durability over extended operational periods.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio defines the balance between hydrogen and carbon monoxide, influencing the chain-growth probability during hydrocarbon synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A molar ratio of 2 (H₂/CO) was maintained throughout the Fischer–Tropsch experiments.
- **Observations:** The H₂/CO ratio of 2 was found to be optimal, promoting the synthesis of longer-chain (C₅⁺) hydrocarbons while resulting in lower methane selectivity, particularly for the in‐Fe/CNT catalyst compared to the out‐Fe/CNT variant.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity (GHSV) measures the flow rate of syngas per unit catalyst mass, impacting the reactant residence time and overall conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A gas hourly space velocity of 2000 cm³ (STP)/(h·g) was employed.
- **Observations:** This setting provided an optimal balance between sufficient contact time and reactant throughput, ensuring sustained CO conversion and minimizing catalyst deactivation over the long-term 125-hour reaction period.

These carefully controlled reaction parameters collectively ensure reproducibility and provide a robust framework for assessing catalyst performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which identifies crystalline phases and estimates crystallite sizes via the Debye–Scherrer equation. This analysis is crucial for understanding the active phases present before and after Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS).
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of both In‐Fe/CNT and Out‐Fe/CNT catalysts after calcination display peaks corresponding to hematite (Fe₂O₃), with the most intense peak at 2θ = 35.78°. Peaks at 2θ = 26° and 43° are attributed to the graphite layers of the multiwalled CNT support. After 125 hours of FTS, both catalysts exhibit magnetite (Fe₃O₄) peaks, but only the used In‐Fe/CNT catalyst shows additional peaks for iron carbide (Fe₅C₂), indicating a higher and more stable carbide content.
- **Observations:** The presence of Fe₅C₂ in In‐Fe/CNT after reaction suggests enhanced formation and stability of the catalytically active carbide phase, which is linked to improved FTS performance. The Out‐Fe/CNT catalyst lacks significant carbide peaks, correlating with lower activity and stability.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were measured by nitrogen adsorption using the BET method, providing insight into the extent of pore blockage and the accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** Acid-treated CNTs exhibit a surface area of 234 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.62 cm³/g. After iron oxide loading, the surface area decreases to 199 m²/g for In‐Fe/CNT and 211 m²/g for Out‐Fe/CNT, with corresponding pore volumes of 0.51 cm³/g and 0.59 cm³/g, respectively.
- **Observations:** The greater reduction in surface area and pore volume for In‐Fe/CNT indicates more extensive pore blockage due to iron oxide deposition inside the nanotubes, consistent with the intended synthesis strategy.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility (Temperature-Programmed Reduction)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) assesses the ease of reduction of iron oxide species, which is critical for catalyst activation and performance.
- **Findings:** The first TPR peak (Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄) occurs at 381 °C for In‐Fe/CNT and at 418 °C for Out‐Fe/CNT. Subsequent reduction steps (Fe₃O₄ to FeO and FeO to Fe) also occur at lower temperatures for In‐Fe/CNT (411 °C and 567 °C) compared to Out‐Fe/CNT (470 °C and 591 °C).
- **Observations:** The lower reduction temperatures for In‐Fe/CNT demonstrate that encapsulation within CNTs facilitates easier reduction of iron species, which is advantageous for catalyst activation.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to determine the location and size distribution of iron oxide particles, providing direct evidence of dispersion and agglomeration.
- **Findings:** TEM analysis shows that approximately 80% of iron oxide particles in In‐Fe/CNT are located inside the nanotubes, with particle sizes ranging from 6–11 nm (fresh: ~9 nm; after FTS: up to 11 nm). In contrast, Out‐Fe/CNT exhibits significant agglomeration, with particle sizes increasing from 7 nm (fresh) to 16–24 nm after FTS.
- **Observations:** The spatial confinement of iron oxide within CNTs in In‐Fe/CNT effectively limits particle growth and agglomeration, resulting in enhanced catalyst stability and performance. Out‐Fe/CNT, with exterior deposition, suffers from pronounced sintering and particle growth, correlating with faster deactivation.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (270 °C, 2 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, space velocity 2 Sl/g-cat/h), both In‐Fe/CNT and Out‐Fe/CNT catalysts achieved high initial CO conversions: 89% for In‐Fe/CNT and 91% for Out‐Fe/CNT. After 125 hours on stream, In‐Fe/CNT maintained a conversion of 85%, while Out‐Fe/CNT dropped to 79%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The In‐Fe/CNT catalyst demonstrated superior stability, with only a minor decrease in conversion over time, whereas Out‐Fe/CNT exhibited more pronounced deactivation.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (wt\%)} = \frac{\text{mass of CH}_4}{\text{total mass of hydrocarbons}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** After 125 hours, CH₄ selectivity was 25.6 wt% for In‐Fe/CNT and 40.5 wt% for Out‐Fe/CNT.
- **Observations and Trends:** The In‐Fe/CNT catalyst consistently produced less methane, indicating a favorable shift toward longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms among the total hydrocarbon products, reflecting the catalyst’s ability to promote chain growth.
- **Experimental Data:** In‐Fe/CNT achieved a C₅⁺ selectivity of approximately 36 wt%, compared to 23 wt% for Out‐Fe/CNT.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher C₅⁺ selectivity for In‐Fe/CNT demonstrates the beneficial effect of iron encapsulation within CNTs on promoting heavier hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, indicates the probability of chain propagation versus termination. Higher α values favor the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** The article reports that In‐Fe/CNT exhibits a higher α value than Out‐Fe/CNT, consistent with its higher C₅⁺ selectivity and lower methane selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced chain growth probability in In‐Fe/CNT is attributed to the spatial confinement of iron particles, which increases the contact time of intermediates and suppresses premature chain termination.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion over time, calculated as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time on Stream (h)}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 125 hours, In‐Fe/CNT’s CO conversion decreased by 4% (from 89% to 85%), corresponding to a deactivation rate of 0.032 % h⁻¹. Out‐Fe/CNT’s conversion dropped by 12% (from 91% to 79%), yielding a deactivation rate of 0.096 % h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The lower deactivation rate for In‐Fe/CNT reflects its enhanced resistance to sintering and agglomeration, as confirmed by post-reaction TEM analysis.

---

**Summary:**  
The In‐Fe/CNT catalyst, with iron oxide encapsulated inside carbon nanotubes, outperforms the Out‐Fe/CNT catalyst in terms of CO conversion stability, lower methane selectivity, higher C₅⁺ selectivity, and greater chain growth probability. These improvements are directly linked to the spatial confinement of active sites, which mitigates deactivation and promotes the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions.
