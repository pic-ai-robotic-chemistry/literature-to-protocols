## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the e‑Fe₂C‑dominant RQ Fe catalyst is achieved through an integrated process that combines rapid quenching, alkali leaching, and on‑site carbidation. This section presents a detailed, reproducible protocol with a comparative analysis of its advantages and challenges relative to conventional methods.

#### Rapid Quenching of Fe₅₀Al₅₀ Alloy
1. • Precursor Mixing: The Fe₅₀Al₅₀ alloy is synthesized using a single roller melt‑spinning method, yielding a rapidly quenched skeletal iron (RQ Fe).  
   • Equipment: Single roller melt‑spinner with a cooling rate of ~10⁷ K s⁻¹, which ensures a nanocrystalline structure characterized by low coordination sites.  
   • Advantage: This rapid solidification minimizes long‑range diffusion, producing a highly reactive precursor.

#### Alkali Leaching Procedure
2. • Precipitation Step: Subject the as‑spun RQ Fe₅₀Al₅₀ alloy (1 g) to alkali leaching by mixing with 2.5 g KOH dissolved in 6.0 ml water at 343 K.  
   • Reaction Conditions: Maintain gentle stirring for 1 h to selectively dissolve aluminum, converting the 50:50 w/w Fe/Al ratio to approximately 92.7Fe/7.3Al.  
   • Observations: Black powder precipitates are collected with a magnet, confirming the removal of aluminum.  
   • Comparative Note: Unlike conventional precipitation–deposition methods, this approach streamlines the process with minimal additional reagents and specialized equipment.

#### On‑site Carbidation under LTFTS Conditions
3. • Post‑processing Treatment: Process the skeletal RQ Fe in a polyethylene glycol (PEG200) medium under low‑temperature Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions.  
   • Reaction Conditions: Employ syngas (H₂/CO/N₂ = 64/32/4) at 30 bar with temperatures ranging between 423 K and 473 K (typically 443 K), and stir at 800 r.p.m. for approximately 20 minutes.  
   • Outcome: The structured carbidation converts α‑Fe into the active e‑Fe₂C phase, benefiting from the precursor’s nanocrystalline and low‐coordinated nature.  
   • Advantage: This method achieves catalyst activation at relatively low temperatures compared to traditional synthesis routes.

Overall, the method’s integration of rapid quenching and controlled alkali leaching creates an optimal precursor for on‑site carbidation, promoting enhanced catalyst performance with reproducible operational parameters and favorable scalability.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: e‑Fe₂C‑dominant RQ Fe Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation (RQ Fe50Al50 Alloy Synthesis)
- **Reagents**: Elemental iron (Fe) and aluminum (Al) in a 50:50 weight ratio.
- **Conditions**: Melt spinning via a single roller method providing a rapid cooling rate of approximately 10⁷ K s⁻¹.
- **Process**:
  - Melt the Fe and Al in the designated ratio.
  - Continuously extrude the molten mixture onto a rotating surface for rapid solidification, aiming to secure a nanocrystalline structure.
- **Key Steps**: Precise control during melt spinning to achieve uniform alloy formation is critical.

##### Step 2: Alkali Leaching
- **Reagents**: 1 g of the as-prepared RQ Fe50Al50 alloy, 2.5 g KOH dissolved in 6.0 ml water.
- **Conditions**: Maintain at 343 K with gentle stirring for 1 hour.
- **Process**:
  - Immerse the alloy in the freshly prepared KOH solution.
  - Sustain the temperature at 343 K while stirring to effectively leach out aluminum, forming a skeletal RQ Fe structure.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor uniform leaching to ensure complete removal of aluminum and preservation of nanostructure.

##### Step 3: Catalyst Assembly and On-Site Carbidation
- **Reagents**: The post-leached RQ Fe dispersed in a polyethylene glycol (PEG200) medium; syngas with a composition of H₂/CO/N₂ = 64/32/4.
- **Conditions**: LTFTS reaction performed at 30 bar, stirring at 800 r.p.m., within a temperature range of 423–473 K (typically 443 K) for 20 minutes.
- **Process**:
  - Disperse the leached RQ Fe uniformly in PEG200.
  - Introduce the syngas mixture to initiate on-site carbidation, transforming the skeletal RQ Fe into the active e‑Fe₂C phase.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain reaction temperature and gas composition to facilitate rapid carbidation while preserving catalyst integrity.

##### Step 4: Purification and Post-Processing
- **Process**:
  - Separate the catalyst using magnetic collection.
  - Sequentially wash the recovered powders three times with distilled water, followed by two washes each with ethanol and PEG200.
- **Key Steps**: Ensuring thorough purification is essential for reproducible, high catalytic performance.

- **Key Notes**: This method is sensitive to nano‑scale structural variations and confirms subtle changes in coordination numbers inherent to the catalyst’s reactive nature.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalyst were determined using both conventional and synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD). These techniques are essential for identifying the formation and purity of active phases, particularly the transformation from metallic iron to iron carbide under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the as-leached rapidly quenched (RQ) Fe precursor display peaks at 2θ = 44.6°, 64.8°, and 82.2°, corresponding to the (110), (200), and (211) reflections of body-centered cubic (bcc) α‑Fe. After low-temperature FTS (LTFTS) at 443 K, new peaks emerge at 2θ = 37.3°, 41.4°, 43.0°, and 56.9°, which are indexable to the (100), (002), (101), and (102) reflections of the O-carbide phase (e‑Fe₂C or ε⁰‑Fe₂.₂C, JCPDS 36-1249). The α‑Fe peak at 44.6° becomes a weak feature, indicating only minor residual metallic iron.
- **Observations:** The dominance of e‑Fe₂C peaks and the near disappearance of α‑Fe reflections confirm the successful and selective formation of the e‑Fe₂C phase. The absence of Fe₃O₄ or other iron oxide peaks further supports the high phase purity of the catalyst.

#### 6.1.2 Local Structure and Coordination Environment
- **Description:** Fe K-edge Extended X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (EXAFS) analysis was employed to probe the local atomic structure, specifically Fe–Fe bond distances and coordination numbers, which are critical for understanding the reactivity and carbidation behavior of the catalyst.
- **Findings:** The as-leached RQ Fe exhibits an expanded Fe–Fe bond distance of 2.50 Å, compared to 2.48 Å in standard Fe foil (α‑Fe). The Fe–Fe coordination number is significantly reduced to 4.0 (from 8.0 in Fe foil), indicating a high density of low-coordination sites.
- **Observations:** The expanded lattice and low coordination number are direct consequences of rapid quenching and alkali leaching, resulting in a nanocrystalline, highly reactive iron structure. These features facilitate rapid and complete carbidation to e‑Fe₂C at low temperatures.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Crystallite Size
- **Description:** The morphology and crystallite size of the catalyst were assessed using XRD (Scherrer equation) and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), providing insight into the nano-scale structure that underpins catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** The as-leached RQ Fe displays a crystallite size of 13.5 nm, while the O-carbide (e‑Fe₂C) phase formed after LTFTS exhibits a reduced crystallite size of 8.1 nm.
- **Observations:** The reduction in crystallite size upon carbidation suggests further refinement of the nanostructure, which, together with the high density of low-coordination sites, is instrumental in achieving superior catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Inductively Coupled Plasma–Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP–AES) was used to determine the elemental composition after alkali leaching, which is important for correlating structure with catalytic function.
- **Findings:** The skeletal RQ Fe material after leaching contains 92.7% Fe and 7.3% Al (molar ratio).
- **Observations:** The effective removal of aluminum and enrichment of iron content are crucial for producing a reactive precursor that can be fully converted to the active e‑Fe₂C phase.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO molecules transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{n_{\text{CO,initial}} - n_{\text{CO,final}}}{n_{\text{CO,initial}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( n_{\text{CO,initial}} \) and \( n_{\text{CO,final}} \) are the initial and final moles of CO, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** The e‑Fe₂C-dominant RQ Fe catalyst demonstrates initial CO conversion rates (r₀) of 16 molCO molFe⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 423 K, 43 molCO molFe⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 443 K, and 71 molCO molFe⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 473 K under LTFTS conditions (30 bar, H₂/CO/N₂ = 64/32/4, PEG200 medium).
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion increases markedly with temperature, indicating enhanced catalytic activity at higher temperatures. Compared to crystalline Fe nanoparticles (3.7 molCO molFe⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 443 K), RQ Co (9.8), and amorphous Co–B (7.8), the e‑Fe₂C catalyst exhibits an order-of-magnitude higher activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 473 K, CO₂ selectivity is 22.6%; at 423 K, it decreases to 10.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower reaction temperatures suppress CO₂ formation, favoring hydrocarbon production and improving carbon efficiency.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated on a carbon basis:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane and C₂–C₄ hydrocarbons dominate at 473 K, while their selectivity decreases at lower temperatures.
- **Observations and Trends:** Decreasing temperature shifts selectivity from methane toward longer-chain hydrocarbons, aligning with increased α values.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbons:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 423 K, the combined gasoline (C₅–C₁₁) and diesel (C₁₂–C₂₀) fractions reach 73% of total hydrocarbons, indicating high C₃⁺ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower temperatures favor the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons, enhancing the yield of transportation fuels.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, indicates the probability of chain propagation:
  - α ranges from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (infinite chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** α increases from 0.71 at 473 K to 0.79 at 423 K.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α at lower temperatures correlates with increased selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons, supporting efficient fuel production.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by the rate of decline in CO conversion over repeated cycles:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate (\% per run)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}}} \times \frac{100}{n}
  \]
  where \( n \) is the number of cycles.
- **Experimental Data:** Over five LTFTS cycles at 443 K, CO conversion decreases by only 4.2%, corresponding to a deactivation rate of ~1% per run.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst maintains high activity and selectivity with minimal deactivation, attributed to its robust nanostructure and stable e‑Fe₂C phase.
