## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps
The catalyst synthesis is based on a continuous coprecipitation process that integrates precise control over precursor mixing, precipitation–deposition, and thermal treatments to ensure reproducibility and high catalyst quality. This section details both the stepwise procedures and a comparative analysis of the methods, emphasizing their advantages in scalability and cost-effectiveness as well as the challenges in maintaining strict pH and temperature control.

#### • Continuous Coprecipitation and Precipitation–Deposition
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Reagents: An aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol (targeting a 50.9 wt % Fe content) is utilized.  
   - Conditions: The mixture is maintained at 80 ± 1 °C with a controlled pH of 8.2–8.5, achieved by the addition of ammonia.  
   - Equipment: pH meter and flow controllers are employed to ensure constant and precise reaction conditions.
   - Observation: Formation of a distinct precipitate signals successful coprecipitation.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - Process: Immediately following mixing, the flowing reagents interact with ammonia, which facilitates the deposition of the precipitate.  
   - Significance: This step is critical for achieving a homogeneous distribution of iron and silica before further processing.

#### • Thermal Processing and Post‑treatment
3. **Drying**  
   - Conditions: The washed and filtered precipitate is dried at 120 °C for 12 hours.  
   - Purpose: Removal of residual water and preparation for subsequent calcination.

4. **Calcination**  
   - Conditions: The dried precursor is calcined at 500 °C for 5 hours in static air.  
   - Function: Calcination assists in purifying the precursor by converting it to a stable iron oxide phase, setting the stage for later carburization.

5. **Post‑treatment (Reduction/Carburization)**  
   - Process: The calcined catalyst is exposed to different gas atmospheres—CO, H₂, or syngas (H₂/CO = 2)—at 300 °C for 20–30 hours in fixed-bed reactors.  
   - Outcome: This step results in the formation of distinct iron carbide phases (ε‑Fe₂C, Fe₇C₃, and χ‑Fe₅C₂), each influencing the catalyst’s performance.

### Comparative Analysis
- **Advantages:**  
  • Continuous operation enhances scalability and reproducibility compared to batch methods.  
  • Precise control over pH and temperature allows for consistent precipitate formation and uniform phase development.

- **Disadvantages:**  
  • Requires specialized equipment (e.g., flow meters, pH meters) for maintaining stringent conditions.  
  • The complexity of phase transformations necessitates careful monitoring to avoid undesirable side reactions.

This structured and detailed methodology, with clearly defined experimental parameters and step sequences, ensures that the synthesis process is reproducible by other researchers aiming to achieve optimal catalytic performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process
#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃, silica sol, and ammonia solution (quantities adjusted to target 50.9 wt % Fe in the calcined catalyst). Distilled water is used as solvent.
- **Conditions**: Maintain a continuous flow of the Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol solution; introduce ammonia to adjust the pH within 8.2–8.5, at a controlled temperature of 80 ± 1 °C. Temperature control is ensured using a jacketed reactor or a temperature-controlled water bath.
- **Process**:
  - Mix the flowing aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol with the ammonia solution to initiate coprecipitation.
  - Immediately monitor the pH and temperature, ensuring they are maintained at 8.2–8.5 and 80 ± 1 °C, respectively, to promote homogeneous precipitation.
  - Continue the reaction until a consistent precipitate forms.
- **Key Steps**: Emphasize precise control of pH and temperature to secure uniform distribution of iron; note the integration of silica sol and Fe precursor as vital for achieving the target composition.

##### Step 2: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Wash the precipitate repeatedly with distilled water to eliminate residual ammonia and unreacted ions.
  - Employ filtration (or centrifugation) steps to ensure complete separation of the solid precursor from the reaction mixture.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough washing, as unremoved impurities may affect later drying and calcination outcomes.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the filtered precipitate in a controlled oven at 120 °C for 12 hours, ensuring all moisture is removed.
- **Calcination**:
  - Heat the dried precursor at a ramp rate of 1 °C/min until 500 °C is reached.
  - Hold at 500 °C for 5 hours in static air to convert the precursor into a stable catalyst oxide.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor the ramp rate and maintain static air conditions to avoid premature reduction.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Reagents & Conditions**:
  - For FeSi-CO, treat with CO gas.
  - For FeSi-H₂, use H₂ gas.
  - For FeSi-syn, employ syngas with an H₂/CO ratio of 2.
  - Each activation is conducted at 300 °C for approximately 20 hours in a fixed-bed reactor with gas space velocities around 4000 mL/(gcat·h).
- **Process**:
  - Introduce the designated gas into the catalyst bed, ensuring the controlled atmosphere drives the formation of specific iron carbide phases (ε‑Fe₂C, Fe₇C₃, or χ‑Fe₅C₂).
- **Key Steps**: Accurate control of gas composition, temperature, and duration is essential to tailor the catalyst’s active phase and performance.

This workflow, beginning with the synthesis of primary precursors and proceeding through purification, drying, calcination, and activation, ensures reproducibility and a clear understanding of the catalyst formulation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is an essential pretreatment step designed to convert the calcined Fe/SiO₂ catalyst into its active carbide phases for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The activation is performed in a fixed-bed reactor configured with quartz granules to ensure proper dilution, uniform gas flow, and heat distribution.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
The calcined catalyst undergoes a controlled thermal treatment, where it is first heated to approximately 120 °C to remove retained moisture. Subsequently, the temperature is increased at a ramp rate of about 1 °C/min until reaching 300 °C. This moderate activation temperature is crucial, as it favors controlled reduction and carburization without inducing sintering. Maintaining 300 °C ensures that active carbide phases form via precise thermal management.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time and Duration  
The activated catalyst is held at 300 °C for a standard duration of 20 hours, which is critical for thorough carburization. In specific control experiments involving CO pretreatment, shorter durations (5–10 hours) were tested to observe the effect on carbide formation. The prolonged exposure at 300 °C facilitates a balance between reduction and carburization, ultimately influencing the proportion of phases such as χ‑Fe₅C₂, ε‑Fe₂C, and Fe₇C₃.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Pressure and Gas Composition  
The activation is conducted at approximately 1 bar when using CO pretreatment, ensuring a controlled environment that minimizes catalyst agglomeration. Three distinct gas atmospheres are employed during activation:  
• Pure CO, which promotes the formation of a mixture including χ‑Fe₅C₂ and Fe₇C₃ phases;  
• Pure H₂, which leads to a catalyst that contains predominantly χ‑Fe₅C₂ and traces of ε‑Fe₂C;  
• Syngas with a H₂/CO ratio of 2, which primarily produces the χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase.  

Additionally, a gas flow rate is maintained at 4 NL/(gcat·h) during the initial heating stage and adjusted to 4000 mL/(gcat·h) during the main activation at 300 °C, ensuring consistent gas–solid contact and optimal reduction kinetics. Observations indicate that these activation parameters directly influence both catalyst stability and the distribution of iron carbide phases for effective Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature defines the thermal environment in which the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis proceeds. According to the article, the catalytic tests were carried out at 260 °C. This temperature is critical for ensuring proper syngas activation while preserving the stability of active iron carbide phases such as Fe₇C₃ over prolonged reaction times. It is noted that maintaining 260 °C helps prevent unwanted phase transformations and supports effective CO conversion.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The reaction pressure plays a vital role in determining syngas concentration and the resulting product distribution. In the experiments, the reaction was performed at a pressure of 3 MPa. This elevated pressure enhances the overall concentration of CO and H₂, which is instrumental in steering the selectivity toward longer-chain hydrocarbons. The article also highlights that operating at this pressure aids in achieving higher CO conversion rates and supports the stability of the catalyst by promoting favorable reaction kinetics.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio is a key parameter in controlling the hydrogenation steps of Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The study employed an H₂/CO ratio of 2, a condition that has been shown to optimize catalytic conversion while balancing product selectivity. Although a higher ratio tends to increase methane formation, the selected value favors the production of desired C₅⁺ hydrocarbons by moderating the extent of hydrogenation, consequently influencing hydrocarbon distribution.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The space-time velocity, expressed via the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), is critical in defining the contact time between the syngas and the catalyst. The article specifies a GHSV of 4 NL/(gcat·h). This parameter is finely tuned to ensure sufficient interaction between reactants and the catalyst, thereby optimizing conversion without compromising catalyst stability. It is noted that while higher space-time velocities might reduce CO conversion, they maintain the overall integrity of the catalyst during the reaction.

Collectively, these parameters—260 °C, 3 MPa, an H₂/CO ratio of 2, and a GHSV of 4 NL/(gcat·h)—are reported to offer reproducible and optimal conditions for evaluating the performance of the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalyst.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural evolution of the Fe/SiO₂ catalysts were determined using X-ray Diffraction (XRD) with a Bruker D2 diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.54056 Å). Crystallite sizes were estimated via the Scherrer equation, using the half-width of selected diffraction peaks. Phase identification was referenced against JCPDS database cards and standard carbides synthesized for comparison.
- **Findings:** 
  - The calcined precursor exhibited highly dispersed α-Fe₂O₃ (average size 5.5 nm).
  - After gas treatments:
    - **FeSi-CO (CO-treated):** Both Fe₇C₃ (JCPDS 17-0333) and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases were present, with Fe₇C₃ crystallite size ~28.2 nm.
    - **FeSi-H₂ (H₂-treated):** α-Fe was initially formed; after FTS, ε‑Fe₂C (JCPDS 36-1249, ~9.1 nm by XRD) and χ‑Fe₅C₂ were detected.
    - **FeSi-syn (syngas-treated):** Only χ‑Fe₅C₂ was observed as the main carbide phase.
- **Observations:** The phase composition is highly sensitive to the carburization atmosphere, with CO favoring Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂, H₂ leading to ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂, and syngas producing exclusively χ‑Fe₅C₂. Larger particles tend to form χ‑Fe₅C₂, while smaller ones favor carbon-rich carbides.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) (JEM-2100F and Talos 200A, 200 kV) was used to assess particle morphology and size distributions. Samples were handled under inert conditions to prevent oxidation.
- **Findings:** 
  - Calcined α-Fe₂O₃ particles averaged 5.5 nm.
  - In spent catalysts:
    - **Fe₇C₃:** Large particles, ~27.5–28.2 nm (TEM and XRD).
    - **χ‑Fe₅C₂:** Small, highly dispersed particles, ~7.1–7.5 nm (TEM).
    - **ε‑Fe₂C:** Intermediate size, 13.9–15.4 nm (TEM).
    - Bimodal distributions were observed in CO- and H₂-treated samples, with χ‑Fe₅C₂ as small particles and Fe₇C₃ or ε‑Fe₂C as larger ones.
    - Amorphous oxide shells were occasionally seen around carbide particles.
- **Observations:** The continuous coprecipitation method yields uniform, finely dispersed nanoparticles. The particle size distribution correlates with the crystalline phase, influencing catalytic behavior.

#### 6.1.3 Iron Speciation and Phase Quantification
- **Description:** Mössbauer Spectroscopy (MES) at 11 K and X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS, including XANES and EXAFS) were employed to quantify iron species, oxidation states, and local coordination environments.
- **Findings:** 
  - MES distinguished Fe₃O₄, α-Fe, χ‑Fe₅C₂, Fe₇C₃, and ε‑Fe₂C, providing relative phase abundances and hyperfine parameters (IS, QS, Hhf).
  - XAFS confirmed the reduction degree and carbide formation, with EXAFS revealing Fe–Fe and Fe–C bond distances and coordination numbers.
  - Both techniques consistently quantified the proportions of iron carbides and oxides in spent catalysts.
- **Observations:** The combination of MES and XAFS enabled precise identification and quantification of active phases, confirming that the carburization atmosphere dictates the dominant iron carbide species present under FTS conditions.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (260 °C, 3.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 4000 h⁻¹), FeSi-CO achieves a CO conversion of 50.8%, FeSi-H₂ reaches 33.1% (with a higher value of 44.8% under adjusted conditions), and FeSi-syn exhibits intermediate performance.
- **Observations and Trends:** The CO-pretreated catalyst (FeSi-CO) consistently delivers the highest CO conversion, attributed to the formation of Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases. H₂-pretreated catalysts (FeSi-H₂), containing ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂, show lower conversion but enhanced selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-CO exhibits a CO₂ selectivity of 9.7%, FeSi-H₂ 4.6%, and FeSi-syn 2.8%.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity is highest for CO-pretreated catalysts and lowest for syngas-pretreated ones, correlating with the dominant iron carbide phases present.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-CO shows the highest CH₄ selectivity at 19.2%, FeSi-syn at 12.9%, and FeSi-H₂ the lowest at 10.6%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane formation is suppressed in H₂-pretreated catalysts (ε‑Fe₂C-rich), while CO pretreatment (Fe₇C₃-rich) promotes higher methane yields.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-H₂ achieves the highest C₅⁺ selectivity (up to 74.4%), FeSi-syn is intermediate (~70%), and FeSi-CO is lowest (~50%).
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of ε‑Fe₂C enhances chain growth, favoring long-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination in FTS, influencing product distribution. Higher α values favor long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** Catalysts with ε‑Fe₂C (FeSi-H₂) exhibit higher α values, while Fe₇C₃-rich (FeSi-CO) catalysts show lower α.
- **Observations and Trends:** A direct correlation exists between higher α, increased C₅⁺ selectivity, and reduced methane formation.

#### 6.2.6 Intrinsic Activity (TOF)
- **Description:** Turnover frequency (TOF) measures the intrinsic activity per active site:
  \[
  \text{TOF} = \frac{\text{Moles of CO converted}}{\text{Moles of surface Fe atoms} \times \text{Time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe₇C₃ exhibits the highest TOF (4.59 × 10⁻² s⁻¹), indicating superior intrinsic activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The intrinsic activity order is Fe₇C₃ > χ‑Fe₅C₂ > ε‑Fe₂C.

#### 6.2.7 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by the rate of decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Moderate carburization and controlled reduction conditions yield stable performance, with minimal deactivation observed over typical reaction durations.
- **Observations and Trends:** Excessive carburization or prolonged reaction can accelerate deactivation due to phase transformation or sintering, as confirmed by post-reaction characterization.

---
**Summary:** The catalytic performance of Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is strongly governed by the iron carbide phase composition, which is controlled by the pretreatment atmosphere. CO-pretreated catalysts maximize CO conversion and intrinsic activity but favor methane formation, while H₂-pretreated catalysts optimize long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity and stability.
