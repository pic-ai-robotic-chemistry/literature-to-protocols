## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is conducted using a continuous coprecipitation process designed for consistency and reproducibility. The method employs an aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ combined with silica sol to achieve a precise iron loading of 50.9 wt %. This solution is continuously mixed with a flowing ammonia solution at 80 ± 1 °C. The controlled addition of ammonia maintains the pH in the narrow range of 8.2–8.5, thereby ensuring uniform precipitation of the catalyst precursor.

#### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   – Reagents: Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol are mixed.  
   – Conditions: The reaction occurs at 80 ± 1 °C with pH maintained at 8.2–8.5 using controlled ammonia addition via mass flow meters.  
   – Equipment: Continuous flow setup with precise pH and temperature monitoring instruments.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   – Process: The controlled mixing promotes the deposition of iron species onto the silica support immediately after precursor mixing.  
   – Observations: Formation of a uniform precipitate is noted, indicating successful deposition.

3. • Washing and Filtering  
   – Procedure: The formed precipitate is washed several times with distilled water to remove residual ammonia and nitrates.  
   – Equipment: Standard filtration apparatus ensuring efficient separation.

4. • Drying  
   – Conditions: The washed precipitate is dried at 120 °C for 12 hours.  
   – Impact: This drying step is critical for eliminating moisture prior to thermal treatment.

5. • Calcination  
   – Conditions: The dried precursor undergoes calcination in static air at 500 °C for 5 hours, facilitating the conversion into a stable oxide structure.

6. • Post‑treatment (Reduction/Carburization)  
   – Process: The calcined catalyst is reduced under varied gas atmospheres (pure CO, pure H₂, or 2H₂/CO syngas) in a fixed-bed reactor at 300 °C for 20 hours.  
   – Additional Control: A gas hourly space velocity of 4000 mL/(g_cat·h) ensures precise control over the formation of carbide phases.

This structured approach—with detailed control over reagent concentrations, reaction conditions, and equipment—assures reproducibility and provides insights into optimizing catalyst phase composition and performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and an appropriate volume of silica sol to achieve a target iron loading of 50.9 wt %.
- **Conditions**: Aqueous solutions are maintained in a continuous flow setup at 80 ± 1 °C with a controlled pH of 8.2–8.5.
- **Process**:
  - Prepare a flowing aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ and a separate flow of silica sol.
  - Initiate coprecipitation by simultaneously mixing these streams with a continuously added ammonia solution (used to adjust the pH).
  - Ensure homogeneous mixing by maintaining controlled solution flow rates.
- **Key Steps**: Verify pH adjustment with ammonia during mixing and maintain temperature rigorously at 80 ± 1 °C.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: The freshly prepared Fe(NO₃)₃-silica sol precursor.
- **Conditions**: Continue the coprecipitation with the controlled addition of ammonia to sustain the target pH.
- **Process**:
  - Allow the precipitate to form fully, reflecting the integration of the iron and silica components.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Wash the precipitate thoroughly with distilled water to remove residual ions.
  - Proceed with filtration to obtain a clean filtered cake; filtration speeds and durations are standard for laboratory settings.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the filtered precipitate at 120 °C for 12 hours in a controlled oven.
- **Calcination**: Calcine the dried precursor at 500 °C for 5 hours in static air, ensuring a gradual temperature ramp to avoid structural collapse.

##### Step 5: Activation
- **Process**:
  - Activate the calcined catalyst by reduction under various gas atmospheres (CO, H₂, or a 2H₂/CO syngas mixture) at 300 °C for 20 hours in a fixed-bed reactor.
  - For CO-treated catalysts, variations in exposure time (e.g., 5 or 10 hours) can adjust the formation of specific carbide phases.
- **Key Conditions**: A gas space velocity of 4000 mL/(g_cat·h) is maintained during activation.

This detailed workflow—from precursor mixing via controlled coprecipitation, thorough purification, precise drying and calcination, to the final gas-phase activation—ensures reproducibility while addressing the key operational questions, such as reagent quantities, pH control, and temperature conditions, for the synthesis of the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X-ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to determine the crystalline phases (e.g., hcp Fe₇C₃, χ‑Fe₅C₂, and residual iron oxides) of the catalysts. The spent catalyst samples were ground into powders and sealed in a homemade holder immediately upon retrieval to minimize oxidation.
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements were performed using a Bruker D2 X-ray diffractometer (Bruker, Germany).
- **Characterization Conditions**: Cu Kα radiation (wavelength 1.54056 Å) was utilized with an operating voltage of 30 kV and a current of 10 mA.
- **Test Parameters**: A continuous scanning mode was adopted with a scan rate of 0.04°/sec over a 2θ range of 10°–90°.
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample sealing is critical to prevent oxidation during analysis, ensuring accurate identification of delicate carbide phases.

### 4.2 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and STEM Mapping
- **Operational Description**: TEM analysis provided high-resolution imaging for determining particle size distributions and morphological details of various iron carbide phases. The catalysts were sealed with paraffin, ground into powders, dispersed in ethanol, and sonicated for 0.5 hours before deposition on a copper grid with a holey carbon film.
- **Instruments and Models**: Imaging was carried out on a JEM-2100F (JEOL, Japan) and a Talos 200A (FEI, USA) microscope. STEM mapping was incorporated to support elemental analysis.
- **Characterization Conditions**: An accelerating voltage of 200 kV was maintained.
- **Test Parameters**: Standard TEM imaging settings were used to assess particle dimensions and distribution.
- **Key Notes**: Dual instrument usage enhances reliability of size and morphology data.

### 4.3 Mössbauer Spectroscopy (MES)
- **Operational Description**: MES was used to quantify and differentiate the iron phases by evaluating magnetic hyperfine fields and isomer shifts. Samples were cooled and measured after FTS or pretreatment.
- **Instruments and Models**: An MR-351 constant-acceleration Mössbauer spectrometer calibrated with α-Fe foil was utilized.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed at an operating temperature of 11 K.
- **Test Parameters**: Spectra were fitted using combinations of quadruple doublets and magnetic sextets, detailing isomer shifts, quadrupole splittings, and hyperfine fields.
- **Key Notes**: The fitting is complex due to overlapping signals from multiple phases, necessitating complementary techniques.

### 4.4 X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS)
- **Operational Description**: XAFS analysis was conducted to probe the local atomic structure and phase composition of the iron sites. Catalyst samples were diluted with Vaseline, pressed into self-supporting tablets, and measured in transmission mode.
- **Instruments and Models**: The analysis was performed at the 1W1B beamline of the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility.
- **Characterization Conditions**: The energy range was set from 6900 to 7900 eV.
- **Test Parameters**: XANES and EXAFS data were processed using the Athena program for background removal and Fourier transformation.
- **Key Notes**: This method complements XRD and MES by providing detailed local structural information essential for understanding catalytic performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a deliberate pre‐reaction treatment designed to transform the calcined Fe/SiO₂ precursor into active iron carbide phases critical for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This pretreatment involves reduction and carburization under carefully controlled conditions within a fixed‐bed reactor.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature controls the reduction of iron oxide and the subsequent carburization to form active phases, particularly guiding the conversion into desired carbide species.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is performed by ramping the temperature to approximately 280 °C for pure CO pretreatment and to around 300 °C for both H₂ and 2H₂/CO syngas pretreatments.
- **Observations:** Operating within the 250–350 °C range favors the formation of the χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase, with the selected temperatures ensuring effective reduction and transformation of precursor species without triggering excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The pressure during activation assists in maintaining the gas–catalyst interactions necessary for effective reduction and carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** In the CO pretreatment, the process is carried out at atmospheric pressure (1 bar).
- **Observations:** This pressure setting facilitates adequate gas contact and reaction kinetics while avoiding agglomeration effects during catalyst transformation.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation is pivotal in steering the reduction and carburization processes, thereby influencing the resulting active phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Three gas atmospheres are employed:
  - Pure CO (leading to predominant χ‑Fe₅C₂ and some Fe₇C₃ formation)
  - Pure H₂ (yielding a mixture of χ‑Fe₅C₂ and ε‑Fe₂C)
  - 2H₂/CO syngas (primarily producing χ‑Fe₅C₂)
- **Observations:** Each atmosphere distinctly impacts the carbide phase distribution and, consequently, the catalyst’s activity and selectivity profiles.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation ensures complete transformation of the precursor while controlling particle growth.
- **Experimental Conditions:** All pretreatment experiments are conducted for 20 hours under the selected gas atmospheres.
- **Observations:** This extended duration enables full development of active phases without inducing detrimental sintering.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Although space-time velocity is crucial during reaction tests to control reactant residence time, no specific numerical flow rate is provided for the activation step.
- **Observations:** Variations in space-time velocity are primarily adjusted during Fischer–Tropsch reaction runs rather than during the catalyst activation stage.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis governs syngas activation and the stabilization of active carbide phases, such as χ‑Fe₅C₂, which is critical for optimal catalytic performance.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst tests were carried out at a controlled temperature of 260 °C.
- **Observations:** Maintaining 260 °C ensured that the desired iron carbide phases remained stable, with minimal phase transformation observed during the catalytic runs.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure directly influences syngas concentration and the kinetics of hydrocarbon formation. It is also key to adjusting product selectivity, particularly in balancing methane versus heavier hydrocarbon yields.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch reactions were performed under pressures ranging from 1 to 3 MPa. The fixed-bed reactor setup, regulated by mass flow meters, ensured that the desired pressure was uniformly established before the onset of the reaction.
- **Observations:** The chosen pressure range was found to favor the formation of C₅+ hydrocarbons while maintaining reliable syngas conversion levels. This setting also minimized unwanted byproducts, although detailed selectivity variations with individual pressure adjustments were not provided.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is a critical parameter that influences the overall product distribution; higher ratios tend to increase methane formation, while lower ratios promote olefin and longer-chain hydrocarbon production.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 2 was consistently maintained throughout all catalytic tests.
- **Observations:** Using a ratio of 2 helped balance the reaction kinetics by controlling methane selectivity and enabling a favorable distribution of longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) indicates the flow rate of syngas relative to the catalyst mass and determines the residence time of reactants on the catalytic surface.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were conducted at a GHSV of 4000 mL/(g_cat·h).
- **Observations:** Variations in space-time velocity were observed to have a negligible effect on the overall hydrocarbon selectivity, although they play a role in the fine-tuning of CO conversion levels and catalyst performance stability.

These carefully controlled parameters underscore the reproducibility of the experimental conditions, enabling direct extraction and future replication of the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation as reported in the study.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases present in the Fe/SiO₂ catalysts were identified using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), which detects characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to specific iron oxide and carbide phases. The evolution of these phases under different reduction and carburization atmospheres is critical for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) performance.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis revealed that the calcined catalyst primarily contained highly dispersed α-Fe₂O₃ or γ-Fe₂O₃, with broad peaks at 34.7° and 62.7°. After reduction/carburization at 300 °C for 20 h, distinct phase compositions were observed depending on the gas atmosphere:
  - **CO-treated (FeSi-CO):** Formation of Fe₇C₃ (peaks at 40.0°, 42.9°, 44.9°, 50.8°, 53.4°) and χ‑Fe₅C₂ (41.0°, 43.6°, 44.7°), indicating a bimodal phase distribution.
  - **H₂-treated (FeSi-H₂):** Presence of ε‑Fe₂C (notably at 42.8°) and χ‑Fe₅C₂.
  - **Syngas-treated (FeSi-syn):** Only χ‑Fe₅C₂ detected.
- **Observations:** The choice of reduction/carburization gas directly determines the resulting carbide phases. Longer CO exposure increases Fe₇C₃ content, while H₂ favors ε‑Fe₂C formation. These phase distributions are closely linked to catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Phase Composition and Quantification
- **Description:** Mössbauer Emission Spectroscopy (MES) and X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS) were employed to quantify the proportions of iron carbide phases and confirm their local atomic environments.
- **Findings:** MES at 11 K, with spectra fitted to known hyperfine parameters, quantified the phase content in spent catalysts:
  - **FeSi-CO:** Dominant Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases.
  - **FeSi-H₂:** 14.7% ε‑Fe₂C and 11.0% χ‑Fe₅C₂.
  - **FeSi-syn:** Predominantly χ‑Fe₅C₂.
  XAFS (EXAFS) analysis corroborated these assignments, confirming the local coordination environments of the respective carbides.
- **Observations:** The agreement between MES and XAFS validates the phase quantification. The stability of these phases before and after FTS was confirmed, indicating robust catalyst durability under reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and HAADF-STEM with EDX mapping were used to assess particle size, morphology, and elemental distribution, providing insight into catalyst dispersion and phase growth.
- **Findings:** 
  - **Calcined catalyst:** Uniform α-Fe₂O₃ particles with an average size of 5.5 nm.
  - **FeSi-CO (spent):** Bimodal distribution with Fe₇C₃ particles averaging 28.2 nm (XRD) or 27.5 nm (TEM), and χ‑Fe₅C₂ particles around 7.1 nm.
  - **FeSi-H₂ (spent):** ε‑Fe₂C particles at 15.4 nm and χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 7.8 nm.
  - **FeSi-syn (spent):** χ‑Fe₅C₂ particles at 6.7 nm, with no significant agglomeration.
- **Observations:** The silica support ensures fine dispersion and inhibits sintering, maintaining small particle sizes and uniform distribution. The particle size correlates with phase formation: larger particles favor Fe₇C₃ or ε‑Fe₂C, while smaller ones form χ‑Fe₅C₂.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Distribution and Support Interaction
- **Description:** STEM-EDX mapping provided spatial distribution of Fe and Si, elucidating the interaction between iron phases and the silica support.
- **Findings:** Elemental mapping confirmed homogeneous dispersion of iron species within the silica matrix, with no evidence of phase segregation or significant migration during synthesis or reaction.
- **Observations:** The intimate contact between iron carbides and silica contributes to phase stabilization and catalyst durability, supporting high activity and selectivity in FTS.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is typically calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (260 °C, 3 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 4000 h⁻¹), the FeSi-CO catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 50.8%, while the FeSi-syn catalyst exhibited a significantly lower conversion of 22.3%. The FeSi-H₂ catalyst showed intermediate performance.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO pretreatment leads to the highest CO conversion, attributed to the formation of highly active Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases. Syngas pretreatment, which yields only χ‑Fe₅C₂, results in the lowest conversion. This demonstrates that the activation atmosphere critically determines the active phase composition and, consequently, the catalytic activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity values were 9.7% for FeSi-CO, 4.6% for FeSi-H₂, and 2.8% for FeSi-syn.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO-treated catalysts exhibit the highest CO₂ selectivity, suggesting enhanced water–gas shift activity. Syngas pretreatment suppresses CO₂ formation, correlating with lower CO conversion.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the weight percentage of methane among total hydrocarbon products:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (wt \%)} = \frac{\text{mass of CH}_4}{\text{total hydrocarbon mass}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-CO shows a CH₄ selectivity of 19.2%, while FeSi-H₂ achieves the lowest value at 10.6%. FeSi-syn presents intermediate selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** H₂ pretreatment, which favors ε‑Fe₂C formation, minimizes methane production and enhances long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity. CO pretreatment increases methane yield, reflecting the influence of Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms in the product distribution:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (wt \%)} = \frac{\text{mass of C}_5^+ \text{ hydrocarbons}}{\text{total hydrocarbon mass}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-H₂ exhibits the highest C₅⁺ selectivity at 74.4%, while FeSi-CO records 50.0%. FeSi-syn falls between these values.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest C₅⁺ selectivity is achieved with H₂ pretreatment, indicating that ε‑Fe₂C promotes chain growth and suppresses methane formation. CO pretreatment, in contrast, favors shorter-chain products.

#### 6.2.5 Intrinsic Activity (TOF)
- **Description:** The turnover frequency (TOF) represents the number of CO molecules converted per surface iron atom per second:
  \[
  \text{TOF (s}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO conversion rate}}{\text{number of surface Fe atoms}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe₇C₃ demonstrates the highest TOF at 0.0459 s⁻¹, surpassing both χ‑Fe₅C₂ and ε‑Fe₂C.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior intrinsic activity of Fe₇C₃ underscores its role as the most active phase under medium-temperature FTS conditions.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring the decline in CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Controlled carburization (moderate CO exposure) results in stable phase composition and sustained activity, as evidenced by minimal changes in CO conversion during extended FTS runs.
- **Observations and Trends:** Excessive or insufficient carburization leads to rapid deactivation or long induction periods, respectively. Silica support enhances phase stability, particularly for Fe₇C₃, contributing to improved catalyst durability.

---
**Summary:** The catalytic performance of Fe/SiO₂ catalysts is governed by the iron carbide phase composition, which is tunable via pretreatment atmosphere. Fe₇C₃ delivers the highest activity, while ε‑Fe₂C ensures superior selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons. Silica support and controlled carburization are critical for achieving optimal performance and stability.
