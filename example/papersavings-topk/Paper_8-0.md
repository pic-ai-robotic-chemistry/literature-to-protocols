## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines two primary synthesis approaches for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts: one based on MIL‑101(Fe) and the other via continuous coprecipitation for unsupported‑Fe. Both methods provide reproducible procedures with explicit reagent quantities, reaction conditions, and equipment specifications.

- **Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques**  
  • MIL‑101(Fe) is first prepared hydrothermally, then converted into Fe@C catalysts through pyrolysis in a 2% C₂H₂/He atmosphere. The pyrolysis temperature (500 °C, 600 °C, or 700 °C) dictates the carbon shell morphology—from amorphous to graphene- or graphite-type. Post‑treatment by hydrogen reduction (400 °C for 24 h) further modifies the catalyst for enhanced performance.  
  • For comparison, an unsupported‑Fe catalyst is prepared via a continuous coprecipitation process, followed by calcination and hydrogen reduction.

### Step‑by‑step Experimental Procedures

#### MIL‑101(Fe)‑Derived Fe@C Catalysts
1. **Precursor Mixing / Hydrothermal Synthesis**  
   • Reagents: 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 mL nitric acid, and 70 mL DMF.  
   • Mix and stir for 2 h to ensure homogenization.  
2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • Transfer the homogeneous mixture into a Teflon‑lined steel autoclave to initiate precipitation–deposition under hydrothermal conditions at 120 °C for 48 h.
3. **Washing & Drying**  
   • Wash the resulting product with DMF and ethanol (×3), followed by drying at 80 °C overnight.
4. **Pyrolysis**  
   • Pyrolyze in a stream of 2% C₂H₂/He at 500 °C, 600 °C, or 700 °C for the designated duration to yield Fe@C‑500 (amorphous carbon), Fe@C‑600 (3–5 layered graphene shell), or Fe@C‑700 (thick graphite shell).
5. **Post‑treatment**  
   • For Fe@C‑600‑H₂, further reduce the pyrolyzed catalyst in H₂ at 400 °C for 24 h.

#### Unsupported‑Fe Catalyst via Coprecipitation
1. **Precursor Mixing**  
   • Mix an aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ with ammonia solution under vigorous stirring at 80 ± 1 °C, maintaining the pH at 8.2–8.5 (preparing for deposition).
2. **Precipitation–Deposition and Aging**  
   • Add ammonia dropwise to form a precipitate and age the mixture for 2 h.
3. **Drying & Calcination**  
   • Dry the precipitate (e.g., 80 °C overnight) and calcine at 500 °C for 5 h in static air.
4. **Hydrogen Reduction**  
   • Reduce the calcined precursor in hydrogen for 24 h (e.g., at 350 °C) to activate the catalyst.

This structured methodology, with clearly numbered steps and precise experimental conditions, ensures reproducibility and provides a comparative framework for assessing catalyst performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: MOF‑Derived Fe@C Catalysts (Fe@C‑500, Fe@C‑600, Fe@C‑700)

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation – MIL‑101(Fe) Synthesis  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (1.5 mmol)  
  • 166 mg terephthalic acid (1 mmol)  
  • 70 mL N,N‑dimethylformamide (DMF)  
  • 5 mL nitric acid (0.074 mmol)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Stirring for 2 hours at room temperature  
  • Transfer to a 100 mL Teflon‑lined autoclave  
  • Heat at 120 °C for 48 hours  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve all reagents in DMF with vigorous stirring until a homogeneous solution is obtained.  
  - Transfer the solution into the autoclave and perform the hydrothermal treatment.  
  - After reaction, wash the solid product with DMF and ethanol (three times) and dry at 80 °C overnight.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Ensure complete dissolution and uniform mixing to facilitate uniform nucleation of MIL‑101(Fe).

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly via Pyrolysis  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Dried MIL‑101(Fe) precursor  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Pyrolysis in a tube furnace under a flow of 2% C₂H₂/He  
  • Heating rate: 5 °C/min until target temperature  
  • Target temperatures: 500 °C (Fe@C‑500), 600 °C (Fe@C‑600), 700 °C (Fe@C‑700)  
  • Residence time of 1 hour at the target temperature  
- **Process**:  
  - Place MIL‑101(Fe) in the furnace and ramp temperature under the controlled gas flow.  
  - Hold at the desired temperature to achieve the specific carbon shell type (amorphous at 500 °C, graphene at 600 °C, graphite at 700 °C).  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Precise temperature control and gas composition are critical to modulate the carbon shell structure and iron carbide phase formation.

##### Step [3]: Activation (for Fe@C‑600‑H₂)  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Fe@C‑600 catalyst  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Reduction in H₂ atmosphere  
  • 400 °C for 24 hours  
- **Process**:  
  - Transfer Fe@C‑600 to a reactor with H₂ flow at 50 mL/min.  
  - Reduce under the prescribed conditions to slightly modify the iron phase without damaging the carbon shell.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Monitor temperature and gas flow to ensure controlled reduction.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Unsupported‑Fe Catalyst via Coprecipitation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ solution and ammonia solution  
- **Conditions & Process**:  
  - Add the Fe(NO₃)₃ solution dropwise into an ammonia solution at 80 ± 1 °C while maintaining the pH at 8.2–8.5.  
  - Stir continuously for 2 hours, then age, wash, and filter the precipitate.  
  - Dry at 80 °C overnight, followed by calcination at 500 °C in air for 5 hours and hydrogen treatment at 350 °C for 24 hours.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Emphasize controlled pH adjustment and gradual reagent addition to ensure uniform particle formation.

These workflows strictly follow the order from precursor preparation to final activation to ensure reproducibility.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

In the present work, catalyst activation is carried out as a deliberate pre-reaction treatment designed to optimize the phase composition and performance of MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This activation step, distinct from any sample treatment during characterization, involves a hydrogen reduction process that transforms the dominant h‑Fe₃C phase into metallic α‑Fe, thereby influencing the catalytic behavior.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical in driving the reduction reaction and altering the catalyst phase. It ensures complete conversion of the iron carbide (h‑Fe₃C) into metallic iron (α‑Fe) while preserving the integrity of the carbon shell.
- **Experimental Conditions:** For the Fe@C‑600 catalyst, the hydrogen reduction is performed at 400 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 400 °C has been verified to yield complete phase transformation without compromising the protective graphene shell that confines the nanoparticles.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The choice of activation gas dictates the reduction mechanism by providing a reducing environment for the catalyst. 
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation employs pure hydrogen, introduced at a flow rate of 50 ml/min.
- **Observations:** Hydrogen effectively reduces the h‑Fe₃C phase to α‑Fe. This controlled reduction facilitates improved metal dispersion and aids in preserving the carbon coating, which minimizes sintering and particle agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation treatment ensures that the reduction reaction proceeds to completion while preventing any detrimental effects such as over-reduction or structural damage.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is subjected to the reduction treatment for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** A 24-hour reduction period at the specified temperature and hydrogen flow ensures full conversion of the active phase with enhanced stability and reproducibility in catalytic performance.

*Note:* No specific activation pressure or space‑time velocity adjustments for the hydrogen reduction are reported. The provided activation parameters are solely associated with preparing the catalyst for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction testing and are not related to any characterization-specific activations.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is pivotal in promoting the activation of syngas, which in turn influences the conversion rates and product distribution in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The fixed-bed reactor was operated at a temperature of 300 °C.  
- **Observations:** At 300 °C, the catalyst exhibits optimal performance by facilitating effective CO conversion while suppressing byproduct formation. This temperature has been shown to strike a balance between reaction kinetics and product selectivity, leading to enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure plays a critical role in maintaining the necessary concentration of reactants and influencing the thermodynamics of the synthesis process.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Experiments were conducted at a pressure of 2.0 MPa.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 2.0 MPa enhances the reaction kinetics and significantly favors the formation of heavier, longer-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺) by providing the appropriate conditions for chain growth and minimizing light hydrocarbon production.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed gas is crucial for directing the product distribution in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A stoichiometric H₂/CO ratio of 1.0 was maintained during the tests.  
- **Observations:** The balanced H₂/CO ratio of 1.0 is effective in optimizing hydrocarbon selectivity; while it ensures adequate syngas conversion, it also reduces the formation of methane, allowing for an increased selectivity towards longer-chain hydrocarbons typical from coal- or biomass-derived syngas.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, often presented as the weight hourly space velocity (WGSV), determines the contact time between the reactants and the catalyst, impacting conversion efficiency and product yield.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The WGSV was set at 8000 ml/(gcat·h) for MOF-derived catalysts and at 3000 ml/(gcat·h) for the unsupported‑Fe catalyst.  
- **Observations:** The higher WGSV for MOF-derived catalysts facilitates shorter residence times without compromising conversion, while the lower WGSV for unsupported‑Fe allows for extended reactant contact time, affecting the overall catalytic performance and long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the Fe@C catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), using reference patterns (JCPDS 35-0772 for h‑Fe₃C, 06-0696 for a‑Fe, and 06-0615 for FeO) to identify each phase. XRD provides insight into the transformation of iron species during pyrolysis and their relation to catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** Fe@C‑500 exhibited the characteristic diffraction line of FeO, indicating incomplete carburization at lower temperature. Fe@C‑600 displayed clear diffraction lines for h‑Fe₃C, graphite carbon, and a‑Fe, confirming the coexistence of these phases. Fe@C‑700 showed sharper and narrower diffraction lines for h‑Fe₃C and graphite carbon, reflecting increased crystallinity and a thicker graphite shell. The phase composition shifted from FeO dominance at 500 °C to h‑Fe₃C and graphite carbon at 700 °C.
- **Observations:** Increasing pyrolysis temperature promotes the conversion of FeO to h‑Fe₃C and enhances graphitization, which is associated with improved catalyst stability and performance.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were used to examine nanoparticle morphology, core–shell structure, and particle size distribution. Elemental mapping via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed spatial distribution of Fe and C.
- **Findings:** Fe@C‑600 exhibited the smallest average nanoparticle size (~5.9 nm), attributed to the protective 3–5 layer graphene shell observed in HRTEM (interplanar spacing 0.346 nm). Elemental mapping confirmed iron nanoparticles encapsulated within a carbon matrix. In contrast, unsupported‑Fe showed much larger particles (~63 nm) and significant aggregation.
- **Observations:** The graphene shell in Fe@C‑600 and Fe@C‑600‑H₂ effectively prevents nanoparticle aggregation and oxidation, maintaining uniform dispersion even after extended Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET) analysis measured surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, providing information on textural properties relevant to catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** MIL‑101(Fe) exhibited a high surface area (1632 m²/g) and pore volume (0.83 cm³/g). After pyrolysis, Fe@C catalysts showed reduced surface areas (135–291 m²/g) and pore volumes (0.23–0.28 cm³/g); for Fe@C‑600, SBET = 280 m²/g, Vp = 0.27 cm³/g, and mean pore size = 3.8 nm.
- **Observations:** The decrease in surface area and porosity is due to MOF framework collapse during pyrolysis, but the retained mesoporosity supports effective reactant diffusion and active site accessibility.

#### 6.1.4 Carbon Shell Structure (Raman Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy characterized the nature and thickness of carbon shells, distinguishing between amorphous, graphene, and graphite structures.
- **Findings:** Fe@C‑500 showed a weak, single 2D peak (~2700 cm⁻¹), indicative of amorphous carbon (ID/IG = 1.10). Fe@C‑600 and Fe@C‑600‑H₂ exhibited broad 2D bands deconvoluted into four peaks, characteristic of 3–5 layer graphene shells (ID/IG = 0.79). Fe@C‑700 displayed two 2D peaks, typical of bulk graphite (ID/IG = 0.91).
- **Observations:** Higher pyrolysis temperatures yield more ordered and thicker carbon shells, with graphene and graphite layers providing enhanced protection and stability for the encapsulated iron nanoparticles.

#### 6.1.5 Phase Quantification (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the relative content of h‑Fe₃C in the catalysts, correlating phase composition with synthesis temperature.
- **Findings:** The h‑Fe₃C content increased with pyrolysis temperature: Fe@C‑500 (68.2%), Fe@C‑600 (87.2%), and Fe@C‑700 (95.5%).
- **Observations:** Enhanced carburization at higher temperatures leads to a predominance of h‑Fe₃C, which is closely linked to improved Fischer–Tropsch catalytic activity and selectivity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 300 °C, 2.0 MPa, and H₂/CO = 1.0, Fe@C‑600 achieved a CO conversion of 65.0% at 24 h, increasing to 74.5% at 184 h. Fe@C‑600‑H₂ exhibited higher conversions of 78.7% (24 h) and 81.5% (184 h). Unsupported‑Fe reached 75.3% at 24 h but declined sharply to 39.2% at 184 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The gradual increase in CO conversion for Fe@C‑600 is attributed to the removal of carbon fragments blocking active sites, while the graphene shell ensures sustained activity. Fe@C‑600‑H₂, dominated by α-Fe, shows higher initial conversion but with increased methane selectivity. Unsupported‑Fe suffers rapid deactivation, highlighting the stabilizing effect of the carbon shell in MOF-derived catalysts.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h, CO₂ selectivity values were: Fe@C‑600, 33.3%; Fe@C‑600‑H₂, 37.3%; unsupported‑Fe, 38.5%.
- **Observations and Trends:** h‑Fe₃C–dominated Fe@C‑600 exhibits lower CO₂ selectivity compared to α-Fe–rich Fe@C‑600‑H₂ and unsupported‑Fe, indicating suppressed water–gas shift activity in the presence of h‑Fe₃C.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h, Fe@C‑600 showed 10.5% CH₄ selectivity, Fe@C‑600‑H₂ 23.4%, and unsupported‑Fe 28.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The h‑Fe₃C phase in Fe@C‑600 effectively suppresses methane formation, while α-Fe (Fe@C‑600‑H₂) and larger, less protected particles (unsupported‑Fe) favor higher methane production.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming long-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺), calculated as:
\[
\text{C₅⁺ Selectivity} = \frac{\text{Weight of C₅⁺ hydrocarbons}}{\text{Total weight of hydrocarbons produced}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h, Fe@C‑600 achieved 60.4% C₅⁺ selectivity, Fe@C‑600‑H₂ 38.6%, and unsupported‑Fe 14.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The dominance of h‑Fe₃C in Fe@C‑600 leads to markedly higher C₅⁺ selectivity, while α-Fe and unsupported catalysts favor lighter hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution:
\[
\alpha = \text{slope of } \ln(W_n/n) \text{ vs. } n
\]
- **Experimental Data:** h‑Fe₃C–dominated catalysts (Fe@C‑600) exhibit α values of 0.67–0.69, significantly higher than unpromoted Fe@C catalysts (0.45–0.48).
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values in Fe@C‑600 correlate with enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity and suppressed methane formation, directly linked to the h‑Fe₃C phase.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate is defined as the decline in CO conversion per hour:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C‑600 and Fe@C‑600‑H₂ display minimal deactivation over 184 h. In contrast, Fe@C‑500, Fe@C‑700, and unsupported‑Fe show deactivation rates of 13.1%, 20.1%, and 47.9%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** The graphene shell in Fe@C‑600 and Fe@C‑600‑H₂ effectively prevents nanoparticle aggregation and oxidation, ensuring superior long-term stability compared to other catalysts.

#### 6.2.7 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY measures the catalytic activity per unit iron mass:
\[
\text{FTY} = \frac{\text{μmol CO converted}}{\text{g Fe} \cdot \text{s}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C‑600 achieves an FTY of 176.5 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹, substantially higher than unsupported‑Fe (39.5 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹).
- **Observations and Trends:** The high FTY of Fe@C‑600 reflects the benefits of small, well-dispersed nanoparticles and the protective graphene shell, enabling efficient and durable catalysis under high space velocity conditions.
