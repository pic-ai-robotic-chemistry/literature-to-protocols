## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

• Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques  
The synthesis of Fischer–Tropsch catalysts employs a systematic approach ensuring both accuracy and reproducibility. Two primary synthesis routes are reported: one for preparing the sodium‐doped ferric carbide (Na‑FeC*x*) and another for generating zeolite promoters (s‑ZSM‑5 and s‑S1‑OH). A comparative analysis reveals that while the co‑precipitation method for Na‑FeC*x* involves standard aqueous chemistry with controlled pH, the zeolite synthesis methods (hydrothermal vs. urea‑assisted) differ in reaction time, temperature, and equipment requirements, affecting scalability and operational costs.

### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

#### Na‑FeC*x* Synthesis  
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Reagents: FeCl₃⋅6H₂O and FeCl₂⋅4H₂O in an acidified aqueous solution.  
   • pH Adjustment: 2.0 M NaOH is added dropwise under vigorous stirring until the pH reaches 10.  
   • Equipment: pH meter (used for monitoring) and standard laboratory glassware.
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   • After initiating Na‑Fe₃O₄ formation, the precipitation–deposition step is integrated by continuing dropwise addition until complete precipitation is observed.  
3. Drying  
   • The solid is separated by centrifugation and washed thoroughly with deionized water (six times) before drying at 100 °C overnight.
4. Calcination and Post‑treatment  
   • Syngas Treatment: The dried precursor is subjected to a syngas atmosphere (CO/H₂ 1:1) at 300 °C for 24 h to convert Na‑Fe₃O₄ into the active carbide phase (Fe₅C₂ dominant).  
   • Observations: Typical experimental notes include uniform precipitate formation and a color change upon carburization.

#### Zeolite Promoter Synthesis  
1. Precursor Mixing  
   • For s‑ZSM‑5, TPAOH (structure‐directing agent) is dissolved in water, and TEOS (silica source) is added; in some cases, aluminium isopropoxide is incorporated.  
2. Hydrothermal Processing  
   • Reactor: A Teflon‑lined autoclave is used.  
   • Conditions: The mixture is autoclaved at 180 °C for 2–3 days.  
3. Post‑processing  
   • After autoclaving, the solid is recovered, washed, and calcined at 550 °C for 4 h.  
4. For siliceous s‑S1‑OH, the organosilane DEMS is added (molar ratio 1:10 relative to total silica) to generate silanol groups and achieve a nanosheet morphology.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the reagent preparation steps for both the Na‑FeC*x* catalyst and the zeolite‐promoted systems (Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5 and Na‑FeC*x*/s‑S1‑OH), organized by weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing to ensure full reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Na‑FeC*x*  
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O**: Weighed 30.0 g using an analytical balance.  
- **FeCl₂·4H₂O**: Weighed 11.9 g using an analytical balance.

##### 3.1.1.2 Zeolite‐Promoted Systems  
For the granule mixing of the active and promoter phases:  
- **Na‑FeC*x***: Weighed 0.1 g.  
- **s‑ZSM‑5**: Weighed 0.3 g (for the Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5 system).  
- **s‑S1‑OH**: Weighed 0.3 g (for the Na‑FeC*x*/s‑S1‑OH system).

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Na‑FeC*x*  
- **Deionized water**: Poured 200 mL into the reaction vessel to dissolve the iron salts.  
- **2.0 M NaOH solution**: Added dropwise under vigorous stirring to adjust the pH to approximately 10, ensuring controlled precipitation of Na‑Fe₃O₄.

##### 3.1.2.2 Zeolite‐Promoted Systems  
For the synthesis of zeolite promoters, liquid reagents are transferred as follows:  
- **TPAOH (25% aqueous solution)**: Dissolved in 5.1 g of deionized water.  
- **TEOS**: For s‑ZSM‑5, 2.8 g of TEOS is poured into the TPAOH solution; for s‑S1‑OH, 2.52 g is similarly added.  
- **DEMS** (organosilane): For the s‑S1‑OH system, 0.199 g is incorporated into the stirring solution.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Na‑FeC*x*  
- **Fe salt–NaOH mixture**: Mixed vigorously at room temperature for 1 hour to ensure complete precipitation and homogenization of the Na‑Fe₃O₄ intermediate.

##### 3.1.3.2 Zeolite‐Promoted Systems  
- **Zeolite precursor solution (s‑ZSM‑5)**: Stirred continuously until a uniform solution was achieved prior to hydrothermal treatment in an autoclave.  
- **Zeolite precursor solution (s‑S1‑OH)**: Mixed under continuous stirring at room temperature for 30 minutes to obtain a homogeneous mixture before proceeding with crystallization steps.

These detailed procedures—including precise chemical masses, liquid volumes, and controlled mixing durations—ensure that both catalyst systems can be reliably reproduced in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Na‑FeC*x* Precursor Synthesis

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation of Na‑Fe₃O₄
- **Reagents**: 30.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 11.9 g FeCl₂·4H₂O, sufficient 12.0 M HCl for acidification, and 2.0 M NaOH.
- **Conditions**: Dissolution in deionized water with stirring at 300–500 rpm for 30–60 minutes. NaOH is added dropwise under vigorous stirring until the pH reaches ~10.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the iron salts in acidified deionized water to form a homogeneous solution.
  - Under continuous vigorous stirring, add the 2.0 M NaOH solution dropwise until a pH of ~10 is achieved.
  - Confirm complete precipitation by visual inspection and, if available, analytical methods (e.g., XRD).
- **Key Steps**: Maintain consistent stirring and control the rate of NaOH addition to prevent local pH variations.

##### Step 2: Purification and Drying
- **Process**:
  - Centrifuge the suspension using a standard laboratory centrifuge to separate the precipitate (specific speed and time may be optimized as needed).
  - Wash the precipitate thoroughly six times with deionized water to remove residual ions.
  - Dry the washed precursor at 100 °C overnight.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough washing post-centrifugation and proper drying to prepare the sample for activation.

##### Step 3: Carburization Activation
- **Conditions**: Exposure to a syngas atmosphere (CO/H₂ at a 1:1 molar ratio) at 300 °C for 24 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Load the dried precursor into a reactor.
  - Perform the syngas treatment under controlled temperature conditions and steady gas flow to convert the precursor into Na‑FeC*x* (predominated by the Fe₅C₂ phase).
- **Key Steps**: Carefully control the temperature ramp and gas composition to facilitate the carburization process effectively.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Zeolite Support Synthesis (s‑ZSM‑5 and s‑S1‑OH)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation for Zeolite
- **Reagents**: For s‑ZSM‑5: TPAOH, TEOS, and optionally aluminum isopropoxide; for s‑S1‑OH: TEOS combined with the organosilane DEMS (molar ratio of DEMS to total silica of 1:10).
- **Conditions**: Mix reagents in deionized water at room temperature with vigorous stirring.
- **Process**:
  - Prepare a homogeneous gel by first dissolving TPAOH, then adding TEOS (and aluminum isopropoxide if needed), ensuring complete mixing.
  - For s‑S1‑OH, incorporate DEMS into the mixture to induce artificial silanol groups.
  - Transfer the gel into an autoclave and crystallize at 180 °C for 2–3 days.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure that the structure-directing agent is uniformly distributed before crystallization.

##### Step 2: Calcination
- **Conditions**: Calcine the crystallized zeolite product at 550 °C for 4 hours in an air atmosphere.
- **Process**:
  - Heat the sample in an oven with a controlled ramp rate until the final temperature is reached, ensuring removal of organic templates.
- **Key Steps**: Precise temperature control during calcination is essential for activating the zeolitic framework.

##### Step 3: Catalyst Assembly via Granule Mixing
- **Process**:
  - Combine the granulated Na‑FeC*x* (from [1]) and zeolite support (prepared as above) using a granule mixing method with particle sizes of 20–40 mesh.
  - The granule mixing procedure ensures uniform distribution while minimizing excessive sodium migration.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain careful mixing protocols to preserve the active components, a critical pause point for troubleshooting and ensuring reproducibility.

This workflow, starting with complete precursor preparation and proceeding through purification, activation, and zeolite synthesis, provides a reproducible procedure for synthesizing the Na‑FeC*x* and zeolite-promoted catalysts.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation consists of a two‐step thermal treatment designed to ensure complete reduction of the iron precursor and controlled carburization to form the active carbide phase.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Temperature is critical for both reduction and carbide formation. Initially, the catalyst is pre‐reduced by heating at 400 °C in a flowing pure H₂ atmosphere for 6 hours. This high-temperature step ensures that the iron oxide precursor is effectively reduced to its metallic form. Following pre‐reduction, the reactor is cooled to 260 °C, at which point the catalyst is exposed to a syngas environment. In some contexts, a syngas treatment at 300 °C for 24 hours is utilized, promoting a balanced carburization process that yields the desired Fe₅C₂-dominant phase while avoiding undue sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
The activation is conducted under a controlled pressure of 2.0 MPa during the syngas treatment. Operating at this elevated pressure enhances gas–solid interactions and facilitates efficient reduction, contributing to a robust activation process that minimizes the risk of catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
Two distinct gas compositions are employed during activation. During the initial pre-reduction, pure hydrogen is used to achieve a clean, complete reduction of the iron precursor. Once cooled, the catalyst is treated with a syngas mixture composed of H₂/CO/Ar in a strict molar ratio of 45/45/10. This controlled mixture provides a reducing environment while also supplying the carbon required for carburization, ensuring the formation of the active carbide phase.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity, calculated based on catalyst weight, is maintained at approximately 2,400 mL/(g_cat·h). This flow condition is essential for achieving uniform gas exposure across the catalyst bed, which in turn favors homogeneous activation without localized over-reduction or under-treatment.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
In addition to the 6-hour reduction period, the catalyst is maintained under syngas flow until steady-state performance is reached—approximately 50 hours under reaction conditions. This extended duration confirms that the active phase is fully developed and stable, as evidenced by consistent catalytic performance and favorable product selectivity.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter for activating syngas and ensuring selective conversion. In these Fischer–Tropsch evaluations, the reactor is maintained at 260 °C. This temperature enables effective CO conversion while favoring the selective formation of lower olefins. Notably, the catalyst achieves stable performance, as confirmed by data recorded after more than 40 hours of reaction, ensuring both steady-state CO conversion and consistent product selectivity.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Operating at a controlled pressure of 2.0 MPa, the system sustains a high syngas concentration, which is essential for driving the Fischer–Tropsch reaction forward. This pressure condition not only ensures rapid CO activation but also fine-tunes the product distribution by enhancing olefin selectivity over methane. The rigorous maintenance of 2.0 MPa helps in stabilizing the reaction equilibrium and curbing undesired side reactions.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The feed gas composition is a key variable in determining reaction performance and product characteristics. In these experiments, Fischer–Tropsch synthesis tests were conducted using H₂/CO ratios of 1, 2, or 3. Adjustments to this ratio exhibit marked influences on CO conversion rates and hydrocarbon product distribution. Specifically, higher ratios have been associated with improved CO conversion—reaching 81.3% at a 2:1 ratio and 82.6% at a 3:1 ratio—while simultaneously ensuring a narrow product distribution dominated by C₂–C₁₀ olefins.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space–Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is defined as the ratio of the syngas flow rate to the catalyst mass, and it directly impacts the residence time of reactants at the catalytic surface. In these tests, the GHSV is set at 2,400 mL·h⁻¹ per g of FeC*x*. While this parameter maintains sufficient contact time for effective conversion, further observations indicate that increasing the flow rate (e.g., to 9,600 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹) leads to a reduction in CO conversion without markedly altering product selectivity, thereby underscoring its importance in balancing conversion efficiency with catalyst stability.

- **Observations:** Maintaining sodium on the FeC*x* phase is crucial for catalytic activity; excessive migration reduces performance.

#### 6.1.6 Olefin Adsorption and Desorption Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature-Programmed Desorption (TPD) and DRIFTS were used to study olefin adsorption/desorption on zeolite surfaces.
- **Findings:** s‑ZSM‑5 and s‑S1‑OH exhibited rapid olefin desorption, while n‑ZSM‑5 showed delayed desorption, as evidenced by TPD and DRIFTS profiles.
- **Observations:** Accelerated olefin desorption on nanosheet zeolites inhibits secondary reactions, favoring high olefin selectivity in FTO.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard low-temperature Fischer–Tropsch to olefins (LT-FTO) conditions (260 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1, GHSV = 2,400 ml h⁻¹ g⁻¹), Na‑FeC*x* alone exhibited a CO conversion of 1.9%. Granule-mixed Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5 achieved up to 82.5% conversion (zeolite/Na‑FeC*x* mass ratio 3), while Na‑FeC*x*/s‑S1‑OH reached 66.5% (H₂/CO = 1) and up to 82.6% (H₂/CO = 3).
- **Observations and Trends:** The dramatic increase in CO conversion upon zeolite promotion highlights the synergistic effect of physical granule mixing, which preserves sodium distribution and enhances reactivity.

#### 6.2.2 Olefin Selectivity and Productivity
- **Description:** Olefin selectivity is the percentage of total hydrocarbons produced that are olefins, excluding CO₂. Olefin productivity is quantified as mmol of olefins produced per gram of FeC*x* per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5 delivered an olefin selectivity of 72.0% and productivity of 15.3 mmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹. Na‑FeC*x*/s‑S1‑OH achieved 13.1 mmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹. The fraction of α-olefins among C₄⁺ olefins was 52.1% for s‑ZSM‑5 and up to 81.7% for s‑S1‑OH.
- **Observations and Trends:** Zeolite promotion, especially with silanol-rich s‑S1‑OH, enhances both the yield and purity of α-olefins, suppressing isomerization and side reactions.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** Methane selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding CH₄, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity was minimized in zeolite-promoted systems: 6.0% for Na‑FeC*x*/s‑S1‑OH and less than 3.8% for Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of MFI zeolite nanosheets effectively suppresses methane formation compared to classical FTO catalysts.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity and Product Distribution
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity refers to the proportion of hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms among total hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** Product distributions were dominated by C₂–C₁₀ olefins, with C₅–C₁₀ olefins comprising 54.7% of C₂–C₁₀ olefins for Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5. C₁₀⁺ selectivity was low (6.3% for Na‑FeC*x*/s‑S1‑OH).
- **Observations and Trends:** Zeolite promotion narrows the product range, favoring lower olefins and minimizing heavy hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth versus termination, influencing hydrocarbon distribution. Higher α values favor longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the product distribution for Na‑FeC*x*/zeolite systems deviates from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, with a pronounced shift toward C₂–C₁₀ olefins and high α-olefin fractions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The use of silanol-rich, non-acidic zeolites (s‑S1‑OH) further increases α-olefin selectivity by preventing isomerization.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 600 h of continuous operation, Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5 maintained CO conversion from 68.1% to 67.6%, indicating a negligible deactivation rate (h⁻¹ ≈ 0).
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibits exceptional durability, with stable performance and minimal loss of activity over extended periods.

---
**Summary:**  
The integration of MFI zeolite nanosheets with Na‑FeC*x* catalysts under optimized conditions yields outstanding CO conversion, high selectivity and productivity for lower olefins (especially α-olefins), suppressed methane and heavy hydrocarbon formation, and remarkable long-term stability. These results underscore the critical role of zeolite morphology and composition in modulating Fischer–Tropsch performance.
