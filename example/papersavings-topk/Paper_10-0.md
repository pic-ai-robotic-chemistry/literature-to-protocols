## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalysts (100Fe/4Cu series) were synthesized using a microemulsion method that guarantees nanosized iron catalyst formation and uniform dispersion of copper and promoter species. The procedure is organized in the following sequential steps:

#### 1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
• A water solution of the metal precursor, FeCl₃·6H₂O, is prepared and mixed with an oil phase composed of 1‑butanol and chloroform in a 1:1 ratio.  
• Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) is added as a surfactant to stabilize the emulsion and ensure uniform mixing.  

#### 2. Precipitation–Deposition  
• Immediately following mixing, hydrazine (25%–30%) is introduced as the precipitating agent to induce rapid nucleation of the catalyst particles.  
• The mixture is stirred continuously for 4 hours, allowing the formation of precipitates, which signal the deposition of metal oxides.  
• For catalysts requiring promoter incorporation (La and Ba), the corresponding lanthanum and barium oxide precursors are added at this stage for synergistic dispersion with the iron and copper components.

#### 3. Drying  
• The resultant solid is left to decant overnight and then recovered via centrifugation.  
• The solid is thoroughly washed with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone to remove residual surfactants and impurities.  
• Drying is performed overnight at 110 °C, ensuring complete removal of solvents.

#### 4. Calcination and Post‑treatment  
• The dried catalyst is calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 hours, a critical step to achieve the cubic hematite crystal structure.  
• Post‑calcination, a reduction step using a 10% H₂–90% N₂ flow is carried out for 3 hours to activate the catalyst by reducing surface species.

This structured approach, with precise reagent concentrations, reaction conditions, and equipment usage (e.g., centrifuge, drying oven, fixed‑bed reactor for later catalytic evaluation), ensures reproducibility and clarity. The method highlights the advantages of nanosizing and controlled promoter dispersion while noting scalability considerations compared to more conventional routes.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe/4Cu and Promoted Variants  
The synthesis of the 100Fe/4Cu series catalysts begins with a clearly defined precursor preparation and follows a strict workflow ensuring both reproducibility and precision.

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Aqueous solution of FeCl₃·6H₂O (as the iron source)  
  • Copper oxide (to yield a 4 mol% Cu contribution)  
  • For promoted variants: lanthanum oxide and/or barium oxide in stoichiometric ratios (e.g., 2La, 1La/1Ba, or 2Ba)  
  • 1-Butanol and chloroform (oil phase components)  
  • Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as surfactant  
  • Hydrazine (precipitating agent)  

- **Conditions**:  
  The iron solution is mixed with the oil phase in a water-in-oil microemulsion system. The mixture is stirred for 4 hours at ambient temperature to ensure homogeneity. Critical parameters include maintaining precise reagent volumes and controlled addition of hydrazine to adjust pH and initiate precipitation.

- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O in deionized water and mix with the oil phase (1-butanol/chloroform) containing SDS.  
  2. Add copper oxide and, for promoted catalysts, the specified amounts of La and/or Ba oxides.  
  3. Introduce hydrazine slowly while stirring vigorously to prevent rapid local pH changes, allowing uniform precipitation of iron hydroxide complexes.  
  4. Continue stirring for 4 hours, then allow the mixture to decant overnight.  

- **Key Steps**:  
  Ensure precise hydrazine dosing to control pH, prevent side reactions, and achieve uniform particle size (targeting 10–30 nm as verified later by TEM).

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Post-Processing  
- **Purification**:  
  Recover the precipitate by centrifugation, then wash sequentially with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone to remove surfactants and residual reagents.

- **Drying & Calcination**:  
  Dry the catalyst overnight at 110 °C. Calcine in air at 400 °C for 4 hours using a controlled ramp rate to transform the precursor into a cubic hematite phase as confirmed by XRD.

- **Activation (if applicable)**:  
  Reduce the catalyst in a 10% H₂–90% N₂ flow for 3 hours, followed by activation in a synthesis gas stream (H₂/CO ratio of 1 at 270 °C) to stabilize active iron carbide phases.

This comprehensive sequence—from precursor preparation through catalyst assembly, purification, drying, calcination, to activation—ensures that every experimental parameter is reported with clarity, enabling reproducibility and facilitating the correlation between promoter addition and Fischer–Tropsch catalytic performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
The catalyst activation process employs a carefully controlled temperature of 270 °C during the synthesis gas treatment. This moderate temperature is selected to provide sufficient thermal energy to reduce the metal oxides—transforming CuO and Fe₂O₃ into their respective active species—while minimizing sintering, thus preserving the nanoscale morphology and optimal metal dispersion.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
During the initial activation phase, the catalyst is treated at atmospheric pressure. This deliberate choice facilitates a gradual reduction of the oxides without inducing stress that could promote catalyst agglomeration. Once the activation phase is complete, the reactor conditions are elevated to 18 bar for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction, ensuring proper operational conditions without interfering with the catalyst pre-treatment.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
The activation protocol is executed in two sequential gas treatment stages. First, the catalyst undergoes a reduction step using a 10% H₂–90% N₂ mixture for 3 hours. This gentle reducing atmosphere is critical for converting the precursor oxides while averting over‐reduction. The catalyst is subsequently exposed to a synthesis gas stream with an H₂/CO ratio of 1 for an additional 24 hours at 270 °C. This second stage not only further reduces the catalyst but also facilitates controlled carburization, ensuring the formation of active iron carbide phases essential for effective Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
The volumetric flow rate during activation is maintained at a space-time velocity of 1.5 nl/(h·g) using precise electronic mass flow controllers. This careful control of gas flow rate ensures uniform exposure of all catalyst particles to the reducing gases, promoting consistent activation throughout the sample.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
The total activation time amounts to 27 hours, combining 3 hours of initial reduction with 24 hours of synthesis gas treatment. This extended activation duration is critical in achieving complete reduction and optimal active phase formation, as evidenced by improved CO conversion rates and catalyst stability during subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Philips PW1840 diffractometer with monochromatized Cu Kα radiation. The Scherrer equation was applied to estimate average crystallite sizes from the (104) hematite peak at 2θ = 33.3°.
- **Findings:** All catalysts, including those promoted with La and/or Ba, exhibited diffraction patterns matching the cubic hematite (Fe₂O₃) phase, as confirmed by comparison with the JCPDS database. No new crystalline phases were detected upon promoter addition, indicating structural preservation across all formulations. The average crystallite sizes, calculated from the XRD peak broadening, were consistent among the samples.
- **Observations:** The retention of the cubic hematite structure regardless of promoter type or loading demonstrates the robustness of the synthesis method and ensures a consistent active phase for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a LEO 912AB instrument was employed to assess nanoparticle morphology and size distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed that all catalyst samples consisted of nanoparticles in the 10–30 nm range. No significant differences in morphology or particle size distribution were observed among the unpromoted and promoted catalysts.
- **Observations:** The uniform nanoscale morphology across all samples is attributed to the microemulsion synthesis method, which provides controlled nucleation and growth, supporting high catalytic activity by maximizing active surface area.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen adsorption–desorption (BET) analysis was conducted to determine surface area, pore volume, and average pore size. Samples were degassed at 100 °C for 1 hour and 300 °C for 2 hours prior to measurement.
- **Findings:** The addition of La and Ba promoters led to a decrease in both BET surface area and pore volume compared to the unpromoted 100Fe/4Cu catalyst. This reduction is attributed to aggregation of crystallites and partial pore blockage by the promoters. The catalysts exhibited moderate surface areas, lower than those typically observed for supported systems.
- **Observations:** Despite the decrease in surface area and pore volume, the promoted catalysts demonstrated enhanced catalytic performance, indicating that surface chemistry modifications outweighed textural changes.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Basicity (CO₂-TPD)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed desorption of CO₂ (CO₂-TPD) was used to quantify surface basicity and the strength of basic sites.
- **Findings:** CO₂-TPD results showed a marked increase in basicity with promoter addition: CO₂ desorption values (mmol/gcat) increased from 123 (100Fe/4Cu) to 575 (100Fe/4Cu/2La), 747 (100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba), and 1062 (100Fe/4Cu/2Ba). Ba-containing catalysts exhibited higher desorption temperatures and broader peaks, indicating stronger and more abundant basic sites.
- **Observations:** The enhanced basicity, especially in dual-promoted and Ba-rich systems, is linked to improved CO adsorption and activation, correlating with superior Fischer–Tropsch and water-gas shift performance.

#### 6.1.5 Reduction Behavior (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction with hydrogen (H₂-TPR) was performed to investigate reducibility and metal-support interactions.
- **Findings:** Promoter addition shifted reduction peaks to higher temperatures. For example, the dual-promoted 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba catalyst exhibited main reduction peaks at 300 °C, 400 °C, and 620 °C, compared to 282 °C, 323 °C, and 522 °C for the unpromoted catalyst. Higher H₂ consumption was also observed in promoted samples.
- **Observations:** Increased reduction temperatures indicate stronger metal-support interactions and greater stability of oxidic phases, which contribute to the enhanced stability and activity of the promoted catalysts under reaction conditions.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is typically expressed as a percentage:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (290 °C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.1, GHSV = 3 nl/(g·h), 72 h time on stream), the following CO conversion values were observed:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 59.6%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 68.5%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 71.8%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 79.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Ba and La promoters, especially in combination, significantly enhances CO conversion. The dual-promoted catalyst (100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La) achieves the highest conversion, demonstrating a strong synergistic effect.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2\,\text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 17.29%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 20.31%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 23.43%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 25.03%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity increases with promoter addition, with the highest value for the dual-promoted catalyst. Enhanced WGS activity is linked to improved catalyst stability via stabilization of iron carbides.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4\,\text{Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.96%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 10.44%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 12.32%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 10.81%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition, particularly Ba and La, suppresses methane formation. The dual-promoted catalyst maintains low CH₄ selectivity while maximizing CO conversion.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms, reflecting chain growth propensity.
- **Experimental Data:** Selectivity for heavier fractions (C₅–C₁₂, C₁₃–C₁₉, C₁₉⁺) increases with promoter addition:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: C₅–C₁₂: 15.79%, C₁₃–C₁₉: 10.35%, C₁₉⁺: 7.69%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: C₅–C₁₂: 19.12%, C₁₃–C₁₉: 10.41%, C₁₉⁺: 6.41%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: C₅–C₁₂: 18.23%, C₁₃–C₁₉: 10.44%, C₁₉⁺: 6.53%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: C₅–C₁₂: 21.80%, C₁₃–C₁₉: 10.50%, C₁₉⁺: 6.56%
- **Observations and Trends:** The dual-promoted catalyst exhibits the highest selectivity for C₅–C₁₂ hydrocarbons, indicating enhanced chain growth and a favorable product distribution.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, quantifies the probability of chain growth versus termination:
  \[
  x_i = (1 - \alpha)\alpha^{i-1}
  \]
  where \( x_i \) is the mole fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( i \).
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 0.61
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 0.71
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 0.66
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 0.68
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition increases α, with the highest value for Ba-only promotion. The dual-promoted catalyst also achieves a high α, reflecting a strong tendency toward longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over 72 h time on stream.
- **Experimental Data:** Promoted catalysts, especially 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La, maintain higher CO conversion throughout the test period compared to the unpromoted catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** The synergistic effect of Ba and La enhances both activity and stability, attributed to improved CO adsorption, increased basicity, and stabilization of active iron carbide phases.
