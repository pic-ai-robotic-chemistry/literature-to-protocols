## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe₃O₄@χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalysts is performed via a sequential procedure ensuring reproducibility and high phase purity, with specific emphasis on controlling reaction conditions to obtain either nanocubes or octahedra on a SiC support. A comparative analysis of the methods reveals that while the use of high boiling solvents offers consistent nanocrystal formation, it requires strict atmosphere control and specialized equipment, such as a fixed‐bed reactor and N₂‐filled glove box.

#### Fe₃O₄ Nanocrystal and Catalyst Preparation

1. • Precursor Mixing  
   - Reagents: 1.4 g Fe(acac)₃, 1.0 g 4‑biphenylcarboxylic acid, 2.5 mL oleic acid (with oleylamine as co‑additive) dissolved in 20.0 mL benzyl ether.  
   - Equipment: Four‑neck flask under an inert atmosphere.  
   - Note: This step is crucial for templating the desired morphology – cubic for nanocubes and octahedral for octahedra.

2. • (Precipitation–Deposition)  
   - Although a precipitation–deposition step is often mentioned in synthesis workflows, in this procedure the formation of well‑defined Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals is achieved without an explicit precipitation–deposition phase.

3. • Reduction  
   - Conditions: Reduced in a fixed‑bed reactor using H₂ at 270 °C for 10 h under 1 bar pressure with a gas flow of 100 mL/min.  
   - Observation: Transformation of precursor complexes into crystalline Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals.

4. • Surface Reconstruction (Carburization)  
   - Conditions: The reduced Fe₃O₄ is subjected to a carburization step at 270 °C for 20 h under 20 bar of syngas composed of 64 vol% CO, 32 vol% H₂, and 4 vol% Ar.  
   - Outcome: Formation of a χ‑Fe₅C₂ shell over the Fe₃O₄ core differentiates nanocubes from octahedra based on the initial crystal template.

5. • Post‑treatment  
   - Procedure: The catalyst is cooled under a continuous flow of N₂ and subsequently transferred to an N₂‑filled glove box.  
   - Advantage: This inert handling prevents oxidation, preserving the sensitive Fe carbides.

This method, while complex, is scalable and cost‑effective. The precise control over reaction conditions and equipment selections ensures reproducibility and high catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ Nanocubes/SiC

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g Fe(acac)₃, 1.0 g 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid, 90% oleic acid (as specified in protocol), 2.5 mL oleylamine, and 50 mL benzyl ether.  
- **Conditions**: Degas at 120 °C for 30 minutes; stir at 300 rpm; heat to 290 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve Fe(acac)₃, 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid, oleic acid, and oleylamine in benzyl ether to form a homogeneous solution.  
  - Degas the mixture under a nitrogen atmosphere at 120 °C for 30 minutes to remove moisture and unwanted volatiles.  
  - Heat the solution to 290 °C and maintain for 30 minutes to induce controlled nucleation and growth of Fe₃O₄ nanocubes.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and effective degassing for reproducibility and prevention of premature oxidation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Reduction  
- **Reagents**: Prepared Fe₃O₄ nanocubes from Step 1.  
- **Conditions**: Reduction in a fixed-bed reactor at 270 °C, under 1 bar H₂ with a flow rate of 100 mL/min for 10 hours; heating ramp set at 1 °C/min.  
- **Process**:  
  - Load the Fe₃O₄ nanocubes into the reactor.  
  - Subject the catalyst to a controlled H₂ reduction to remove residual organics and activate the surface.  
  - Maintain steady gas flow throughout to ensure uniform reduction.
- **Key Steps**: Precisely control gas flow and temperature to secure complete reduction without structural damage.

##### Step 3: Carburization and Surface Reconstruction  
- **Reagents**: Syngas mixture composed of 64 vol% CO, 32 vol% H₂, and 4 vol% Ar.  
- **Conditions**: 20 bar pressure at 270 °C for 20 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Expose the reduced Fe₃O₄ nanocubes to the syngas mixture to induce surface carburization, forming a coherent χ-Fe₅C₂ shell.  
  - Post-carburization, cool the catalyst under a nitrogen atmosphere (using an N₂-filled glove box) to prevent oxidation of the sensitive carbide phase.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain precise gas composition and controlled atmosphere during cooling to ensure integrity of the core–shell structure.

*The synthesis for octahedral morphologies follows a similar sequence with adjustments in precursor solvent composition and heating profiles to favor octahedral crystallite formation.*

- **Precursor Preparation**: Use a modified solvent mixture designed to direct octahedral growth while still using Fe(acac)₃ and 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation procedure is divided into two distinct stages, each designed to modify the catalyst’s surface structure for optimal performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Reduction Activation

In the first stage, the Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals undergo a controlled reduction treatment. The catalyst is subjected to a pure H₂ atmosphere at an activation temperature of 270 °C. This temperature is critical to promote the removal of surface organics and induce partial reduction without altering the nanocrystal morphology. The process is carried out under 1 bar pressure using a fixed-bed reactor (inner diameter: 9 mm) to ensure a uniform gas flow and temperature distribution. A gas flow rate of 100 mL/min is maintained throughout the process, and the activation time extends for 10 hours. The controlled use of H₂ at these parameters helps preserve the nanostructure while preparing a pristine surface for subsequent transformations.

Observations during this reduction step indicate that the careful control over temperature and gas flow not only ensures complete reduction but also minimizes the risk of excessive sintering. This stage is fundamental in establishing the foundation for the later surface reconstruction, and its reproducibility is vital for achieving consistent catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.2 Surface Reconstruction (Carburization) Activation

Following the reduction, the catalyst undergoes a surface reconstruction phase aimed at carburizing the surface to form the χ-Fe₅C₂ shell. In this stage, the catalyst remains at 270 °C, but the activation environment is changed to a syngas mixture composed of 64 vol% CO, 32 vol% H₂, and 4 vol% Ar. The activation pressure is elevated to 20 bar to enhance the reaction kinetics, and the catalyst is maintained under these conditions for 20 hours. The CO in the syngas provides the carbon source required for carburization, while H₂ assists in facilitating CO dissociation and smooth surface reconstruction; Ar acts as a diluent to temper the reaction environment.

The coordinated implementation of these parameters results in a uniform formation of an iron carbide shell and helps maintain the catalyst’s facet-dependent properties—vital for high catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. Post-activation, the system is cooled under an inert N₂ atmosphere to protect the sensitive carbide phase from oxidation.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is a critical parameter in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis that governs syngas activation and the balance between the direct CO dissociation and hydrogen-assisted pathways.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were conducted at a fixed temperature of 270 °C.  
- **Observations:** At this temperature, the Fe3O4@χ-Fe5C2 nanocubes demonstrated an effective dual CO dissociation mechanism, and the catalyst maintained a highly stable performance with a CO conversion fluctuation within 1% over a 100-hour period.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the overall CO conversion and directs the selectivity towards heavier hydrocarbons by shifting the reaction equilibrium.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were performed under a pressure of 20 bar using a fixed-bed reactor setup.  
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure promoted enhanced conversion rates and improved product distribution. Specifically, this condition favored the formation of heavier hydrocarbons, as evidenced by the higher catalytic performance of the nanocubes compared to the octahedra.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are essential for identifying phase transitions and quantifying the relative abundance of iron oxide and carbide phases, which directly influence Fischer–Tropsch activity.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed distinct reflections corresponding to Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) and χ-Fe₅C₂ (iron carbide), confirming the formation of a core–shell structure. The (400) facet of Fe₃O₄ (d = 0.21 nm) in the core closely matched the (202) facet of χ-Fe₅C₂ (d = 0.22 nm) in the shell, with a lattice mismatch of 4.65%, supporting an epitaxial relationship. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the phase composition: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes contained 63.4 wt% Fe₃O₄ and 33.2 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂ before reaction, with the χ-Fe₅C₂ fraction increasing to 39.8 wt% after 100 h on stream.
- **Observations:** The epitaxial growth of the χ-Fe₅C₂ shell over the Fe₃O₄ core is critical for catalyst stability and activity. The increase in χ-Fe₅C₂ content during reaction indicates progressive carburization under syngas.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Facet Exposure
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) were employed to analyze particle morphology, core–shell architecture, and exposed crystal facets, which are known to affect catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** Both nanocubes (average size: 40.1 ± 3.8 nm) and octahedra (45.4 ± 3.5 nm) exhibited a well-defined Fe₃O₄ core and a χ-Fe₅C₂ shell. The shell thickness was 2.0 nm for nanocubes and 1.7 nm for octahedra. Fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis confirmed that nanocubes exposed {202} facets of χ-Fe₅C₂, while octahedra exposed {112} facets. After 100 h of reaction, the shell thickness increased, indicating continued surface reconstruction.
- **Observations:** The uniformity and facet specificity of the nanocubes are linked to their superior catalytic stability and activity compared to octahedra.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Distribution
- **Description:** Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) were used for elemental mapping, providing spatial resolution of Fe, O, and C within the core–shell structure.
- **Findings:** EDS/EELS mapping showed Fe and O concentrated in the core (Fe₃O₄), while Fe and C dominated the shell (χ-Fe₅C₂). Line-scanning profiles confirmed the spatial separation of these elements, validating the core–shell architecture.
- **Observations:** The clear elemental segregation supports the successful synthesis of the intended nanostructures and underpins their functional properties.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) pore size analyses were conducted to assess textural properties, which influence reactant diffusion and catalytic efficiency.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area was 20.4 m²/g for nanocubes and 22.0 m²/g for octahedra. Both morphologies were initially nonporous, as confirmed by HAADF-STEM. After 100 h on stream, octahedra developed mesopores (average diameter: 16.0 nm), while nanocubes remained nonporous.
- **Observations:** The stable, nonporous shell of nanocubes contributes to their enhanced catalytic stability, while the formation of mesopores in octahedra correlates with structural degradation and deactivation.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and CO Adsorption
- **Description:** In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was used to probe CO adsorption and reaction intermediates under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.
- **Findings:** Nanocubes exhibited both direct and hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathways, evidenced by the presence of CHO* and CO₂ species. Octahedra primarily showed direct CO dissociation, lacking CHO* or COH* signals. Experimental conditions included 1 bar CO or 20 bar syngas at 270 °C.
- **Observations:** The dual CO dissociation mechanism on nanocubes is associated with lower activation energy and superior catalytic performance, highlighting the importance of facet engineering.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (270 °C, 20 bar, H₂/CO ≈ 2), Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a CO conversion of 45.4%, while Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ octahedra/SiC reached 21.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes catalyst, exposing the {202} facet, exhibited more than double the CO conversion of the octahedra ({112} facet), highlighting the critical role of facet engineering in enhancing catalytic activity.

#### 6.2.2 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF measures the intrinsic activity of the catalyst, defined as the moles of CO converted per mole of surface Fe atoms per hour:
  \[
  \text{TOF} = \frac{\text{Moles of CO converted per hour}}{\text{Moles of surface Fe atoms}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The nanocubes/SiC catalyst displayed a TOF of 645.9 h⁻¹, significantly higher than the octahedra/SiC catalyst (372.7 h⁻¹).
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher TOF for nanocubes confirms superior intrinsic activity, attributed to the hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway enabled by the {202} facet.

#### 6.2.3 Product Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity reflects the distribution of hydrocarbon products. C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of long-chain hydrocarbons, while C₂–C₄ selectivity represents light hydrocarbons. Selectivity is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{C}_n\text{H}_m \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{n \times F_{\text{C}_n\text{H}_m,\text{out}}}{\sum_i i \times F_{\text{C}_i\text{H}_m,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For nanocubes/SiC, C₅⁺ selectivity was 44.8 C%, and C₂–C₄ selectivity was 41.0 C%. For octahedra/SiC, C₅⁺ selectivity was 27.6 C%, and C₂–C₄ selectivity was 53.1 C%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes favor the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons, while octahedra produce more light hydrocarbons, indicating facet-dependent product distribution.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth during hydrocarbon formation, following Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) statistics. Values closer to 1 indicate a higher tendency for long-chain product formation.
- **Experimental Data:** The α value for nanocubes/SiC is 0.66, and for octahedra/SiC is 0.62.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both catalysts exhibit similar chain growth probabilities, suggesting that facet sensitivity has limited influence on α, though nanocubes show a marginally higher value.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. Deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 100 h on stream, nanocubes/SiC maintained stable CO conversion (carbon balance 98.7%), while octahedra/SiC conversion declined from 20.1% to 11.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes catalyst demonstrated superior stability, attributed to robust morphology and reduced carbon deposition, whereas octahedra suffered from void formation and rapid deactivation.
