## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

• Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques:  
The Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalyst is synthesized via a continuous coprecipitation method that ensures a homogeneous distribution of iron species on the silica support. This technique is subsequently complemented by reduction and gas–solid carburization steps carried out in controlled reactors. A comparative analysis of the method reveals advantages in reproducibility and scalability, although the complexity and equipment requirements are higher than for some batch processes.

#### Precursor Mixing / Coprecipitation  
1. An aqueous solution containing Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol is mixed to achieve a final iron loading of 50.9 wt %.  
   • Reagents: Iron(III) nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃) and silica sol.  
   • Conditions: Temperature maintained at 80 ± 1 °C with a pH of 8.2−8.5, controlled by the gradual addition of ammonia solution.  
   • Observations: The onset of precipitate formation confirms that the controlled pH is inducing precipitation.

#### Precipitation–Deposition  
2. Directly following the initial mixing, a precipitation–deposition step is conducted.  
   • This step ensures uniform deposition of the catalyst precursor on the support prior to further processing.

#### Drying  
3. The precipitate is washed with distilled water, filtered, and dried for 12 hours at 120 °C.  
   • Observations: Thorough drying eliminates residual moisture and prepares the material for calcination.

#### Calcination  
4. The dried precursor is calcined in static air at 500 °C for 5 hours.  
   • Observations: Calcination promotes crystallization of the oxide precursor, as later confirmed by X‐ray diffraction.

#### Post‑processing: Reduction and Gas–solid Carburization  
5. The catalyst precursor is then transferred to a fixed-bed reactor where it undergoes reduction and gas–solid carburization.  
   • Conditions: Carburization is performed at 300 °C with a gas space velocity of 4000 mL/(gcat·h) using CO, H₂, or a syngas mixture for 20 hours.  
   • Observations: The controlled reactor environment enables selective formation of the carbide phases (ε‑Fe₂C, Fe₇C₃, and χ‑Fe₅C₂).  
   • Safety/Handling: Inert atmospheres (e.g., paraffin protection, glovebox transfers) are applied to prevent oxidation during these stages.

Comparative Analysis:  
• The continuous coprecipitation method offers strong reproducibility with exact control over temperature, pH, and reagent ratios, attributes that are crucial for achieving the desired catalyst structure. However, its implementation requires specialized equipment and operational precision compared to simpler impregnation techniques.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: An aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ (adjusted to achieve a 50.9 wt % target loading) combined with silica sol forms the basis of the precursor. Ammonia solution is added to control the pH.
- **Conditions**: The continuous coprecipitation is maintained at 80 ± 1 °C while the pH is controlled between 8.2 and 8.5. 
- **Process**:
  - Prepare two separate flowing streams: one containing Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol, and a second comprising ammonia solution.
  - Mix these streams in a reactor under a constant temperature of 80 °C; continuously monitor and adjust the pH by dropwise addition of ammonia.
  - Allow the reaction to proceed until a uniform wet precipitate is formed.
- **Key Steps**: Precise control of pH and temperature ensures reproducible formation of the catalyst precursor; consistent mixing avoids localized concentration differences.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Reagents**: The freshly precipitated catalyst precursor.
- **Conditions**: Wash with distilled water to remove any residual ions.
- **Process**:
  - Immediately wash the precipitate multiple times with distilled water.
  - Filter the washed suspension to separate the clean catalyst cake.
- **Key Steps**: Repeated washing and efficient filtration are critical to removing impurities that may affect later stages.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the filtered cake at 120 °C for 12 hours in a controlled oven to remove adsorbed moisture without causing structural changes.
- **Calcination**: Calcine the dried precursor at 500 °C for 5 hours in static air. Although specific ramp rates are not detailed, controlled heating is assumed to avoid sintering.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain strict time and temperature control during both drying and calcination to preserve catalyst integrity.

##### Step 4: Activation

#### 5.1.2 Reactor Configuration

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition

  • A pure H₂ atmosphere that leads to the coexistence of ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂.

- **Observations:** This activation time is optimized to achieve thorough reduction and carburization while preventing overgrowth or sintering of the active particles.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural evolution of the Fe/SiO₂ catalysts were determined primarily by X-ray Diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer Emission Spectroscopy (MES). These techniques enable identification and quantification of iron oxide and carbide phases formed under different pretreatment and reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis revealed that the catalyst phases depend on the carburization atmosphere: χ‑Fe₅C₂ is formed under syngas, Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ under CO, and ε‑Fe₂C with χ‑Fe₅C₂ under H₂. MES further quantified the phase composition, confirming the coexistence of these carbides and tracking the transformation from Fe₂O₃ to α-Fe and then to specific carbides. Crystallite sizes, estimated via the Scherrer equation, were χ‑Fe₅C₂ ~7–7.5 nm, Fe₇C₃ ~27.5–28.2 nm, and ε‑Fe₂C ~9.1–13.9 nm.
- **Observations:** The selective formation of iron carbide phases is achieved by tuning the carburization conditions. The silica support stabilizes these phases, preventing sintering and aggregation during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS).

#### 6.1.2 Local Atomic Structure and Elemental Composition
- **Description:** X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS) and MES were employed to probe the local coordination environment and valence states of iron species. XAFS provides coordination numbers and bond distances, while MES distinguishes between iron oxides and carbides.
- **Findings:** XAFS fitting for the FeSi-CO catalyst indicated Fe–O, Fe–C, and Fe–Fe shells, with Fe–Fe bond distances of 2.17 Å for χ‑Fe₅C₂ and 2.06 Å for Fe₇C₃. MES at 11 K quantified the relative amounts of each phase, supporting the XRD assignments and revealing the presence of both iron carbides and minor oxide components.
- **Observations:** The coexistence of multiple carbide phases and their local environments directly correlate with catalytic performance, with Fe₇C₃ exhibiting the highest intrinsic activity and ε‑Fe₂C the lowest methane selectivity.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), including HRTEM and STEM with EDX mapping, was used to assess particle morphology, size, and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed that the fresh catalyst comprised finely dispersed nanoparticles (~5.5 nm). After reaction, a bimodal distribution was observed: small χ‑Fe₅C₂ particles (~7–7.5 nm) and larger Fe₇C₃ (~27.5 nm) or ε‑Fe₂C (~13.9 nm) particles, depending on the pretreatment. STEM-EDX mapping confirmed uniform dispersion of iron and the presence of amorphous oxide shells.
- **Observations:** The silica support ensures high dispersion and phase stability, with minimal aggregation even after FTS. The observed particle sizes are critical for phase selectivity and catalytic behavior.

#### 6.1.4 Phase Distribution and Carburization Degree
- **Description:** MES provided quantitative evaluation of phase distribution and carburization degree, essential for correlating structure with catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** MES spectra quantified the proportions of χ‑Fe₅C₂, Fe₇C₃, and ε‑Fe₂C in each catalyst after specific treatments. Controlled carburization yielded targeted phase compositions, with syngas favoring χ‑Fe₅C₂, CO favoring Fe₇C₃, and H₂ favoring ε‑Fe₂C.
- **Observations:** The degree of carburization and resulting phase distribution are key determinants of FTS performance, enabling precise tuning of activity and selectivity through synthesis conditions.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet CO flow rates, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (260 °C, 3.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 4000 h⁻¹), the following CO conversion values were observed:
  - FeSi-CO (CO-pretreated, Fe₇C₃ + χ‑Fe₅C₂): **50.8%**
  - FeSi-H₂ (H₂-pretreated, ε‑Fe₂C + χ‑Fe₅C₂): **33.1%** (up to 44.8% under adjusted conditions)
  - FeSi-syn (syngas-pretreated, χ‑Fe₅C₂): **22.3%** (up to 49.2% under adjusted conditions)
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of Fe₇C₃ correlates with the highest CO conversion, while χ‑Fe₅C₂ alone yields lower conversion. Adjusting reaction conditions (e.g., space velocity) can enhance conversion, but phase composition remains the dominant factor.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity (C mol %) for each catalyst:
  - FeSi-CO: **9.7%**
  - FeSi-H₂: **4.6%** (5.7% under alternate conditions)
  - FeSi-syn: **2.8%**, **3.6%**, **4.8%** (varied conditions)
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts with ε‑Fe₂C (FeSi-H₂) exhibit the lowest CO₂ selectivity, suggesting suppressed water–gas shift activity compared to Fe₇C₃- or χ‑Fe₅C₂-containing catalysts.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity (wt %) for each catalyst:
  - FeSi-CO: **19.2%**
  - FeSi-H₂: **10.6%**
  - FeSi-syn: **13.2%**
- **Observations and Trends:** The lowest CH₄ selectivity is achieved with ε‑Fe₂C (FeSi-H₂), indicating enhanced C–C coupling and preference for longer-chain hydrocarbons. Catalyst phase, rather than conversion level or space velocity, primarily determines CH₄ selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, reflecting chain growth efficiency.
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivity (wt %) for each catalyst:
  - FeSi-CO: **61.2%**
  - FeSi-H₂: **74.4%**
  - FeSi-syn: **68.2%**
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest C₅⁺ selectivity is observed for ε‑Fe₂C-containing catalysts, consistent with suppressed methane formation and enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination in FTS, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher tendency for long-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Data:** Specific α values are not numerically reported; however, higher C₅⁺ selectivity in ε‑Fe₂C and Fe₇C₃ phases implies higher α factors.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts with ε‑Fe₂C and Fe₇C₃ phases favor higher α, correlating with increased selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time; deactivation rate is typically defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** No explicit deactivation rates are reported, but the catalysts demonstrated stable performance with minimal phase transformation during FTS.
- **Observations and Trends:** Controlled carburization and silica support effectively minimize sintering and phase changes, ensuring sustained activity and selectivity throughout prolonged operation.
