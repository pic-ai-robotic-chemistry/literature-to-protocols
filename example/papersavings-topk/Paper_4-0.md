## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe₃O₄@χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalysts is achieved via a controlled surface reconstruction process using pre‑synthesized Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals as templates. The approach guarantees reproducibility through precise control of reaction parameters and sequence of steps, as detailed below.

#### Fe₃O₄ Nanocrystal Preparation
• The Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals are initially prepared by dissolving iron(III) acetylacetonate [Fe(acac)₃] and 4‑biphenylcarboxylic acid in benzyl ether and oleic acid.  
• The precursor solution is heated at a controlled rate of 10 °C min⁻¹ until reaching ca. 290 °C, then maintained for 30 minutes.  
• The resulting nanocrystals serve as a uniform template, facilitating consistent catalytic behavior by providing a controlled core morphology.

#### Catalyst Template Loading and H₂ Reduction
• Approximately 50 mg of the pre‑synthesized Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals are loaded into a fixed‑bed reactor with an inner diameter of 9 mm.  
• A hydrogen (H₂) reduction step is performed at 1 bar. The reactor is operated at 270 °C under a hydrogen flow rate of 100 mL min⁻¹, employing a heating ramp of 1 °C min⁻¹ and sustaining these conditions for 10 hours.  
• This step ensures the removal of residual organic species and sets the stage for controlled surface transformations.

#### Surface Reconstruction via Syngas Exposure
• Following reduction, a surface reconstruction is carried out under a syngas mixture (32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, 4 vol% Ar) at 20 bar pressure.  
• The reconstruction is maintained at 270 °C with a space velocity of 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹ for 20 hours, enabling the conversion of the Fe₃O₄ core surface to form the χ‑Fe₅C₂ shell.  
• Inert nitrogen (N₂) is subsequently introduced to replace the reactive gases, thereby protecting the catalyst during storage and transfer to a glove box.

This method, with its clearly defined steps and precise parameter control, assures that both the nanocube and octahedron morphologies yield the desirable core–shell architectures, as evidenced by replicated structure and catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ Nanocubes

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g Fe(acac)₃, 2.5 g 4‐biphenylcarboxylic acid, 100 mL benzyl ether, 20 mL oleic acid.
- **Conditions**: Degas at 120 °C for 30 minutes under N₂; heat to ca. 290 °C at a rate of 10 °C min⁻¹ with constant stirring.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve Fe(acac)₃ and 4‐biphenylcarboxylic acid in benzyl ether and oleic acid to form a homogenous solution.
  - Degas the solution at 120 °C under N₂ flow.
  - Heat the solution to 290 °C and maintain for 30 minutes, ensuring the nucleation and controlled growth of Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals.
- **Key Steps**: Emphasize the role of Fe(acac)₃ as the iron source and the use of benzyl ether (high-boiling) and oleic acid (stabilizer) for controlled morphology.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: 50 mg of pre-synthesized Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals.
- **Conditions**: Load into a fixed-bed reactor (9 mm inner diameter); ensure uniform dispersion.
- **Process**:
  - Transfer Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals into the reactor.
  - Set up gas flow configuration with 100 mL min⁻¹ H₂.
  - Ensure reproducible loading to maintain catalyst consistency.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Purge reactor system with He or N₂ to remove any residual solvents or organic species.
  - Confirm removal of impurities prior to further treatment.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the material at 100 °C for 2 hours in a controlled oven.
- **Calcination**: 
  - Perform H₂ reduction with a heating ramp of 1 °C min⁻¹ until reaching 270 °C.
  - Maintain these conditions for 10 hours at 1 bar H₂ to ensure surface restructuring.

##### Step 5: Activation (Surface Reconstruction)
- **Conditions**: Switch to syngas (32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, 4 vol% Ar) at 20 bar.
- **Process**:
  - Subject the reduced Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals to a 20-hour treatment at 270 °C.
  - After reaction, flush with N₂ and transfer to an N₂-filled glove box for storage.
- **Key Steps**: This step promotes the formation of the outer χ-Fe₅C₂ shell, with critical control of gas composition and flow (space velocity of 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹) ensuring selective exposure of active {202} facets.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is executed in two sequential stages to prepare Fischer–Tropsch catalysts comprising Fe₃O₄@χ‑Fe₅C₂ nanostructures. The first stage accomplishes the reduction of precursor Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals to metallic Fe, while the subsequent stage induces surface reconstruction and carburization to yield the active χ‑Fe₅C₂ shell.

#### 5.1.1 Hydrogen Reduction Step

• **Definition:**  
This step facilitates the reduction of Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals, effectively removing oxygen species and generating a metallic Fe core. Uniform reduction is critical for achieving high metal dispersion and serves as the foundation for subsequent surface modification.

• **Experimental Conditions:**  
The catalyst is heated with a controlled ramp of 1 °C·min⁻¹ to 270 °C. At this temperature, it is maintained for 10 hours under a pure H₂ atmosphere at 1 bar pressure. The hydrogen gas is introduced at a flow rate of 100 mL·min⁻¹, ensuring an even and complete reduction across all catalyst particles.

• **Observations:**  
The controlled thermal ramp and gas flow enable thorough reduction without inducing significant sintering. This treatment results in a uniform metallic phase that is essential for preserving the structural integrity of the catalyst during further activation stages.

#### 5.1.2 Syngas Surface Reconstruction Step

• **Definition:**  
Following reduction, the catalyst undergoes a surface reconstruction process that promotes carburization. This stage transforms the metallic Fe into a core–shell structure, forming the active χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase with well-defined exposed facets, which are crucial for the catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

• **Experimental Conditions:**  
The activation is conducted at 270 °C under a syngas environment maintained at 20 bar. The gas mixture is composed of 32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar. The catalyst is treated under these conditions for 20 hours, with a controlled space-time velocity of 2400 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹.

• **Observations:**  
The syngas treatment drives the carburization process with precision, yielding a stable core–shell morphology. The inclusion of 4 vol% Ar ensures consistency in the gas mixture and acts as an inert internal standard. Post-activation, the catalyst is transferred under an inert N₂ atmosphere to prevent oxidation, ensuring reproducibility and optimal performance during subsequent reaction testing.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a key parameter that drives the activation of syngas molecules and influences the kinetics of Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. In this study, the reaction is maintained at a fixed temperature of 270 °C. This temperature is critical for ensuring efficient catalyst activation and promoting the necessary surface reactions. Observations indicate that under these conditions, Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes exhibit excellent stability, with CO conversion fluctuating within 1% over a 100-hour test, and the catalyst’s cubic morphology and shell integrity are well preserved.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure plays a vital role in controlling the concentration of reactants, thereby affecting conversion efficiency and product selectivity. The Fischer–Tropsch tests are conducted at a controlled pressure of 20 bar in a fixed-bed reactor. This elevated pressure enhances the availability of active gas molecules at the catalyst surface, promoting higher reaction rates and consistent syngas conversion. The robust performance under these conditions confirms that the setup provides a highly reproducible environment for catalytic evaluation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide is fundamental in determining the hydrocarbon distribution and the preferred reaction pathway. In the described experimental setup, the syngas composition consists of 64 vol% H₂ and 32 vol% CO, which implies an effective H₂/CO ratio of 2:1. This ratio is crucial as it steers the catalytic process towards either higher methane selectivity or the production of longer-chain hydrocarbons. Controlled adjustments in this parameter have demonstrated direct impacts on product selectivity.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
Space-time velocity (STV) is defined as the ratio of syngas flow rate to the catalyst mass, affecting reactant contact time and overall conversion efficiency. The reported gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) in the study is 2400 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹. This condition is optimized to balance sufficient syngas-catalyst interactions while preventing excessive residence time that may otherwise lead to catalyst deactivation. The selected STV contributes to the reproducible performance observed across multiple catalytic tests.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. XRD identifies crystalline phases and lattice matching, while Mössbauer quantifies iron species and their oxidation states.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed distinct peaks for both Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂, confirming the formation of a core–shell structure. The (400) peak of Fe₃O₄ and the (202) peak of χ-Fe₅C₂ were used to differentiate the core and shell, respectively. Lattice spacings of 0.21 nm (Fe₃O₄(400)) and 0.22 nm (χ-Fe₅C₂(202)) indicated a small mismatch (f = 4.65%), supporting epitaxial shell growth. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the phase composition: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes contained 63.4 wt% Fe₃O₄, 33.2 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂, and 3.4 wt% Fe(II)/Fe(III) species; octahedra contained 65.1 wt% Fe₃O₄ and 29.5 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The coexistence of Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂ phases, with a well-matched interface, is critical for the stability and activity of the catalysts.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Distribution and Core–Shell Architecture
- **Description:** Elemental mapping and spatial distribution were analyzed using STEM-EDX and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS), providing insight into the core–shell structure.
- **Findings:** STEM-EDX mapping showed Fe distributed throughout the particles, O concentrated in the core, and C enriched in the shell. EELS confirmed these distributions, with the core region containing Fe and O (Fe₃O₄) and the shell region containing Fe and C (χ-Fe₅C₂).
- **Observations:** These results confirm the successful synthesis of Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ core–shell nanostructures for both nanocubes and octahedra.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were measured by N₂ physisorption using the BET method, with additional pore analysis by BJH.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area was 20.4 m² g⁻¹ for nanocubes and 22.0 m² g⁻¹ for octahedra. Both morphologies exhibited nonporous surface layers. After 100 h on stream, spent octahedra developed mesopores with an average diameter of 16.0 nm.
- **Observations:** The similar surface areas suggest that catalytic differences arise from facet exposure rather than textural properties. The formation of mesopores in spent octahedra indicates structural degradation during reaction.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Facet Exposure
- **Description:** Morphology and exposed facets were characterized by TEM and HAADF-STEM, with crystallographic analysis via FFT.
- **Findings:** Nanocubes displayed uniform cubic shapes with χ-Fe₅C₂ shells exposing {202} facets, while octahedra exhibited octahedral shapes with {112} facets. The average nanocube size was 40.1 ± 3.8 nm. Core–shell structures were clearly resolved in both morphologies.
- **Observations:** The selective exposure of {202} facets in nanocubes and {112} facets in octahedra underpins the facet-dependent catalytic behavior observed in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and Reaction Mechanism Probing
- **Description:** In-situ DRIFTS was employed to monitor surface species and probe CO dissociation mechanisms under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS spectra revealed peaks for gaseous CO, linearly adsorbed CO, and CO₂, indicating direct CO dissociation. Upon H₂ introduction, additional peaks for CH₃*, CH₂*, and CHO* appeared, with CHO* observed only on nanocubes, evidencing a hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway. Octahedra primarily exhibited direct CO dissociation.
- **Observations:** The presence of hydrogen-assisted intermediates on nanocubes correlates with their higher activity and stability, while octahedra favor direct CO dissociation and are more prone to deactivation.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Fe Dispersion
- **Description:** CO pulse chemisorption quantified the dispersion of surface Fe atoms, relevant for calculating turnover frequencies.
- **Findings:** Chemisorption experiments, conducted after He cleaning at 270 °C and CO pulsing at 50 °C, enabled accurate determination of surface Fe atom numbers for both morphologies.
- **Observations:** These measurements provided the basis for comparing intrinsic catalytic activities (TOF) between nanocubes and octahedra.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO molecules transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (20 bar, 270 °C, 64% H₂/32% CO/4% Ar, 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹), Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a CO conversion of 45.4%, while octahedra/SiC reached 21.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes consistently outperformed octahedra, with more than double the CO conversion under identical conditions. This superior activity is attributed to the exposure of the {202} facet in nanocubes, which facilitates a more favorable reaction pathway.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC, CH₄ selectivity is 15.5 C%. Octahedra/SiC favor higher methane production compared to nanocubes/SiC.
- **Observations and Trends:** The octahedral morphology, exposing the {112} facet, promotes methane formation due to a lower surface CO/H₂ ratio and a lower energy barrier for CH₂* hydrogenation. In contrast, nanocubes suppress methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms among all hydrocarbon products:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\sum_{n=5}^{\infty} n \cdot F_{\text{C}_n\text{H}_m,\text{out}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} i \cdot F_{\text{C}_i\text{H}_m,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC exhibit a C₅⁺ selectivity of 44.8 C%, while octahedra/SiC show 27.6 C%. For comparison, χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC yield 34.9 C% in the C₅–C₁₂ range.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes promote longer hydrocarbon chain growth, consistent with their higher α factor and facet-dependent mechanism.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from Anderson–Schulz–Flory statistics, reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
  - Values range from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (infinite chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values are 0.66 for nanocubes/SiC, 0.62 for octahedra/SiC, and 0.63 for χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher α value for nanocubes correlates with enhanced selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended operation. Deactivation is indicated by a decline in conversion.
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC maintained CO conversion within 1% fluctuation over 100 hours. Octahedra/SiC showed a decrease from 20.1% to 11.7% over the same period.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes demonstrate exceptional stability, retaining morphology and activity, while octahedra suffer from significant deactivation due to carbon deposition and structural collapse.

#### 6.2.6 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF measures the number of CO molecules converted per surface Fe atom per hour, providing an intrinsic activity metric.
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC achieved a TOF of 645.9 h⁻¹, compared to 372.7 h⁻¹ for octahedra/SiC.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher TOF for nanocubes is attributed to the exposure of the {202} facet, which enables a lower-energy, hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway.

---
**Summary:**  
Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC exhibit superior catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, with higher CO conversion, greater selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons, higher α factor, and remarkable stability compared to octahedra/SiC. These advantages are directly linked to the facet-dependent reaction mechanisms enabled by the nanocube morphology.
