## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines two detailed synthesis routes used for Fe-based catalysts: a hydrothermal pyrolysis method for MOF‑derived Fe@C core–shell catalysts and a co‑precipitation process for an unsupported‑Fe reference. Both approaches emphasize reproducibility through clearly defined steps, reagents, and equipment.

#### Hydrothermal Pyrolysis Method (MOF‑derived Fe@C Catalysts)
1. Precursor Synthesis – MIL‑101(Fe)  
   • Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, terephthalic acid, nitric acid, and N,N‑dimethylformamide (DMF).  
   • Conditions: Conducted at 120 °C for 48 h in a Teflon‑lined autoclave with 70 ml of DMF, ensuring a controlled hydrothermal environment.  
   • Observation: Formation of a well‑defined metal–organic framework (MOF) precursor with high surface area.

2. Pyrolysis – Catalyst Formation  
   • Process: The MIL‑101(Fe) precursor is subjected to pyrolysis in a tube furnace under a 2% C₂H₂/helium flow.  
   • Temperature & Duration: Pyrolyzed at 500, 600, or 700 °C for 1 h, where the pyrolysis temperature dictates the carbon shell structure; 500 °C yields an amorphous shell, 600 °C forms a 3–5 layer graphene structure, and 700 °C produces a well‑oriented graphite shell.  
   • Note: The carbon filament from C₂H₂ assists in reduction and carburization, generating a predominant h‑Fe₃C phase.

3. Post‑treatment (Fe@C‑600)  
   • Additional step: A hydrogen reduction is performed at 400 °C for 24 h (with a flow rate of 50 ml/min) to yield Fe@C‑600‑H₂, modifying the phase composition and promoting a change towards α‑Fe.

#### Co‑precipitation Method (Unsupported‑Fe Catalyst)
1. Precursor Mixing  
   • Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ (1 mol/L) and ammonia solution (3 mol/L) are mixed dropwise while maintaining the pH at 8.2–8.5.  
   • Conditions: Carried out at 80 ± 1 °C under continuous stirring, with a pH meter monitoring the environment.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   • Process: The reaction mixture is aged for 2 h to allow uniform deposition and formation of a visible precipitate.  

3. Drying and Calcination  
   • Drying: The precipitate is dried at 80 °C overnight to remove residual solvents.  
   • Calcination: The dried material is subsequently calcined for 5 h at 500 °C in static air, followed by a reduction step in hydrogen (100 ml/min) at 350 °C for 24 h.

### Comparative Analysis  
• The pyrolysis method integrates ligand decomposition and carbon deposition in fewer steps, offering scalability and a distinct control over the active carbon shell morphology.  
• The co‑precipitation approach is cost‑effective and ensures uniform metal dispersion, though it may require additional purification to remove unreacted species.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@C Catalysts from MIL-101(Fe) Precursor  
##### Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 mL nitric acid, and 70 mL DMF.  
- **Conditions**: Stirring the mixture for 2 hours at room temperature followed by transfer into a Teflon-lined steel autoclave, then hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 48 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve all reagents in DMF and stir until a homogeneous solution is obtained.  
  - Seal the solution in an autoclave and heat at 120 °C for 48 hours.  
  - Upon cooling, wash the resulting MIL-101(Fe) three times with DMF and ethanol consecutively and dry at 80 °C overnight.  
- **Key Steps**: Emphasize proper mixing and controlled hydrothermal conditions to secure reproducible MOF formation.

##### Catalyst Assembly and Pyrolysis  
- **Reagents**: MIL-101(Fe) precursor and a 2% C₂H₂/helium gas mixture.  
- **Conditions**: Pyrolysis in a tube furnace with a heating rate of 5 °C/min at three setpoints: 500 °C, 600 °C, and 700 °C, each maintained for 1 hour.  
- **Process**:  
  - Place the dried MIL-101(Fe) in the tube furnace.  
  - Pyrolyze under a continuous flow of 2% C₂H₂ in helium to facilitate carbon deposition and formation of a protective shell around the iron core.  
- **Key Steps**: Strict temperature control is critical as it determines the carbon shell structure: amorphous for Fe@C-500, a few-layer graphene for Fe@C-600, and well-oriented graphite for Fe@C-700.

##### Post-Processing – H₂ Reduction  
- **For Fe@C-600-H₂**:  
  - **Conditions**: Reduce the Fe@C-600 catalyst under a flow of H₂ (50 mL/min) at 400 °C for 24 hours.  
  - **Process**: Subject the pyrolyzed catalyst to hydrogen post-treatment to enhance the catalytic phase, transitioning h‑Fe₃C to a more pronounced α‑Fe fraction.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the Fe@C catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Co Kα radiation (λ = 0.179 nm), operated at 35 kV and 40 mA, with a scan step of 0.03° over 2θ = 10–90°. Phase identification was supported by reference patterns (JCPDS).
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that Fe@C-600 contains h‑Fe₃C (JCPDS 35‑0772), graphite carbon (JCPDS 41‑1487), and α‑Fe (JCPDS 06–0696). Fe@C-700 shows pronounced h‑Fe₃C and graphite carbon peaks, while Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe display α‑Fe and graphite carbon or only α‑Fe, respectively. Increasing pyrolysis temperature enhances h‑Fe₃C formation, as evidenced by sharper and more intense diffraction lines.
- **Observations:** The transformation from amorphous carbon to graphene and then to graphite shells with increasing temperature is corroborated by XRD. H₂ post-treatment of Fe@C-600 results in the disappearance of h‑Fe₃C peaks, indicating complete reduction to α‑Fe.

#### 6.1.2 Phase Composition (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES) at room temperature quantified the relative content of iron phases, using α-Fe foil for velocity calibration and referencing isomer shifts to α-Fe.
- **Findings:** The h‑Fe₃C content increases with pyrolysis temperature: 68.2% in Fe@C-500, 87.2% in Fe@C-600, and 95.5% in Fe@C-700. Fe@C-600-H₂ contains only α‑Fe after H₂ reduction.
- **Observations:** Higher h‑Fe₃C content correlates with improved catalyst stability and selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.3 Carbon Shell Structure (TEM, HRTEM, Raman)
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HRTEM) at 200 kV and Raman spectroscopy (532 nm excitation) were used to analyze the morphology and ordering of the carbon shell.
- **Findings:** HRTEM images show Fe@C-500 has an amorphous carbon shell, Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ possess 3–5 layer graphene shells (interplanar spacing ≈0.346 nm), and Fe@C-700 features a well-oriented graphite shell. Raman spectra confirm these assignments: Fe@C-500 exhibits a weak, single 2D peak; Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ display four 2D peaks; Fe@C-700 shows two merged 2D peaks. The D/G intensity ratio (ID/IG) decreases with increasing graphitization.
- **Observations:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 provides optimal protection against nanoparticle agglomeration and oxidation, as confirmed by post-reaction TEM.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity (N₂ Physisorption)
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption measurements provided BET surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution.
- **Findings:** MIL-101(Fe) exhibits a high surface area (1632 m²/g), pore volume (0.83 cm³/g), and average pore size (1.9 nm). After pyrolysis, Fe@C catalysts show reduced surface areas (135–291 m²/g) and pore volumes (0.23–0.28 cm³/g). Unsupported-Fe has a much lower surface area (15.9 m²/g).
- **Observations:** MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts retain higher porosity than unsupported-Fe, facilitating better reactant diffusion and catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Morphology and Particle Size (SEM, TEM)
- **Description:** SEM (FEI Quanta 499 FEG) and TEM analyses characterized particle morphology and size distribution.
- **Findings:** Fe@C catalysts display uniformly distributed nanoparticles encapsulated within the carbon shell, with minimal aggregation even after extended reaction times. Unsupported-Fe shows larger, aggregated particles and poor stability.
- **Observations:** The carbon shell, especially the graphene type, effectively inhibits nanoparticle sintering, maintaining catalyst integrity during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is typically calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (300 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.0), CO conversion at 24 h on stream was: Fe@C-500 (64.1%), Fe@C-600 (65.0%), Fe@C-700 (73.2%), Fe@C-600-H₂ (78.7%), and unsupported-Fe (75.3%). At 184 h, values were: Fe@C-500 (55.7%), Fe@C-600 (74.5%), Fe@C-700 (58.5%), Fe@C-600-H₂ (81.5%), and unsupported-Fe (39.2%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@C-600-H₂ consistently exhibited the highest CO conversion, with Fe@C-600 also maintaining high conversion and stability over time. Unsupported-Fe showed significant deactivation, as evidenced by the sharp decline in conversion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h, CO₂ selectivity was: Fe@C-500 (35.2%), Fe@C-600 (33.3%), Fe@C-700 (33.9%), Fe@C-600-H₂ (37.3%), and unsupported-Fe (38.5%). At 184 h, Fe@C-600 (33.7%) and Fe@C-600-H₂ (36.9%) maintained similar values.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@C-600 demonstrated the lowest CO₂ selectivity, indicating more efficient hydrocarbon production with minimal CO₂ byproduct.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h, CH₄ selectivity was: Fe@C-600 (10.5%), Fe@C-600-H₂ (23.4%), and unsupported-Fe (28.7%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@C-600 significantly suppressed methane formation compared to unsupported-Fe and Fe@C-600-H₂, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon synthesis.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity denotes the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of long-chain hydrocarbon production:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h, C₅⁺ selectivity was: Fe@C-600 (60.4%), Fe@C-500 (59.0%), Fe@C-700 (59.7%), Fe@C-600-H₂ (38.6%), and unsupported-Fe (14.7%).
- **Observations and Trends:** h‑Fe₃C–dominated Fe@C catalysts (especially Fe@C-600) achieved high C₅⁺ selectivity, while Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe favored shorter-chain products.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon growth, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution:
  \[
  \alpha = \text{slope of } \ln(W_n/n) \text{ vs. } n
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** h‑Fe₃C–dominated Fe@C catalysts exhibited α values of 0.67–0.69, higher than unpromoted Fe@C catalysts (0.45–0.48).
- **Observations and Trends:** High α values correlate with enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity and are attributed to the dominance of h‑Fe₃C.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}}} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Deactivation rates: Fe@C-500 (13.1%), Fe@C-700 (20.1%), unsupported-Fe (47.9%). Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ showed minimal deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ effectively inhibited nanoparticle agglomeration, resulting in superior catalyst stability compared to amorphous or graphite shells and unsupported-Fe.

#### 6.2.7 Additional Performance Indicators
- **Iron Time Yield (FTY):** Defined as μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹, FTY was highest for Fe@C-600-H₂, reflecting increased activity post-H₂ treatment.
- **Olefin/Paraffin Ratio:** Fe@C-600 maintained a higher olefin/paraffin ratio in C₂–C₄ products compared to Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe, indicating favorable selectivity for valuable olefins.

**Overall, the combination of a graphene carbon shell and h‑Fe₃C phase in Fe@C-600 delivers optimal FTS performance, balancing high activity, long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity, and catalyst stability.**
