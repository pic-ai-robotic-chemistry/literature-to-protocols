## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis method for Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocrystals is executed in a two‐stage process using a fixed-bed reactor. This approach enables precise control over catalyst morphology (nanocubes with {202} facets and octahedra with {112} facets) and ensures reproducibility across experiments.

#### Step 1: Catalyst Precursor Loading
• Precursor: Load 50 mg of preformed Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals (or octahedra) supported on SiC.  
• Equipment: Fixed-bed reactor with an inner diameter of 9 mm, which ensures controlled gas distribution and catalyst bed uniformity.  
• Note: Precise loading is critical for maintaining consistent reaction conditions and promoting controlled morphological evolution.

#### Step 2: H₂ Reduction
1. Place the loaded catalyst into the reactor and initiate the reduction under 1 bar H₂.
2. Reaction conditions include:  
 • Temperature: 270 °C  
 • Duration: 10 h  
 • Heating ramp: 1 °C min⁻¹  
 • Gas flow rate: 100 mL min⁻¹  
3. Observation: The gradual reduction of Fe₃O₄ leads to a uniform transformation that primes the catalyst for subsequent surface reconstruction.

#### Step 3: Syngas Surface Reconstruction
• Immediately following reduction, switch to a syngas atmosphere to promote the formation of the χ‑Fe₅C₂ shell.  
1. Reaction conditions:  
 • Pressure: 20 bar  
 • Temperature: 270 °C  
 • Duration: 20 h  
 • Syngas Composition: 32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar  
 • Space velocity: 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹  
2. Purpose: This controlled carburization step reconstructs the catalyst surface while preserving the core–shell structure and determining the exposed facets.

#### Step 4: Post‑processing and Passivation
• After the syngas treatment, introduce inert N₂ to halt the reaction and protect the active phases.  
• Transfer the catalyst into an N₂-filled glove box to prevent oxidation and preserve the catalyst integrity prior to characterization.

This comprehensive two‐stage process, employing H₂ reduction followed by syngas treatment, ensures controlled phase transformation and morphology tuning. The method’s robust design enhances reproducibility and scalability while allowing detailed comparative analysis of synthesis advantages and potential drawbacks related to reactor configuration, temperature control, and gas flow management.

- **Reaction mixture**: Mixed by stirring at 800 rpm for 30 minutes at 120 °C under a nitrogen (N₂) atmosphere. The reagents were degassed under N₂ flow before heating to ensure an inert environment throughout the process.

Finally, mixing under controlled temperature and inert atmosphere guarantees that the reagents achieve a homogeneous solution or suspension. This detailed methodological approach is integral to obtaining the desired core–shell structure of Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂, providing a replicable protocol for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst preparation.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ Nanocrystals (Nanocubes and Octahedra supported on SiC)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • For Nanocubes: 1.4 g Fe(acac)₃ and 1.0 g 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid dissolved in 20.0 mL BE and 2.5 mL OA.  
  • For Octahedra: 1.0 g Fe(acac)₃ with a mixture of 20.0 mL BE, 2.3 mL OAm, and 1.6 mL OA.  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Degas at 120 °C for 30 minutes under nitrogen.  
  • Heat to 290 °C at a rate of 10 °C min⁻¹, then maintain temperature for 30 minutes with stirring at 300 rpm.
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve the raw precursors in the specified solvent mixtures to form homogeneous solutions.  
  2. Degas the solutions under inert conditions to remove moisture and volatile impurities.  
  3. Heat the mixtures gradually to the target temperature without pH adjustments, ensuring controlled nucleation and growth of Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Reagents and Conditions**:  
  • Load 50 mg of the prepared Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals into a fixed-bed reactor (inner diameter 9 mm).  
- **Process**:  
  1. For octahedra synthesis, precipitate the particles with ethanol, wash three times with hexane, and re-disperse in hexane.
  2. Ensure uniform distribution by gentle stirring during loading.

##### Step 3: Drying, Calcination & Activation
- **Drying**:  
  • Degas the loaded catalyst at 120 °C for 30 minutes to remove residual solvents.
- **Activation – Reduction**:  
  • Reduce the Fe₃O₄ under 1 bar H₂ at 270 °C for 10 hours with a controlled gas flow of 100 mL min⁻¹ and a ramp rate of 1 °C min⁻¹.
- **Surface Reconstruction**:  
  • Switch to syngas (32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, 4 vol% Ar) at 20 bar, maintain 270 °C for 20 hours with a space velocity of 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹.
- **Post-Reaction Handling**:  
  • Immediately purge with inert N₂ to protect the catalyst, then store in a glove box.

##### Key Steps & Caution
- Ensure all precursor quantities and heating profiles are strictly followed for reproducibility.
- Monitor temperature and gas composition carefully during reduction and reconstruction to safeguard the catalyst’s core–shell structure and facet integrity.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation procedure was conducted in two distinct stages to ensure the proper formation of the χ-Fe₅C₂ phase from preformed Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals. Each stage was carefully designed to balance catalyst reduction and surface reconstruction while preserving the core–shell structure.

#### 5.1.1 Reduction Stage
During the initial reduction phase, 50 mg of Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals (either nanocubes or octahedra) were loaded into a fixed-bed reactor (inner diameter: 9 mm). The reactor was purged with H₂ and heated using a controlled ramp of 1 °C/min until reaching 270 °C. This precise heating pattern minimizes thermal shock and ensures uniform reduction. Once the temperature was achieved, the catalyst was exposed to a 1 bar H₂ atmosphere with a gas flow rate of 100 mL/min for 10 hours. This step is critical for reducing Fe₃O₄ and preparing the surface for subsequent carburization.

#### 5.1.2 Surface Reconstruction Stage
Following the reduction, the catalyst underwent surface reconstruction under syngas to induce carburization and controlled oxidation, forming the desired iron carbide phase. The same temperature (270 °C) was maintained, but the reaction pressure was increased to 20 bar. The syngas composition was precisely controlled at 32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar. A space-time velocity of 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹ was applied to ensure uniform gas exposure over the catalyst surface during the 20-hour treatment. Elevated pressure and the specific gas mixture drive CO dissociation and carburization, forming χ-Fe₅C₂ while concurrently supporting reduction processes.

#### 5.1.3 Post-Activation Handling
Immediately after the high-pressure syngas treatment, inert N₂ was introduced to the reactor. This step prevents any further reaction, thereby preserving the catalyst’s active core–shell structure prior to subsequent characterization. The controlled conditions throughout both activation phases play a pivotal role in achieving high catalyst performance with a uniform dispersion of active sites and minimal sintering.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was carried out under a defined set of parameters to ensure reproducibility and optimal catalytic performance. Detailed below are the key experimental conditions along with their roles in governing the reaction kinetics, catalyst behavior, and product selectivity.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is the operational condition at which the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis occurs, crucial for CO and H₂ activation and the subsequent hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was maintained at 270 °C.
- **Observations:** At this temperature, the catalyst exhibited favorable kinetics, lowering the activation energy for CO dissociation particularly on the {202} facets of the Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes. The elevated temperature contributed to a dynamic balance between carburization and oxidation, which is integral for sustained catalyst performance over prolonged periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure is the total system pressure that influences the concentration of reactants and the equilibrium between different reaction pathways.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was conducted at a total pressure of 20 bar.
- **Observations:** This high pressure enhanced the syngas concentration, facilitating improved CO conversion and promoting the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺). The 20 bar setting was critical in achieving the desired product selectivity, as it favored heavier hydrocarbon formation in accordance with Anderson–Schulz–Flory statistics.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio represents the proportion of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed syngas and directly affects the pathways of hydrocarbon synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The syngas comprised 64 vol% H₂, 32 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar, corresponding to a H₂/CO molar ratio of 2:1.
- **Observations:** This ratio was optimal for balancing methane versus olefin formation, enhancing CO hydrogenation and ensuring effective chain growth while also enabling the use of Ar as an internal standard for product quantification.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity is defined as the volumetric gas flow rate per gram of catalyst, directly impacting the residence time of reactants over the catalyst surface.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A space velocity of 2400 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹ was employed.
- **Observations:** This condition ensured that the reactants were in contact with the catalyst for an appropriate duration, which effectively balanced CO conversion rates while preserving the catalyst’s structural integrity, as evident from the contrasting stability observed between the nanocubes and octahedra over extended periods.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are essential for identifying phase composition and transformations during synthesis and reaction, which directly impact catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that the initial Fe₃O₄ nanocubes and octahedra underwent partial transformation to χ-Fe₅C₂ after syngas treatment at 270 °C and 20 bar. Diffraction peaks corresponding to χ-Fe₅C₂ appeared after 5 h of syngas exposure, confirming the formation of the carbide shell. Quantitative XRD analysis indicated χ-Fe₅C₂ contents of 29.8 wt% (nanocubes) and 27.6 wt% (octahedra). Mössbauer spectroscopy corroborated these results, showing 33.2 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 63.4 wt% Fe₃O₄ in nanocubes, and 29.5 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 65.1 wt% Fe₃O₄ in octahedra, with minor Fe(II)/Fe(III) species (~3.4 wt%). FFT analysis of HAADF-STEM images further identified the exposed facets: {202} for nanocubes and {112} for octahedra.
- **Observations:** The agreement between XRD and Mössbauer data confirms the reliability of phase quantification. The facet-dependent shell structure is directly linked to catalytic behavior.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Distribution
- **Description:** Elemental mapping and distribution were assessed using electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) and STEM energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), providing spatial resolution of Fe, O, and C within the core–shell architecture.
- **Findings:** EELS and STEM-EDS analyses demonstrated that the core region was rich in Fe and O (Fe₃O₄), while the shell was enriched in Fe and C (χ-Fe₅C₂). Fe was homogeneously distributed throughout the particles, with O and C localized to the core and shell, respectively.
- **Observations:** The clear segregation of elements supports the successful formation of the intended core–shell structure.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were measured by N₂ physisorption using the BET method for surface area and the BJH method for pore size distribution. These parameters influence reactant accessibility and catalyst stability.
- **Findings:** Both Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes and octahedra exhibited similar BET surface areas (20.4 m²/g and 22.0 m²/g, respectively) and were nonporous prior to reaction. After 100 h on stream, nanocubes remained nonporous, while octahedra developed mesopores with an average diameter of 16.0 nm.
- **Observations:** The emergence of mesoporosity in octahedra after reaction is associated with structural degradation, whereas nanocubes maintain their integrity.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphology and particle size were characterized by TEM and HAADF-STEM, which are critical for correlating structure with catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** TEM images confirmed uniform nanocube (40.1 ± 3.8 nm) and octahedra (41.2 ± 4.1 nm) sizes. HAADF-STEM revealed a core–shell structure with shell thicknesses of 2.0 nm (nanocubes) and 1.7 nm (octahedra). After 100 h, nanocubes retained their morphology, while octahedra exhibited voids and pore formation.
- **Observations:** The uniformity and stability of nanocubes underpin their superior catalytic durability.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Fe Dispersion
- **Description:** Surface Fe atom dispersion was quantified by CO pulse chemisorption, which is directly related to the number of active sites.
- **Findings:** Nanocubes exhibited a higher surface Fe dispersion (24.1 µmol g⁻¹) compared to octahedra (19.5 µmol g⁻¹).
- **Observations:** Higher dispersion in nanocubes contributes to their enhanced catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Chemistry and CO Dissociation Pathways
- **Description:** In-situ DRIFTS/FTIR was employed to probe CO adsorption and dissociation mechanisms on catalyst surfaces, elucidating the role of exposed facets.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS spectra at 270 °C showed that nanocubes facilitate both direct and hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation, while octahedra only support direct dissociation. The activation energy for CO dissociation was lower on nanocubes (68.6 kJ mol⁻¹) than on octahedra.
- **Observations:** The facet-dependent CO dissociation pathway is a key factor in the superior performance of nanocube catalysts.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (20 bar, 270 °C, 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹, syngas: 64% H₂, 32% CO, 4% Ar), Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a CO conversion of 45.4%, while octahedra/SiC reached 21.2%. In a 100-hour stability test, nanocubes maintained conversion within 1% fluctuation, whereas octahedra declined from 20.1% to 11.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior CO conversion of nanocubes is attributed to the exposure of {202} facets, which facilitate a hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway with lower activation energy. Octahedra, exposing {112} facets, exhibit lower activity and significant deactivation over time.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC exhibited the highest CH₄ selectivity at 15.5 C%. Among the core–shell catalysts, octahedra/SiC favored higher CH₄ production, while nanocubes/SiC suppressed CH₄ formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The facet-dependent behavior is evident, with nanocubes promoting longer-chain hydrocarbon formation at the expense of methane selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms, using:
  \[
  \text{C}_n\text{H}_m \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{n \times F_{\text{C}_n\text{H}_m,\text{out}}}{\sum_i i \times F_{\text{C}_i\text{H}_m,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC demonstrated higher selectivity toward C₅⁺ hydrocarbons compared to octahedra/SiC, especially at matched CO conversion levels.
- **Observations and Trends:** The {202} facet exposure in nanocubes enhances chain growth, favoring the production of longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model, indicates the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
  - α = 0.66 for nanocubes/SiC
  - α = 0.62 for octahedra/SiC
  - α = 0.63 for χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC
- **Experimental Data:** Values were obtained by fitting product distributions to the ASF model.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α for nanocubes reflects their enhanced ability to promote long-chain hydrocarbon formation, consistent with their facet-dependent reactivity.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC showed negligible deactivation (CO conversion stable within 1% over 100 h). Octahedra/SiC deactivated significantly, with conversion dropping from 20.1% to 11.7% in 100 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The stability of nanocubes is linked to preserved morphology and minimal carbon deposition, while octahedra suffer from structural collapse and increased carbon accumulation, as confirmed by TEM, Raman, and TGA analyses.

#### 6.2.6 Turnover Frequency (TOF) and Surface Fe Dispersion
- **Description:** TOF quantifies intrinsic activity per surface Fe atom, with surface Fe dispersion determined by CO pulse chemisorption.
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC: 24.1 µmol g⁻¹ surface Fe, TOF 645.9 h⁻¹; Octahedra/SiC: 19.5 µmol g⁻¹, TOF 372.7 h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher surface Fe dispersion and TOF in nanocubes correlate with their superior catalytic performance.
