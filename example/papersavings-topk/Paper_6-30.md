## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis methods detailed in the article focus on two primary routes: the coprecipitation-based preparation of the CoMnC catalyst and the subsequent polymerization of PDVB. Below is a comparative, step‑by‑step outline of each method, emphasizing reproducibility and experimental precision.

#### • CoMnC Catalyst via Coprecipitation
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Reagents: Cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O) and manganese(II) nitrate (Mn(NO₃)₂) are dissolved in deionized water.  
   - Process: Both metal precursor solutions are simultaneously dropwise added to a Na₂CO₃ solution maintained in a beaker equipped with a pH meter, ensuring the pH remains at approximately 8.  
   - Equipment: pH meter, magnetic stirrer, and temperature-controlled bath to hold the reaction at ~30°C.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - As the dropwise addition occurs, the formation of metal hydroxide precipitates is observed (evidenced by a color change and precipitate formation). This serves as the in situ precipitation–deposition step.

3. **Drying**  
   - Conditions: The washed precipitate is dried at 100°C for 10 hours to remove all residual moisture.

4. **Calcination**  
   - Conditions: The dry material is calcined at 330°C for 3 hours to convert the precursor oxide into a more stable phase.

5. **Post‑Treatment**  
   - Reduction: The calcined oxide is reduced in a H₂ atmosphere at 300°C for 5 hours to generate the active cobalt–manganese carbide (CoMnC) phase.

#### • PDVB Synthesis via Polymerization
1. **Initiation and Mixing**  
   - Reagents: Divinylbenzene is combined with AIBN, a radical initiator.  
   - Process: The mixture is stirred at room temperature for 1 hour to ensure homogeneity.

2. **Autoclave Treatment (Polymerization)**  
   - Conditions: The reaction mixture is transferred to a Teflon‑lined autoclave and treated at 100°C for 24 hours, completing the polymerization.

3. **Washing and Drying**  
   - Washing: The resultant PDVB is washed with methanol and ethanol to remove unreacted monomers and by-products.
   - Drying: Finally, the material is dried at 100°C.

### Comparative Analysis
The coprecipitation method offers simplicity and uniform precursor formation but can be challenging in scalability due to strict pH control and temperature regulation. In contrast, the PDVB synthesis, though thermally demanding with a 24‑hour autoclave process, provides a robust hydrophobic promoter. Both methods are designed for precision and reproducibility, ensuring that subsequent physical mixing (at various weight ratios) yields catalysts with enhanced CO conversion and olefin selectivity through effective water removal and improved active site exposure.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: CoMnC Catalyst
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 
  - 29.4 g cobalt nitrate hexahydrate [Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O]
  - 17.9 g manganese nitrate (50 wt% solution)
  - 75 mL deionized water
  - Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) solution (prepared separately)
- **Conditions**: 
  - Temperature maintained at ~30°C with vigorous stirring
  - pH adjusted to ~8 using Na₂CO₃ (added dropwise)
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve cobalt and manganese nitrates in deionized water to form a homogeneous solution.
  - Under vigorous stirring at room temperature, add the Na₂CO₃ solution dropwise to adjust the pH to 8 and initiate coprecipitation.
  - Stir the suspension for 1 hour followed by aging for 2 hours to ensure complete precipitation of the mixed oxide precursor.
- **Key Steps**: 
  - Monitor pH closely to maintain ~8.
  - Ensure homogeneous mixing for proper precipitation.

##### Step 2: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Purification Process**:
  - Centrifuge the coprecipitated mixture and wash the precipitate multiple times with deionized water to remove residual impurities.
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the washed precursor in an oven at 100°C for 10 hours.
- **Calcination**:
  - Calcine the dried precursor at 330°C for 3 hours in air with a controlled ramp rate.
  
##### Step 3: Activation
- **Activation Process**:
  - Reduce the calcined precursor under a pure H₂ flow at 300°C for 5 hours to form the active CoMnC phase.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: PDVB Synthesis
##### Step 1: Polymer Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 
  - 10 g divinylbenzene
  - 0.5 g AIBN (radical initiator)
- **Conditions**:
  - Stir the mixture at room temperature for 1 hour.
- **Process**:
  - Combine AIBN with divinylbenzene to initiate polymerization.
  
##### Step 2: Thermal Treatment and Post-Processing
- **Process**:
  - Transfer the mixture to an autoclave and conduct thermal treatment at 100°C for 24 hours.
  - After treatment, wash the polymer with methanol and ethanol to remove any unreacted residues.
  - Finally, dry the polymer at 100°C.
- **Variants**:
  - For nanoporous PDVB (PDVB-DMF or PDVB-EA), dissolve the reagents in 50 g of the respective solvent and treat at 100°C for 12 hours.

#### Final Catalyst Assembly: Physical Mixing
- **Process**:
  - Mix the activated CoMnC catalyst with PDVB in predefined weight ratios (e.g., 1:1, 0.5:1, 1.5:1) to optimize dehydration and catalytic performance.
- **Key Steps**:
  - Ensure uniform distribution during the physical mixing to preserve the active phase’s structure and maximize water removal without altering the catalyst’s intrinsic properties.

This detailed workflow supports reproducibility and provides clarity for each critical stage—from precursor preparation through activation and final physical assembly.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation procedure for the CoMnC/PDVB Fischer–Tropsch catalyst is performed during the catalyst synthesis as a pre-reaction treatment. This process is critical to ensure complete reduction of the oxide precursor and formation of the active cobalt–manganese carbide phase.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is vital for initiating the reduction reaction that transforms the oxide precursor into an active carbide phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated by in‐situ reduction at 300 °C.
- **Observations:** Maintaining 300 °C ensures that the reduction reaction is efficient, leading to the removal of oxygen species and exposing the active metal sites required for effective Fischer–Tropsch catalysis.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The activation pressure impacts the interaction between the activation gas and the catalyst surface, thus influencing the reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction is conducted under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Operating at atmospheric pressure provides a stable environment for hydrogen reduction without introducing high-pressure effects that could cause catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The choice of activation gas determines the reduction pathway; pure hydrogen (H₂) serves as a strong reducing agent.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure H₂ is used during the reduction step.
- **Observations:** The use of pure hydrogen efficiently strips oxygen from the precursor, ensuring the formation of a carbide phase with well-exposed active sites.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Parameters

- **Experimental Conditions:** The hydrogen gas is introduced at a flow rate of 20 mL/min.
- **Observations:** A controlled flow of H₂ at 20 mL/min under atmospheric conditions aids in uniform reduction across the catalyst material and minimizes any potential over-reduction or uneven distribution.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process is crucial to guarantee full transformation of the precursor into the active phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to the reducing environment for 5 hours.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was conducted at a controlled temperature of 250 °C. This parameter is critical as it governs the activation of syngas and influences the distribution of hydrocarbon products. At 250 °C, the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst demonstrated high CO conversion and enhanced light olefin selectivity, while keeping methane formation below 5%. This temperature also contributed to maintaining the catalyst’s stability over extended runs (up to 120 hours), ensuring minimal deactivation attributed to efficient water desorption from the catalyst surface.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The experiments were performed at a reaction pressure of 0.1 MPa. Operating under this mild pressure ensures a balanced syngas concentration, which is important for achieving the desired product distribution. At 0.1 MPa, the catalyst showed optimal performance, with favorable selectivity toward light olefins and efficient suppression of undesired by-products. This operating pressure supports a reaction environment that effectively facilitates CO conversion while keeping by-product formation to a minimum.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The Fischer–Tropsch evaluation primarily used an H₂/CO ratio of 2, which enables a balanced pathway for hydrocarbon chain growth. However, an alternative value of 3.6 was also reported for efforts aimed at optimizing product selectivity. The adjustment in the H₂/CO ratio noticeably influenced hydrocarbon distribution, as a higher ratio promoted improved CO conversion and altered selectivity profiles, favoring olefin production while reducing CO₂ formation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) was defined based on the weight of CoMnC, with reported values ranging from 1800 to 7200 mL·h⁻¹·gCoMnC⁻¹. This parameter is essential for controlling the contact time between syngas and the catalyst. Variations in GHSV were observed to affect both the CO conversion and catalyst stability; lower GHSV values were associated with higher CO conversion, whereas higher GHSV values provided insights into catalyst performance under more rapid gas flow conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structural Stability
- **Description:** Crystal phase composition and stability were assessed using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is critical for verifying the preservation of the active carbide phase under reaction and water exposure conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirm that the CoMnC phase remains stable when physically mixed with PDVB, with negligible differences observed between CoMnC and CoMnC/PDVB during reaction periods. The Co₂C (cobalt carbide) phase is maintained in CoMnC/PDVB, while water treatment of bare CoMnC leads to oxidation and the appearance of a CoO (cobalt oxide) peak. After removal of PDVB from the used catalyst, the CoMnC component exhibits XRD patterns and catalytic performance comparable to the as-prepared material.
- **Observations:** The physical mixing strategy preserves the active carbide phase and prevents water-induced oxidation, directly correlating with sustained catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Distribution
- **Description:** Morphological features and particle distribution were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), providing insight into the physical arrangement and integrity of catalyst components.
- **Findings:** SEM images reveal tightly packed, disordered distributions of CoMnC and PDVB within catalyst granules, with intergranular distances ranging from nanometers to micrometers. TEM analysis shows that the morphology of CoMnC remains unchanged after reaction and after separation from PDVB, indicating that physical mixing does not alter the particle structure.
- **Observations:** The close, random packing and preserved morphology facilitate efficient water removal and maintain catalyst integrity, supporting the role of PDVB as a hydrophobic promoter.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were determined by nitrogen sorption isotherms (BET analysis), which are important for understanding the accessibility and diffusion properties of the catalyst and promoter.
- **Findings:** Nanoporous PDVB variants exhibit high surface areas and pore volumes: PDVB-DMF has a surface area of 623.3 m²/g and a pore volume of 1.98 cm³/g; PDVB-EA has 488.2 m²/g and 0.74 cm³/g. In contrast, nonporous PDVB has a surface area of less than 5 m²/g.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and porosity of nanoporous PDVB enhance its effectiveness in promoting rapid water desorption, thereby improving catalyst performance.

#### 6.1.4 Thermal Stability
- **Description:** Thermal stability of PDVB was evaluated using thermogravimetric analysis coupled with mass spectrometry (TG-MS) and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC), ensuring the promoter’s integrity under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** PDVB is thermally stable at the reaction temperature of 250°C, with no detectable weight loss or decomposition products. Decomposition begins at approximately 370°C, and endothermal signals suggest melting above 300°C.
- **Observations:** The stability of PDVB at operational temperatures ensures its persistent hydrophobic function during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Hydrophobicity was quantified by water-droplet contact angle measurements, which directly reflect the material’s ability to repel water.
- **Findings:** PDVB and PDVB-containing catalysts exhibit high contact angles (e.g., ~145° for nonporous PDVB), confirming strong hydrophobic character.
- **Observations:** Enhanced hydrophobicity facilitates rapid water removal from the catalyst surface, minimizing water-induced deactivation.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Species and Water Desorption
- **Description:** In situ Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to monitor surface species and water desorption dynamics during CO hydrogenation.
- **Findings:** On CoMnC/PDVB, FTIR spectra show strong olefin product signals and extremely weak water signals, indicating rapid water desorption. In contrast, bare CoMnC exhibits strong, increasing water signals, signifying water accumulation.
- **Observations:** The presence of PDVB prevents water from hindering CO adsorption, thereby sustaining high catalytic activity and selectivity for light olefins.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{\text{CO}_{\text{in}} - \text{CO}_{\text{out}}}{\text{CO}_{\text{in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch conditions (250°C, 0.1 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 1800 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), the baseline CoMnC catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 32.2%. Upon physical mixing with PDVB (CoMnC/PDVB, 1:1 weight ratio), CO conversion increased to 63.5%. Varying the PDVB/CoMnC ratio from 0.5 to 1.5 further raised conversion from 56.4% to 70.5%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nearly doubled CO conversion with PDVB addition is attributed to enhanced water removal from the catalyst surface, as confirmed by in situ FTIR and water adsorption studies. Higher PDVB content correlates with increased conversion, highlighting the role of hydrophobicity in maintaining active sites for CO adsorption.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{CO}_2}{\text{CO}_{\text{in}} - \text{CO}_{\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Both CoMnC and CoMnC/PDVB catalysts exhibited CO₂ selectivity in the range of 46–49% under standard conditions. Adjusting the H₂/CO ratio to 3.6 and co-feeding CO₂ reduced CO₂ selectivity to 23.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of PDVB does not significantly alter CO₂ selectivity, indicating that its primary effect is on water management rather than shifting the CO₂ formation pathway.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{CH}_4}{\text{CO}_{\text{in}} - \text{CO}_{\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity for CoMnC is 3.3%, increasing slightly to 4.8% with PDVB addition (Table S3). Across all tested PDVB/CoMnC ratios, CH₄ selectivity remains below 5%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of PDVB suppresses excessive methane formation, maintaining low CH₄ selectivity while boosting olefin production.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–C₄ Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity for light olefins (C₂–C₄) is a key performance metric for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, calculated as:
\[
\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4 \text{ Olefin Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{C}_2\text{–C}_4 \text{ olefins}}{\text{CO}_{\text{in}} - \text{CO}_{\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The bare CoMnC catalyst achieves a light olefin selectivity of 60.8% (CO₂-free basis), while CoMnC/PDVB reaches 71.4%. With varying PDVB/CoMnC ratios, selectivity remains high (62.8–71.4%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Incorporation of PDVB consistently enhances light olefin selectivity, with the highest values observed at a 1:1 PDVB/CoMnC ratio. This improvement is linked to rapid water desorption facilitated by the hydrophobic polymer.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over extended operation.
- **Experimental Data:** The CoMnC/PDVB catalyst maintains stable CO conversion (~62.8%) and light olefin selectivity (70.0%) over 120 hours of continuous reaction.
- **Observations and Trends:** The physical mixing strategy preserves the active phase structure and ensures sustained catalytic performance, with negligible deactivation observed during long-term testing.
