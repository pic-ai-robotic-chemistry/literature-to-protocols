## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

• The catalyst synthesis employs a multi‐step procedure integrating hydrothermal synthesis of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles, deposition–precipitation of a Mn promoter, and subsequent coating with a SiO₂ shell via a modified Stöber process. A hydrophobic variant is later achieved through surface silanization.  
• In comparison, the hydrothermal method yields high‐purity nanoparticles with controlled morphology but requires high‑pressure equipment, whereas the co‑precipitation (deposition) process provides a more scalable yet slightly less uniform alternative.

#### Hydrothermal Synthesis Method

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Combine 7.5 g of FeCl₃·6H₂O, 12.4 g of sodium acetate trihydrate, 3.8 g of CTAB, and 26 mL of ethylenediamine.  
   - Stir continuously at 30 °C for 2 hours (using a pH meter for monitoring where necessary).  
   - Observation: Uniform dispersion of metal precursors.

2. Hydrothermal Treatment  
   - Transfer the mixture to a Teflon‑lined autoclave and treat at 200 °C for 10 hours.  
   - Equipment: Teflon‑lined autoclave.  
   - Outcome: Formation of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.

3. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Dropwise addition of 5 mL aqueous KMnO₄ solution (containing 0.078 g KMnO₄) into the Fe₂O₃ suspension in ethanol.  
   - Stir at 30 °C for 2 hours to promote Mn deposition with observable precipitate formation and color change.

4. Drying and Calcination  
   - Dry at 100 °C for 12 hours.  
   - Calcine at 500 °C (in air) for 5 hours, ensuring complete conversion and phase development.

#### SiO₂ Shell Formation and Hydrophobic Modification

1. Shell Formation  
   - Apply a modified Stöber method by adding TEOS (using volumes of 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL) into an ethanol-water-ammonia system, with careful pH control.  
   - Observation: Gradual formation of a conformal SiO₂ shell.

2. Hydrophobic Modification (for Fe@Mn@xSi‑c)  
   - Post-treatment involves immersion in n‑hexane with chlorotrimethylsilane followed by ultrasonic treatment, noting that residual chlorine may affect catalyst activity.

3. Reduction  
   - Finally, conduct reduction treatment in pure H₂ at 350 °C, 0.1 MPa for 20 hours to convert the iron species into their active carbide phases.

This structured methodology ensures reproducibility and precise control over synthesis parameters essential for reliable Fischer–Tropsch catalyst performance.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe@Mn@xSi  
- **Fe@Mn precursor**: Weighed 0.5 g using an analytical balance.  
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O (for Fe₂O₃ synthesis)**: Weighed 7.5 g using an analytical balance.  
- **Sodium acetate trihydrate (CH₃COONa·3H₂O)**: Weighed 12.4 g.  
- **Hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB)**: Weighed 3.8 g.

##### 3.1.1.2 Fe@Mn@xSi‑c  
- **Fe@Mn precursor**: Weighed 0.5 g using an analytical balance.  
- The same solid precursors as in Fe@Mn@xSi are used prior to the hydrophobic modification.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe@Mn@xSi  
- **Ethanol**: Poured 150 mL into the reaction vessel using a graduated cylinder.  
- **Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS)**: Transferred in volumes of 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL using a micropipette.  
- **Deionized water**: Poured 10 mL into the dispersion container using a pipette.  
- **Ammonia solution (25–28%)**: Poured 2.5 mL with a syringe into the mixture for controlled SiO₂ shell formation.

##### 3.1.2.2 Fe@Mn@xSi‑c  
- Follow the same steps as for Fe@Mn@xSi for ethanol, TEOS, water, and ammonia.  
- **n‑Hexane**: Poured 70 mL into a clean beaker using a graduated cylinder.  
- **Chlorotrimethylsilane**: Added 0.5 mL dropwise using a micropipette for surface silanization.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe@Mn@xSi  
- **SiO₂ formation mixture**: Mixed by stirring at 30 °C for 3 hours during TEOS addition and then for 4 hours after adding water and ammonia, ensuring complete hydrolysis under modified Stöber conditions.  
- **Dispersion**: Prior to stirring, the Fe@Mn precursor was ultrasonically treated at room temperature for 1 hour to ensure homogeneity.

##### 3.1.3.2 Fe@Mn@xSi‑c  
- After the initial reagent mixing, the freshly prepared Fe@Mn@xSi catalyst is dried in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for 10 hours and cooled.  
- **Hydrophobic modification**: The dried catalyst is redispersed in ethanol and ultrasonically treated at room temperature for 1 hour. Following the addition of chlorotrimethylsilane in n‑hexane, the suspension is mixed at room temperature for 1 hour to complete the surface silanization process.

This detailed procedure, with precise reagent quantities, liquid transfer methods, and controlled mixing conditions, ensures full reproducibility for both hydrophilic (Fe@Mn@xSi) and hydrophobic (Fe@Mn@xSi‑c) catalyst preparations in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles Synthesis  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 12.4 g sodium acetate trihydrate, 3.8 g CTAB, 26 mL ethylenediamine, deionized water  
- **Conditions**: Autoclave treatment at 200 °C for 10 hours; stirring at room temperature initially and 0.5 hours post FeCl₃·6H₂O dissolution  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O in deionized water; add sodium acetate and CTAB with stirring.  
  - After 0.5 hours, introduce ethylenediamine slowly and continue stirring for 1 hour.  
  - Transfer the reaction mixture to an autoclave and maintain at 200 °C for 10 hours.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and uniform mixing prior to hydrothermal treatment.

##### Step 2: Mn Promoter Deposition  
- **Reagents**: Prepared Fe₂O₃ particles (0.5 g), 0.078 g KMnO₄ dissolved in 5 mL aqueous solution, ethanol (300 mL)  
- **Conditions**: Stir at 30 °C for 2 hours  
- **Process**:  
  - Disperse 0.5 g Fe₂O₃ in ethanol.  
  - Add the KMnO₄ solution dropwise under continuous stirring.  
  - Maintain the reaction at 30 °C for 2 hours.  
- **Key Points**: Dropwise addition and consistent temperature ensure uniform Mn deposition.

#### Catalyst Assembly and SiO₂ Shell Formation  
##### Step 3: SiO₂ Coating  
- **Reagents**: Fe@Mn particles, TEOS (x mL; x = 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8), 150 mL ethanol, 10 mL water, 2.5 mL ammonia solution  
- **Conditions**: Stir at 30 °C; initial stirring for 3 hours then additional 4 hours after TEOS hydrolysis initiation  
- **Process**:  
  - Disperse Fe@Mn in ethanol and add the specified TEOS volume.  
  - Introduce water and ammonia to trigger the controlled hydrolysis of TEOS under modified Stöber conditions.  
  - Stir for 4 hours, then centrifuge and wash with ethanol before drying at 100 °C for 12 hours.  
- **Pause Point**: Verify complete SiO₂ deposition through standard analytical techniques (e.g., XRD).

##### Step 4: Hydrophobic Modification (for Fe@Mn@xSi-c)  
- **Reagents**: 0.5 g of dried Fe@Mn@xSi, 70 mL n-hexane, 0.5 mL chlorotrimethylsilane  
- **Conditions**: Ultrasonicate at room temperature for 1 hour; subsequent vacuum drying at 80 °C for 6 hours  
- **Process**:  
  - Dry Fe@Mn@xSi at 120 °C for 10 hours, cool, then disperse in n-hexane.  
  - Add chlorotrimethylsilane, ultrasonicate, centrifuge, wash with n-hexane, and dry.  
- **Caution**: Residual chlorine may remain, potentially impacting catalyst activity.

#### Catalyst Activation  
- **Reduction**: Treat in pure H₂ at 350 °C (0.1 MPa) for 20 hours  
- **Activation**: Subsequent syngas treatment at 320 °C (0.1 MPa) for 5 hours  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure controlled temperature ramp and maintenance of pressure to achieve complete reduction/carburization for optimal Fischer–Tropsch activity.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is executed in two sequential stages to develop the active iron carbide phases essential for efficient CO conversion and C–C coupling.

#### 5.1.1 Reduction in Pure H₂
- **Definition:** The reduction step converts iron oxide precursors into metallic iron, which is a necessary precursor for subsequent carbonization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced in a pure H₂ atmosphere at 350 °C under a pressure of 0.1 MPa for 20 hours.
- **Observations:** This prolonged H₂ reduction effectively removes oxygen from the iron oxide, ensuring a clean transition to metallic iron without significant sintering, thereby laying a solid foundation for the next activation step.

#### 5.1.2 Syngas Activation (Carbonization)
- **Definition:** The second stage involves activation with syngas to carburize the reduced metallic iron into active iron carbide phases, predominantly χ‑Fe₅C₂, critical for optimal catalytic performance.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Following the H₂ reduction, the catalyst is further treated with a syngas mixture consisting of 31.6% CO and 68.4% N₂ at 320 °C and maintained at 0.1 MPa for 5 hours. In some model experiments, the syngas treatment is performed at 2.0 MPa for 20 hours; however, for pre-reaction treatment, the 5-hour step is applied.
- **Observations:** Activation at 320 °C is carefully chosen to promote the formation of the desired carbide phase while minimizing unwanted oxidation to Fe₃O₄ and preventing excessive sintering. The tailored syngas composition aids in controlled carbonization, ensuring stable catalyst performance during subsequent synthesis reactions.

These precisely defined activation parameters are critical for reproducibility. The initial reduction in pure H₂ eliminates oxygen and prepares the catalyst, while the subsequent syngas activation converts the metallic phase into active iron carbide. Both stages are designed specifically for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst synthesis, ensuring that the materials exhibit robust activity and stability during long-chain olefin formation in the actual reaction environment.

### 5.2 Reaction

This section details the reaction conditions employed in the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation, providing the experimental parameters critical for replicating catalyst performance and product selectivity.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was conducted at a reaction temperature of 320 °C. This temperature is instrumental for activating the iron-based catalyst, facilitating the critical phase evolution into active iron carbide species responsible for CO activation and C–C coupling. Notably, at 320 °C, the balance between catalyst activation and potential oxidation by H₂O is maintained. For the hydrophobic catalyst variant (Fe@Mn@xSi-c), the SiO₂ modification inhibits the oxidation of iron carbide to Fe₃O₄, thus enhancing catalyst stability over prolonged operation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The reaction is performed under a pressure of 2.0 MPa, corresponding to approximately 20 bar. This elevated pressure enhances syngas conversion and significantly influences product distribution. Higher reaction pressures favor the formation of heavier hydrocarbons (C₅+), contributing to an optimal distribution of multicarbon products, which is essential in achieving the desired catalytic selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
An H₂/CO ratio of 2 is maintained throughout the experimental evaluations. This ratio is critical in steering the hydrocarbon selectivity; a higher hydrogen content typically enhances hydrogenation steps leading to lighter hydrocarbons such as methane, whereas a lower ratio promotes olefin formation. The specified ratio thus serves to balance the dual objectives of high CO conversion and selectivity towards longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The experimental setup employs a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹. This parameter defines the flow rate of reactants per unit mass of catalyst, thereby controlling the exposure time of syngas to the active sites. Such a controlled space-time velocity is fundamental for reproducibility, ensuring consistent catalytic performance and allowing the observed trends in selectivity and catalyst stability to be directly correlated to the specified reaction conditions.

Collectively, these carefully defined parameters provide a comprehensive and reproducible framework for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, facilitating future comparative studies and optimization of iron-based catalysts.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Stability
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and its evolution under reaction conditions were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Ultima IV-185, XPert Pro, and Rigaku SmartLab diffractometers. In situ XRD enabled monitoring of phase changes during exposure to CO, H₂, and H₂O atmospheres, which is critical for understanding catalyst stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** For hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts, XRD patterns revealed transformation of Fe₂O₃ to χ‑Fe₅C₂ under syngas, followed by oxidation to Fe₃O₄ upon H₂O exposure. In contrast, hydrophobic Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalysts maintained stable iron carbide phases (χ‑Fe₅C₂ and θ‑Fe₃C) even in the presence of H₂O, with no Fe₃O₄ formation observed.
- **Observations:** The hydrophobic modification effectively inhibits H₂O-induced oxidation, preserving the active carbide phase essential for high Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.2 Phase Composition Quantification (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to quantify the relative proportions of iron phases after reaction, providing insight into the active phase distribution.
- **Findings:** For Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c, the post-reaction phase composition was 74.1% χ‑Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ‑Fe₃C. Hydrophilic catalysts exhibited increased Fe₃O₄ content, especially with thinner SiO₂ shells.
- **Observations:** The dominance of iron carbide phases in hydrophobic catalysts correlates with enhanced catalytic performance and stability.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction and Carburization Behavior (CO-TPR and H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (CO-TPR and H₂-TPR) assessed the reducibility and carburization of iron species, with peak positions and areas reflecting accessibility and transformation efficiency.
- **Findings:** Increasing SiO₂ shell thickness (higher TEOS dosage) shifted reduction/carburization peaks to higher temperatures and reduced their area, indicating hindered CO access and decreased iron carbide formation. The thinnest shell (0.05 mL TEOS) resulted in only Fe₃O₄ after reaction.
- **Observations:** Optimal shell thickness balances protection against oxidation with sufficient carburization, while excessive thickness impedes active phase formation.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Elemental Distribution (TEM and EDS)
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were used to examine particle morphology, shell thickness, and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images confirmed homogeneous encapsulation of Fe@Mn cores by amorphous SiO₂ shells, with shell thickness tunable via TEOS dosage. EDS mapping showed uniform Mn promoter distribution and clear core–shell architecture.
- **Observations:** The structural integrity and uniformity of the core–shell design are crucial for phase stability and controlled reactivity.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and Hydrophobicity (XPS and Contact Angle)
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyzed surface composition, particularly chlorine residues from hydrophobic modification, while contact angle measurements quantified surface hydrophobicity.
- **Findings:** XPS detected chlorine only on catalysts with insufficient SiO₂ protection, confirming that an adequate shell prevents chlorine contamination. Contact angle measurements (Kruss DSA100) demonstrated increased hydrophobicity after silanization.
- **Observations:** Effective hydrophobization limits H₂O adsorption, thereby stabilizing iron carbide phases and preventing deactivation.

#### 6.1.6 CO Adsorption Behavior (FTIR and DRIFTS)
- **Description:** FTIR and in situ DRIFTS characterized CO adsorption on catalyst surfaces, revealing the nature of active sites and the impact of surface modifications.
- **Findings:** On chlorine-free, hydrophobic catalysts, strong CO adsorption was evidenced by persistent carbonate bands and new adsorption sites. Chlorine-containing surfaces showed only gaseous CO, indicating hindered adsorption.
- **Observations:** Surface cleanliness and hydrophobicity are vital for maintaining active CO adsorption sites, directly influencing catalytic efficiency.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (320 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts exhibit CO conversion values that decrease with increasing SiO₂ shell thickness: for example, Fe@Mn@0.05Si achieves 53.8%, while Fe@Mn@2.8Si drops to 24.3%. Hydrophobic modification (Fe@Mn@xSi-c) generally results in lower CO conversion due to residual chlorine from silanization, with Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c showing a notably low conversion (<2%) in CO + H₂O model experiments.
- **Observations and Trends:** Thicker SiO₂ shells hinder CO access and carburization, reducing conversion. Hydrophobic catalysts with optimal shell thickness (e.g., Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c) maintain stable conversion by suppressing water-induced deactivation, though excessive chlorine residues can poison activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift (WGS) side reaction:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Hydrophilic catalysts show high CO₂ selectivity (up to 40%), while hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c achieves a significantly lower value (7.9%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic surface modification restricts water diffusion, suppressing the WGS reaction and thus reducing CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe@Mn@0.2Si, CH₄ selectivity is 21.5%; for Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c, it decreases to 14.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic modification suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c demonstrates increased C₃⁺ selectivity, correlating with enhanced olefin production (olefin selectivity rises from 49.8% to 64.3% upon hydrophobic modification).
- **Observations and Trends:** Improved C₃⁺ selectivity reflects the catalyst’s enhanced C–C coupling ability and suppression of undesired hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination (0 < α < 1).
- **Experimental Data:** Hydrophobic catalysts exhibit higher α values, consistent with increased selectivity toward long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** The stabilization of iron carbide phases in hydrophobic catalysts promotes chain growth, as evidenced by higher α factors and olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time; the deactivation rate is:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c maintains stable CO conversion and phase composition over 110 hours of continuous operation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic surface modification significantly enhances catalyst durability by preventing water-induced oxidation and deactivation.

---
**Summary:** The systematic comparison of hydrophilic and hydrophobic Fe-based catalysts demonstrates that surface hydrophobization—when optimized for shell thickness and minimal chlorine residue—substantially improves selectivity for long-chain olefins, suppresses CO₂ and CH₄ formation, and enhances catalyst stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
