## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article details three synthesis routes for Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, each with a defined sequence to ensure reproducibility:

#### • Fe@Si Catalyst (Solvothermal with Modified Stöber Process)
1. Precursor Mixing:  
   - 7.39 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and 7.21 g polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, M_w 58,000) are dissolved in 800 mL anhydrous ethanol and stirred for 5 h.  
   - The solution is transferred to a stainless steel autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 18 h to form Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.
2. Precipitation–Deposition:  
   - For the SiO₂ shell, 25 mL tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) is added dropwise into a stirred mixture containing 144 mL NH₃·H₂O (25%), 1656 mL deionized water, and 1276 mL ethanol along with CTAB as the structure-directing agent.  
   - TEOS is hydrolyzed over 6 h and the mixture is stirred for an additional 48 h.
3. Drying & Calcination:  
   - The composite is dried and subsequently calcined at 550 °C for 6 h to remove organic templates and develop crystallinity.

#### • Fe/Si Catalyst (Incipient Wetness Impregnation) 
1. Precursor Mixing:  
   - 10.0 g mesoporous SiO₂ is impregnated with an ethanol solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ (2 M) for 5 h.
2. Precipitation–Deposition:  
   - The impregnated support is subjected to hydrothermal treatment in a Teflon‑lined autoclave at 180 °C for 18 h.
3. Drying & Calcination:  
   - Post-treatment involves drying at 120 °C for 24 h followed by calcination at 550 °C for 6 h.

#### • Fe–Si Catalyst (Co‑precipitation)
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation:  
   - 20.6 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and 31.7 g silica sol are dissolved in distilled water.
   - 200 mL NH₃·H₂O is added to maintain a pH of 9.0 ± 0.1 at 85 °C.
2. Precipitation–Deposition:  
   - As the precipitate forms uniformly, the process ensures a strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction.
3. Drying & Calcination:  
   - The recovered solid is washed, dried at 120 °C, and calcined at 550 °C for 6 h.

### Comparative Analysis
• The Fe@Si method offers superior control over nanoparticle dispersion with a protective SiO₂ shell, though the embedding may impede reduction.  
• The Fe/Si catalyst benefits from a straightforward impregnation technique but is prone to particle agglomeration.  
• The co‑precipitation approach for Fe–Si ensures uniform iron embedding under controlled pH, favoring enhanced metal–support interaction despite potential challenges in particle size control.

Each method employs precise reagent quantities, reaction conditions, and equipment settings (e.g., autoclaves, pH control) ensuring clear reproducibility and scalability as detailed in the article.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Si  
##### Step 1: Synthesis of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles  
- **Reagents**: 7.39 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O, 7.21 g polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), 800 mL anhydrous ethanol  
- **Conditions**: Stir at room temperature for 5 hours; transfer to a stainless steel autoclave and heat at 180 °C for 18 hours  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and PVP in ethanol to form a homogeneous solution.  
  - Stir continuously for 5 hours to ensure complete dissolution and particle stabilization.  
  - Transfer the solution to an autoclave and conduct solvothermal treatment at 180 °C for 18 hours to generate well-dispersed Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure consistent stirring and timely temperature control to prevent aggregation.

##### Step 2: SiO₂ Shell Formation via Modified Stöber Process  
- **Reagents**: Tetraethoxysilane (TEOS), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), deionized water, and ammonia solution  
- **Conditions**: Maintain pH at 9.0 ± 0.1, temperature at 85 °C  
- **Process**:  
  - Add TEOS to a solution containing CTAB under controlled pH conditions to promote hydrolysis and condensation.  
  - Allow the formation of a mesoporous SiO₂ shell around the Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.  
  - Stir for a defined period to ensure uniform shell encapsulation.  
- **Key Steps**: The use of CTAB is critical for templating the mesoporous structure that protects against sintering during calcination.

##### Purification and Final Processing  
- **Process**:  
  - Wash the catalyst suspension via centrifugation to remove unreacted species.  
  - Dry the material at 120 °C for 6 hours.  
- **Drying & Calcination**:  
  - Calcine at 550 °C for 6 hours to achieve crystallinity and eliminate organic residues.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD is employed to identify the crystalline phases of the catalysts by recording the diffraction patterns. Catalyst samples are prepared in a suitable form and placed in the instrument; sample alignment is performed automatically by the diffractometer.
- **Instruments and Models**: D8 ADVANCE diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Co Kα radiation (λ = 1.79026 Å), operated at 35 kV and 40 mA.
- **Test Parameters**: 2θ range from 30° to 90° with a step size of 0.04°.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform sample preparation is critical to minimize measurement errors.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Approximately 100 mg of catalyst is loaded into a U-type tube reactor. The catalyst is pretreated if necessary, and the reduction behavior is monitored as the temperature is ramped.
- **Instruments and Models**: Autochem II 2920.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Reaction conducted in 10% H₂/90% Ar flowing at 50 mL/min; temperature ramp from 50 to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Water produced during reduction is eliminated using an isopropyl alcohol gel maintained at −97 °C.
- **Key Notes**: Maintaining precise temperature control and uniform catalyst loading ensures reproducible reduction profiles.

### 4.3 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: Determines the surface area and porosity of catalysts via N₂ physisorption.
- **Instruments and Models**: ASAP 2420.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements performed at −196 °C.
- **Test Parameters**: Samples are degassed under vacuum first at 90 °C for 120 minutes and then at 350 °C for 480 minutes.
- **Key Notes**: Proper degassing is essential for accurate determination of pore size and surface area.

### 4.4 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)
- **Operational Description**: TEM is utilized to assess catalyst morphology and aggregation. In parallel, EDS mapping confirms elemental distributions.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Talos F200X operated at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are dispersed in ethanol for 20 minutes and deposited onto carbon membranes.
- **Test Parameters**: Standard TEM imaging protocols are followed.
- **Key Notes**: The combination of TEM and EDS is pivotal for visualizing the protective SiO₂ shell and identifying regions of aggregation.

### 4.5 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT‑IR)
- **Operational Description**: FT‑IR is applied to assess the Fe–SiO₂ interactions by analyzing vibrational modes.
- **Instruments and Models**: VERTEX 70 FT‑IR spectrophotometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Spectra are acquired over 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.
- **Test Parameters**: Samples are mixed with KBr in a 1/50 ratio and pressed into translucent disks.
- **Key Notes**: Sample preparation via KBr pellet formation facilitates consistent spectral quality.

### 4.6 Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: Used to characterize iron oxidation states and Fe–SiO₂ interactions by recording Mössbauer spectra.
- **Instruments and Models**: MR-351 constant‐acceleration Mössbauer spectrometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements are performed at 50 K.
- **Test Parameters**: Spectra are fitted using a combination of singlets, quadruple doublets, and magnetic sextets with a Lorentzian profile.
- **Key Notes**: This method provides insight into both the oxidation state and local environment of iron species.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for the Fischer–Tropsch experiments is conducted as a pre‐reaction treatment designed to reduce iron oxides and initiate partial carburization under controlled conditions. This step is critical for developing the active phase of the catalyst, ensuring optimal performance during subsequent high-pressure reaction tests.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The temperature at which the catalyst is thermally treated to facilitate reduction while minimizing sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Catalysts are activated at 350 °C.
- **Observations:** At this temperature, the protective SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively maintains metal dispersion, with minimal signs of sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The pressure during activation impacts the kinetics of gas-solid interactions and reduction efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed at atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** This standard pressure ensures consistent flow dynamics of the activating gas across different catalyst formulations.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition is essential for driving reduction and carburization processes simultaneously.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with an H₂/CO ratio of 2 is used.
- **Observations:** The hydrogen-rich component facilitates the reduction of Fe₂O₃, while CO assists in partial carburization without triggering excessive carbide formation.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** This parameter relates the volume flow of activation gas to the catalyst weight, ensuring uniform exposure.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is maintained at 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹.
- **Observations:** This flow rate is optimized to prevent over-reduction and guarantees complete treatment of the catalytic surface.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation, which must be sufficient to allow complete reduction while mitigating sintering risks.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A 24-hour activation period is implemented.
- **Observations:** The extended duration helps achieve thorough activation, ensuring that each catalyst, whether prepared by impregnation or co‐precipitation, reaches a stable active state before entering the high-pressure reaction phase.

### 5.2 Reaction

This section details the precise reaction conditions used during the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation, ensuring that experimental parameters are clearly defined and reproducible.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is fundamental for syngas activation, influencing both hydrogenation kinetics and the reduction of iron oxides.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were performed at a uniform temperature of 280 °C.  
- **Observations:** At 280 °C, the catalysts—Fe@Si, Fe/Si, and Fe–Si—show effective CO conversion while mitigating excessive sintering. This temperature was optimized to maintain catalyst stability during prolonged operation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure dictates the concentration of CO in the syngas mixture and influences hydrocarbon selectivity by promoting chain growth reactions.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The fixed-bed reactor was operated at a pressure of 2.0 MPa.  
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure improved CO conversion and enhanced the formation of heavier hydrocarbons (C₅⁺), particularly benefiting catalysts with strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio directly affects product distribution by altering the kinetic pathways for hydrocarbon formation.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A fixed H₂/CO ratio of 2 was maintained throughout the catalytic tests.  
- **Observations:** This ratio was critical for balancing methane and higher hydrocarbon production; it facilitated optimal catalyst performance by favoring chain-growth reactions without excessive methane formation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity (GHSV)
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) reflects the volumetric flow rate of syngas relative to the catalyst mass, thereby controlling the contact time between the reactants and the catalyst.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 5000 mL∙h⁻¹∙g_cat⁻¹ was applied.  
- **Observations:** The selected GHSV ensured a sufficient reactant residence time, which promoted efficient CO conversion while allowing for reliable comparisons of catalyst performance during long-term evaluations.

Collectively, these standardized conditions—280 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, and GHSV = 5000 mL∙h⁻¹∙g_cat⁻¹—provide a reproducible framework for assessing the catalytic activity and longevity of Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts under realistic operating conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural features of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a D8 ADVANCE diffractometer (Co Kα, λ = 1.79026 Å, 35 kV, 40 mA, 2θ = 30°–90°, step size 0.04°). Phase identification was referenced to standard JCPDF patterns.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts exhibit well-crystallized hematite (α-Fe₂O₃), with characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 38.7°, 41.7°, 63.9°, and 74.1°. The Fe–Si catalyst displays broad peaks at 2θ = 42° and 75°, indicative of amorphous iron oxides with small crystallites. After activation, XRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy confirm the formation of Fe₃O₄ and iron carbides as dominant phases in all catalysts, with Fe@Si and Fe/Si showing stronger iron carbide peaks than Fe–Si.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si slightly inhibits reduction and carburization compared to Fe/Si, as evidenced by a lower iron carbide content (52.01% vs. 55.75% in Fe/Si), while Fe–Si (29.67% iron carbides) demonstrates suppressed reduction due to stronger Fe–SiO₂ interaction.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were measured by N₂ physisorption (BET method) using an ASAP 2420 instrument. Samples were degassed at 90 °C for 120 min and 350 °C for 480 min prior to analysis.
- **Findings:**  
  - Fe@Si: 652 m²/g (surface area), 0.64 cm³/g (pore volume), 2.62 nm (average pore size)  
  - Fe/Si: 642 m²/g, 0.55 cm³/g, 2.55 nm  
  - Fe–Si: 295 m²/g, 0.46 cm³/g, 6.28 nm
- **Observations:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si possess significantly higher surface areas and smaller, more uniform pores than Fe–Si, which is beneficial for iron dispersion and catalytic activity. The larger pore size and lower surface area of Fe–Si may facilitate chain growth but reduce active site density.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Fe–SiO₂ Interaction
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and FT-IR spectroscopy were used to assess reducibility and Fe–SiO₂ interactions. H₂-TPR was performed on an Autochem II 2920 instrument.
- **Findings:** All catalysts exhibit two main reduction processes below 500 °C: Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄ (peak I) and partial Fe₃O₄ → FeO (peak II). H₂ consumption (0.30–0.36 mol H₂/mol Fe) exceeds the theoretical value for Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄, indicating partial reduction to FeO. FT-IR spectra show Fe–O–Si bonds (957–977 cm⁻¹) in all samples, confirming Fe–SiO₂ interaction.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si increases H₂O diffusion resistance, inhibiting reduction compared to Fe/Si. Stronger Fe–SiO₂ interaction in Fe–Si further suppresses reduction and carburization.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphology and particle size were examined by TEM (FEI Talos F200X) and EDX mapping.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si consists of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles (~50.4 nm) embedded in a porous SiO₂ shell, maintaining an average iron particle size of 45.1 nm after reaction. EDX mapping confirms uniform iron distribution. Fe/Si shows severe aggregation and growth of iron particles post-reaction, lacking a protective shell. Fe–Si displays slight aggregation due to stronger Fe–SiO₂ interaction.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively inhibits sintering, preserving particle size and morphology, while Fe/Si is prone to aggregation and sintering.

#### 6.1.5 Phase Composition and Oxidation States
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was used to quantify iron phase composition and oxidation states.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si contains 52.01% iron carbides, Fe/Si 55.75%, and Fe–Si 29.67%. Superparamagnetic Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions, Fe₃O₄, and iron carbides are present in all catalysts.
- **Observations:** The lower iron carbide content in Fe@Si and Fe–Si is attributed to the SiO₂ shell and strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction, respectively, which hinder reduction and carburization.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standardized conditions (280 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹), the Fe@Si catalyst exhibited a high initial CO conversion of 66.6%. The Fe/Si catalyst also showed high initial conversion, while the Fe–Si catalyst started lower (10.1%) but increased to 29.7% after 64 hours on stream.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts both experienced gradual deactivation, with CO conversion declining over time due to oxidation of iron carbides and sintering (more severe in Fe/Si). In contrast, the Fe–Si catalyst, benefiting from strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions, displayed increasing CO conversion with time, attributed to ongoing carburization and stabilization of active phases.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is typically calculated as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity was determined by gas chromatography after 64 hours on stream. The Fe@Si catalyst, due to the SiO₂ shell’s confinement effect and increased local H₂O partial pressure, exhibited higher CO₂ selectivity compared to Fe/Si and Fe–Si.
- **Observations and Trends:** The embedded structure in Fe@Si promoted the water–gas shift reaction, leading to increased CO₂ formation, while Fe–Si, with stronger Fe–SiO₂ interaction, showed lower CO₂ selectivity due to more stable iron carbide phases.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity was assessed via product analysis after 64 hours. The Fe@Si catalyst, with its well-dispersed iron nanoparticles and SiO₂ shell, demonstrated lower CH₄ selectivity relative to Fe/Si and Fe–Si.
- **Observations and Trends:** The suppression of iron particle aggregation in Fe@Si reduced methane formation, whereas Fe/Si and Fe–Si, prone to aggregation, exhibited higher CH₄ selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of chain growth. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe@Si catalyst achieved higher C₅⁺ selectivity compared to Fe/Si and Fe–Si, as determined by GC analysis.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si favored the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons by inhibiting sintering and maintaining high dispersion, while Fe/Si and Fe–Si showed lower C₅⁺ selectivity due to aggregation and less effective chain growth.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of hydrocarbon chain propagation versus termination, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher likelihood of forming long-chain products.
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe@Si catalyst exhibited the highest α factor among the three, correlating with its superior C₅⁺ selectivity. Fe/Si and Fe–Si showed lower α values, consistent with their product distributions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The embedded structure and physical separation in Fe@Si promoted chain growth, while aggregation in Fe/Si and Fe–Si limited α.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time, typically expressed as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate (h}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si both showed significant deactivation, with CO conversion decreasing over 64 hours. Fe–Si, however, exhibited an increase in activity, indicating minimal deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively inhibited sintering but introduced diffusion constraints, leading to gradual deactivation. In contrast, the strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction in Fe–Si stabilized the active phase, resulting in improved or sustained activity.
