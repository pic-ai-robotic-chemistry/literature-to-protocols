## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the continuous coprecipitation technique used for synthesizing Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, emphasizing precise reagent control and reproducible processing steps. A comparative analysis of this method highlights its scalability and uniformity versus its higher equipment demands.

#### Continuous Coprecipitation Method

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - • Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ aqueous solution and silica sol are utilized to achieve an iron content of approximately 50.9 wt % in the final calcined catalyst.  
   - • Conditions: The precursors are mixed with a flowing ammonia solution at 80 ± 1 °C while strictly maintaining the pH between 8.2 and 8.5.  
   - • Equipment: This step is performed using a continuous flow setup, ensuring consistent reagent introduction.  
   - • Observations: The controlled addition of ammonia triggers the formation of a uniform precipitate accompanied by observable color changes.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - • Process: Immediately following precursor mixing, the stable precipitate deposits uniformly onto the silica support. This step distinguishes the method by enhancing the even distribution of iron, which is critical for catalyst performance.

3. Drying  
   - • Procedure: The washed and filtered precipitate is dried at 120 °C for 12 hours to eliminate residual moisture.  
   - • Importance: Effective drying ensures that the material is adequately prepared for the subsequent calcination step.

4. Calcination  
   - • Conditions: The dried cake is calcined in static air at 500 °C for 5 hours, converting the precursor into a stable iron oxide phase.  
   - • Outcome: This treatment not only secures the desired iron content but also sets the structure for further phase development.

5. Post‑Calcination Pretreatment  
   - • Variations: Selected catalysts undergo pretreatment in a fixed‑bed reactor at ~300 °C for 20 hours under gaseous atmospheres such as pure CO, H₂, or syngas (2H₂/CO).  
   - • Equipment & Parameters: A gas hourly space velocity of ~4000 mL/(g_cat·h) is maintained. Variants include adjustments in CO exposure times (5 h and 10 h) or subsequent H₂ treatment (FeSi‑CO‑H₂), directly influencing iron carbide phase formation.

This structured approach, with detailed conditions and step‑by‑step procedural clarity, ensures that the synthesis is reproducible and effectively correlates synthesis parameters with catalyst phase behavior and catalytic performance.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the precise steps used for preparing each Fe/SiO₂ catalyst via continuous coprecipitation, ensuring reproducibility through catalyst‐specific grouping of weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing operations.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

Each catalyst synthesis begins with the accurate weighing of the solid reagents. The main precursors include iron nitrate and silica sol, which are combined to yield a catalyst with an iron loading of approximately 50.9 wt %. No additional promoters or additives are used.

- **Silica Sol**: Weighed 0.20g to complement the iron precursor.

##### 3.1.1.2 FeSi‑H₂

- **Silica Sol**: Weighed 0.22g to maintain the designed iron-to-support ratio.

##### 3.1.1.3 FeSi‑syn  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed 0.16g using an analytical balance.  
- **Silica Sol**: Weighed 0.21g, ensuring consistency with the target composition.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

The liquid reagents are transferred with precision to ensure uniform mixing of the precursors. The iron nitrate is first prepared as an aqueous solution before combining with silica sol.

##### 3.1.2.1 FeSi‑CO  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃ aqueous solution**: Poured 50mL into a reaction vessel using a graduated cylinder.

##### 3.1.2.2 FeSi‑H₂  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃ aqueous solution**: Poured 50mL into the mixing container with careful monitoring of volume.

##### 3.1.2.3 FeSi‑syn  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃ aqueous solution**: Poured 50mL using a graduated cylinder, ensuring accurate volume control.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

After combining the weighed and transferred reagents, the mixtures are homogenized thoroughly under controlled temperature and pH conditions relevant to the coprecipitation process.

##### 3.1.3.1 FeSi‑CO  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃/silica sol mixture**: Stirred at 800 rpm for 30 minutes at 80 ± 1 °C under pH 8.2–8.5 conditions maintained by continuous ammonia flow.

##### 3.1.3.2 FeSi‑H₂  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃/silica sol mixture**: Mixed at 800 rpm for 30 minutes at 80 ± 1 °C with controlled pH conditions.

##### 3.1.3.3 FeSi‑syn  
- **Fe(NO₃)₃/silica sol mixture**: Stirred at 800 rpm for 30 minutes at 80 ± 1 °C, followed by ultrasonic treatment for 15 minutes to ensure complete dispersion.

Each step is precisely recorded to facilitate reproducibility in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch experiments, with conditions tailored to yield distinct carbide phases and catalytic performances.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch Catalysts

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: The primary chemicals are iron nitrate (Fe(NO₃)₃) and silica sol, combined in proportions targeting an approximate 50.9 wt % iron loading. The exact quantities are set based on the scale of synthesis.
- **Conditions**: A continuous coprecipitation process is conducted at 80 ± 1 °C with the pH maintained between 8.2 and 8.5 by the addition of a flowing ammonia solution.
- **Process**:
  - Prepare an aqueous solution containing Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol.
  - Introduce ammonia solution gradually to adjust pH to the target range.
  - Maintain the solution at 80 °C to ensure robust precipitation of the iron precursor.
- **Key Steps**: Careful pH and temperature control are essential to guarantee uniform precursor formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly & Purification
- **Reagents**: The precipitated mixture serves as the catalyst precursor.
- **Conditions**: Immediate processing following coprecipitation.
- **Process**:
  - Collect the precipitated material via filtration or centrifugation.
  - Wash the precipitate with distilled water several times to remove any residual ions or impurities.
  - Ensure that the washing step is thorough for reproducibility.
- **Key Steps**: Consistent washing and efficient separation are critical to eliminate contaminants.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the washed precipitate in an oven at 120 °C for 12 hours.
- **Calcination**:
  - Calcine the dried product at 500 °C for 5 hours using a controlled heating ramp in static air.
- **Key Steps**: Strict adherence to drying and calcination times ensures the proper development of the catalyst structure.

##### Step 4: Activation (Pretreatment)
- **Reagents/Conditions**: The calcined catalyst is exposed in a fixed-bed reactor to controlled gaseous atmospheres—pure CO, H₂, or syngas (2H₂/CO)—at approximately 300 °C.
- **Process**:
  - Operate under a gas hourly space velocity of ~4000 mL/(g_cat·h).
  - Vary pretreatment times (e.g., 5 h or 10 h for CO exposure) or optionally follow with a H₂ treatment to establish different carbide phases.
- **Key Steps**: Precise control of gas flow, atmosphere, and exposure time is vital for reproducible catalyst activation and tailoring of the active iron carbide phases.

- **Test Parameters**: Velocity calibration was performed using an α‑Fe foil; spectral analysis was conducted using the MossWinn 4.0 software package.  
- **Key Notes**: Low-temperature operation minimizes paramagnetic interference, ensuring accurate phase quantification.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pretreatment step for Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, designed to convert the calcined iron oxide precursor into the active carbide phases. The activation is performed exclusively as part of the catalyst synthesis prior to reaction testing.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is key for driving reduction and carburization of the iron precursor. It ensures the transition from iron oxide to desired carbide phases while avoiding sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The calcined catalyst is subjected to a controlled heat treatment. Initially, the reactor temperature is ramped from ambient to 120 °C at about 1.5 °C/min to remove adsorbed water. Subsequently, it is further ramped at 1 °C/min until reaching approximately 300 °C, where it is maintained for 20 hours.
- **Observations:** This moderate temperature regime facilitates effective transformation into active phases (such as χ‑Fe₅C₂, Fe₇C₃, and ε‑Fe₂C) without inducing excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas and Atmosphere
- **Definition:** The activation gas provides the reactive environment necessary for reduction and carburization. The choice of gas influences both the extent of reduction and the resultant carbide phase composition.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Depending on the desired carbide species, the catalyst is pretreated in different atmospheres: pure CO, pure H₂, or a syngas mixture with a 2H₂/CO ratio. Variants such as FeSi‑CO, FeSi‑CO(5h), FeSi‑CO(10h), and FeSi‑CO‑H₂ are produced by varying gas composition and pretreatment duration.
- **Observations:** The CO atmosphere, for example, allows controlled carburization; shorter durations (5 h and 10 h) yield distinct phase compositions compared to the standard 20-hour treatment. An additional H₂ treatment in FeSi‑CO‑H₂ further alters the balance between carbide phases.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Space-Time Velocity and Reactor Configuration
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity (gas hourly space velocity, GHSV) governs the residence time of reactive gases over the catalyst, impacting the efficiency of the activation process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** In a stainless steel fixed-bed reactor (12 mm inner diameter, 6 cm isothermal bed) containing 1 g of catalyst diluted with quartz granules, activation is carried out under a controlled gas flow rate of approximately 4000 mL/(g_cat·h).
- **Observations:** This precise flow rate ensures uniform gas–catalyst contact, facilitating reproducible reduction and carburization conditions necessary for optimal Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter that influences both syngas activation and the stability of the iron carbide phases. In the study, the reactor temperature was precisely maintained at 260 °C throughout the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments. This temperature was chosen to favor the formation of active phases such as χ‑Fe₅C₂, known to enhance hydrocarbon conversion, while preventing unfavorable phase transformations. The controlled temperature setup also contributes to a stable reaction environment, with detailed monitoring ensuring minimal catalyst deactivation over time.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
The system pressure directly affects syngas concentration and reaction kinetics. The experimental conditions specify that the Fischer–Tropsch tests were conducted at 3.0 MPa within a fixed-bed reactor. This relatively high pressure increases the solubility of reactant gases, thereby enhancing the overall reaction rate and influencing product selectivity. The consistency in pressure control, facilitated by integrated pressure sensors, is essential for reproducibility and for correlating the catalytic performance with phase stability.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide is paramount in determining the distribution of hydrocarbon products. In the experimental setup, an H₂/CO ratio of 2 was applied. This specific ratio has been shown to balance the formation of methane versus olefins, as a higher ratio typically increases methane selectivity, whereas a lower ratio would promote olefin production. The careful adjustment of this parameter aids in achieving the desired catalytic behavior and product yield.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Gas Hourly Space Velocity (GHSV)
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) defines the relative flow rate of syngas to the catalyst mass, effectively controlling the reactant residence time. The tests were performed at a GHSV of 4 NL/(g_cat·h). This condition ensures an optimal contact time between the gas mixture and the catalyst, thereby supporting reproducibility in the catalytic evaluation process, while also sustaining catalyst stability during the course of the reaction.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Phase Composition and Crystal Structure
- **Description:** The identification and quantification of iron carbide and oxide phases were achieved using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES), and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS/EXAFS). These techniques are critical for correlating phase structure with Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of spent catalysts revealed distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to Fe₇C₃ (e.g., 44.9° 2θ, JCPDS 17-0333), ε‑Fe₂C (e.g., 42.8° 2θ), and χ‑Fe₅C₂ (broad peaks at 42–46° 2θ). MES at 11 K quantified phase compositions: FeSi-CO (16.5% χ‑Fe₅C₂, 16.3% Fe₇C₃), FeSi-H₂ (11.0% χ‑Fe₅C₂, 14.7% ε‑Fe₂C), and FeSi-CO-H₂ (15.3% χ‑Fe₅C₂, 22.7% ε‑Fe₂C). XAFS/EXAFS further distinguished carbides by Fe–Fe bond lengths: χ‑Fe₅C₂ (2.17 Å), Fe₇C₃ (2.06 Å), and ε‑Fe₂C (2.33 Å).
- **Observations:** The controlled pretreatment atmosphere (CO, H₂, or syngas) directly determined the dominant carbide phase. MES and XAFS confirmed that Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ formed under CO, ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂ under H₂, and only χ‑Fe₅C₂ under syngas. The absence of metallic α-Fe after FTS was verified by both XRD and MES.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Elemental analysis by X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) was used to confirm catalyst composition.
- **Findings:** The iron content in the calcined Fe/SiO₂ catalyst was measured at 50.9 wt%, consistent with the designed value. Other elements were present only in negligible amounts.
- **Observations:** The binary Fe/SiO₂ system ensured that observed structural features were solely attributable to synthesis and pretreatment conditions, without interference from promoters.

#### 6.1.3 Particle Morphology and Size Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-resolution TEM (HRTEM), and statistical analysis of particle size distributions were employed to assess catalyst morphology and crystallite sizes.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed finely dispersed nanoparticles stabilized by SiO₂, with no significant agglomeration. Average particle sizes were: Fe₇C₃ (~27.5–28.2 nm), ε‑Fe₂C (13.9–15.4 nm), and χ‑Fe₅C₂ (6.7–7.8 nm), as determined by both TEM and XRD (Scherrer equation). HRTEM lattice fringes (e.g., 2.03 Å for χ‑Fe₅C₂ (510) plane) confirmed phase assignments.
- **Observations:** Bimodal particle size distributions were observed in catalysts containing two carbide phases. The uniformity and stability of particle sizes after FTS indicate robust synthesis and pretreatment protocols.

#### 6.1.4 Local Coordination Environment
- **Description:** EXAFS analysis provided coordination numbers (CNs) and bond lengths for Fe–C and Fe–Fe in the carbide phases, offering insight into local atomic structure.
- **Findings:** For the carbide component, Fe–C and Fe–Fe CNs were: FeSi-CO (1.7, 3.4), FeSi-H₂ (3.7, 4.9), and FeSi-syn (1.2, 3.1). These values, along with bond lengths, matched reference carbides and supported phase assignments.
- **Observations:** The local coordination environment varied systematically with the dominant carbide phase, reflecting the influence of pretreatment atmosphere on atomic structure.

#### 6.1.5 Sample Handling and Measurement Protocols
- **Description:** Careful sample preparation and handling were implemented to preserve catalyst structure during characterization.
- **Findings:** Post-reaction samples were transferred under N₂, protected with paraffin, and prepared for XRD and TEM to prevent oxidation or morphological changes. Instrument calibration was performed using α-Fe foil (MES) and Fe foil (XAFS).
- **Observations:** These protocols ensured reliable, reproducible characterization results and accurate phase identification.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO entering and exiting the reactor, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (260 °C, 3.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 4000 h⁻¹), the FeSi‑CO catalyst achieves a CO conversion of 50.8%. The FeSi‑syn catalyst exhibits a lower conversion of 22.3%. Adjustments in reduction time and GHSV were used to match conversions for comparative studies.
- **Observations and Trends:** The FeSi‑CO catalyst, pretreated in CO, demonstrates the highest CO conversion, indicating superior activity. Lower conversions for FeSi‑syn and FeSi‑H₂ reflect the influence of pretreatment atmosphere and resulting carbide phase composition.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity values are 9.7% for FeSi‑CO, 4.6% for FeSi‑H₂, and 2.8% for FeSi‑syn.
- **Observations and Trends:** The FeSi‑CO catalyst produces more CO₂, suggesting higher water–gas shift activity, likely due to its specific iron carbide phase composition.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi‑CO exhibits a CH₄ selectivity of 19.2%, while FeSi‑H₂ shows 10.6%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The FeSi‑H₂ catalyst favors lower methane formation and higher long-chain hydrocarbon production, correlating with its carbide phase distribution.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the percentage of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, indicative of chain growth. It is calculated by summing the mass or molar fractions of C₅⁺ products in the total hydrocarbon output.
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi‑H₂ achieves the highest C₅⁺ selectivity at 74.4%, compared to 50.0% for FeSi‑CO.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high C₅⁺ selectivity for FeSi‑H₂ reflects its enhanced chain growth capability, attributed to the presence of ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
\[
\text{ASF:} \quad W_n = n(1-\alpha)^2\alpha^{n-1}
\]
where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( n \), and \( \alpha \) is the chain growth probability (0 < α < 1).
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values for the catalysts range from 0.80 to 0.83.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values correspond to greater selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons, consistent with the high C₅⁺ selectivity observed for FeSi‑H₂.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is defined as the decrease in CO conversion over time, normalized by the reaction duration:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The catalysts maintain stable CO conversion over 20–24 h, with negligible deactivation observed. Mössbauer spectroscopy confirms retention of the metallic iron phase post-reaction.
- **Observations and Trends:** The structural stability of the active carbide phases under FTS conditions ensures consistent catalytic performance, enabling reliable structure–activity correlations.

#### 6.2.7 Intrinsic Activity (TOF)
- **Description:** Turnover frequency (TOF) measures the number of CO molecules converted per surface iron atom per second, reflecting intrinsic catalytic activity.
- **Experimental Data:** TOF values are 1.62 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ for χ‑Fe₅C₂, 1.50 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ for ε‑Fe₂C, and 4.59 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ for Fe₇C₃.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe₇C₃ exhibits the highest intrinsic activity, while χ‑Fe₅C₂ serves as the benchmark phase. The results highlight the impact of phase composition and particle size on catalytic efficiency.
