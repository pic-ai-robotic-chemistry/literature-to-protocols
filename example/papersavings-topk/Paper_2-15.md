## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

#### Raney Iron Precursor Synthesis and Promoter Incorporation

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - The synthesis begins with the introduction of iron–aluminium alloy powder into an 8 mol·L⁻¹ KOH solution to selectively dissolve aluminum.  
   - The resultant mixture is washed rigorously: deionized water is used for ten consecutive washes followed by seven ethanol washes, ensuring the removal of residual K⁺ and AlO₂⁻ ions.  
   - The cleaned precursor is subsequently dried in an argon flow at room temperature for 6 hours, maintaining an oxygen‑free environment to prevent premature oxidation.

2. Precipitation–Deposition (Promoter Incorporation)  
   - Immediately after precursor drying, a wet impregnation step is implemented by adding a Mn(NO₃)₂ solution dropwise, targeting loadings between 0.5–12.5 wt% of manganese.  
   - In parallel or as an alternative, potassium is introduced via a KNO₃ solution at a fixed loading of 1 wt%.  
   - Post‑impregnation, the sample is dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for approximately 12 hours to secure homogeneous promoter distribution and adhesion.

3. Post‑treatment Procedures  
   - Passivation is achieved by exposing the dried precursor to 1% O₂ in helium at room temperature for 20 hours, which stabilizes the iron surface and prevents uncontrolled oxidation.  
   - Carburization follows: the passivated precursor is subjected to a syngas flow comprising H₂, CO, and He in the ratio of 100:3.2:21.8. The temperature is increased at a controlled rate of 1°C per minute until reaching 350°C, where the sample is held for a dwell time of 6 hours to effect complete conversion to phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂.

### Comparative Analysis  
- Advantages: This method offers a streamlined process that is scalable and minimizes equipment complexity while ensuring high phase purity and reproducibility.  
- Disadvantages: The multi‐step protocol requires stringent control over washing, drying, and temperature parameters to avoid contamination and ensure uniform promoter incorporation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and structural evolution of the catalyst were determined using in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Rietveld refinement. These techniques are critical for confirming the formation and purity of the active χ‐Fe₅C₂ (Hägg carbide) phase, which is essential for optimal Fischer–Tropsch (FT) performance.
- **Findings:** In situ XRD, performed on a Rigaku D/max-2600/PC instrument with a D/teX ultrahigh-speed detector, monitored the transformation of the Raney iron precursor under a syngas atmosphere (H₂/CO/He = 100/3.2/21.8) as the temperature was ramped at 0.5°C/min to 350°C and held for 6 hours. The onset of χ‐Fe₅C₂ formation was observed at 300°C, with complete conversion at 350°C. Rietveld refinement (Fullprof software) confirmed phase purity, with no detectable secondary phases. The reported goodness-of-fit parameters (χ² = 7.58%, Rp = 18.7%, Rwp = 15.3%, Rexp = 5.6%) and lattice parameters matched published values for χ‐Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The exclusive presence of χ‐Fe₅C₂, as verified by both XRD and Rietveld analysis, demonstrates successful synthesis of the desired active phase, which is maintained throughout activation and reaction.

#### 6.1.2 Morphological Evolution and Microstructure
- **Description:** Environmental transmission electron microscopy (ETEM) was employed to visualize the morphological transformation of the catalyst at the nanoscale during carburization. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) imaging provided detailed insights into the spatial progression of phase changes.
- **Findings:** The initial passivated Raney iron precursor consisted of crystalline iron particles enveloped by an amorphous oxide layer. Upon exposure to syngas (H₂/CO = 30, 1,200 Pa, 350°C), the transformation to χ‐Fe₅C₂ initiated at the particle core and propagated outward, completing within 30 minutes at low CO partial pressure (40 Pa). The final χ‐Fe₅C₂ grains exhibited a characteristic lattice spacing of ~2.7 Å along the (311) direction.
- **Observations:** The rapid and uniform transformation, as well as the preservation of particle integrity, indicate effective carburization and structural robustness, with no evidence of carbon deposition or promoter migration in Mn-promoted samples.

#### 6.1.3 Phase Purity and Stability (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was utilized to confirm the phase purity and stability of the catalyst under both synthesis and FT reaction conditions. Measurements were conducted at −153°C to enhance spectral resolution.
- **Findings:** Spectra collected after carburization and following FT operation revealed only the χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase, with no signals from iron oxides or other carbides. This result was consistent for both unpromoted and Mn-promoted catalysts.
- **Observations:** The persistence of a single-phase signature after prolonged catalytic operation (up to 225 hours) demonstrates the exceptional stability and phase purity of the catalyst under industrially relevant conditions.

#### 6.1.4 Structural Effects of Promoter Incorporation
- **Description:** The impact of manganese (Mn) and potassium (K) promoters on the catalyst structure was assessed through comparative analysis of unpromoted and promoted samples using the above techniques.
- **Findings:** Mn and K incorporation via wet impregnation did not alter the χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase or induce secondary phases. TEM and Mössbauer data confirmed the absence of Mn migration or carbon deposition, and the structural integrity of the carbide phase was preserved.
- **Observations:** Promoter addition enhances catalyst stability and selectivity by suppressing carbon deposition and facilitating oxygen removal, without compromising the structural or phase purity of χ‐Fe₅C₂.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst, typically expressed as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ catalyst demonstrates high CO conversion across a range of conditions. At 250°C and 2.3 MPa, unpromoted χ‐Fe₅C₂ achieves 27.8% CO conversion, while Mn promotion further enhances conversion, with values up to 91.2% at 320°C. The catalyst time yield (CTY) at 250°C is reported to be 3–7 times higher than state-of-the-art references, and a CTY of 265 μmol g_cat⁻¹ s⁻¹ is maintained over 225 h at 320–325°C.
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese promotion significantly increases CO conversion and stability, outperforming literature catalysts, especially at lower temperatures. Optimal promoter levels are critical for maximizing conversion while maintaining selectivity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ at 250°C, CO₂ selectivity is 11%. Under industrial conditions, phase-pure χ‐Fe₅C₂ achieves 9% CO₂ selectivity. Reference catalysts typically exhibit higher CO₂ selectivity (37–47%), with only a few exceptions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The integration of manganese and potassium promoters suppresses CO₂ formation by enhancing oxygen removal, resulting in lower CO₂ selectivity compared to unpromoted or reference catalysts.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit CH₄ selectivity values are not detailed, the article notes that phase-pure χ‐Fe₅C₂ and manganese promotion suppress methane formation, favoring higher selectivity to longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Structural purity and promoter optimization are linked to reduced methane selectivity, as confirmed by in situ characterization.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The catalyst achieves up to 51% selectivity to C₂–C₁₀ linear α-olefins under industrial conditions, with overall carbon efficiency reaching 89%.
- **Observations and Trends:** High C₃⁺ selectivity is attributed to the suppression of secondary reactions and the maintenance of phase-pure χ‐Fe₅C₂.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model. Higher α values indicate a greater tendency toward long-chain hydrocarbon production.
- **Experimental Data:** The product distribution for Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ fits the ASF model, with α values consistent with high selectivity to C₂–C₁₀ olefins (up to 51%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese promotion and phase purity enhance the α factor, favoring the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ maintains stable CO conversion and selectivity over 225 h at 320–325°C and 2.3 MPa, with negligible deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibits exceptional long-term stability, attributed to promoter effects and phase purity, making it suitable for industrial FT synthesis.
