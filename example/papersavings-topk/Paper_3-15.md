## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis is based on a controlled precipitation method designed to form ZnFe2O4 spinel nanoparticles that are later transformed into the active Fe0@ZnO structure through hydrothermal reduction.

#### Co‑precipitation and Precipitation–Deposition Method
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (total 0.18 mol in 500 mL deionized water) with a Fe:Zn ratio of 4:1, 2:1, or 1:1.  
  – Stir the solution at 700 rpm for 30 minutes.  
2. • pH Adjustment and Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Slowly add ammonia to adjust the solution to pH 9.  
  – After the pH adjustment, the precipitation–deposition step facilitates the formation of ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles; the process is continued with vigorous stirring for 1 hour.  
3. • Drying  
  – Filter and wash the resulting precipitate with deionized water.  
  – Dry the washed material at 100 °C for 12 hours to remove residual solvent.
4. • Calcination  
  – Calcine the dried powders in an air atmosphere at 550 °C for 5 hours to stabilize the catalyst phase.
5. • Post‑synthesis Hydrothermal Reduction  
  – Reduce the calcined material in a fixed-bed reactor using H₂ at 450 °C for 5 hours under 0.2 MPa. This step converts ZnFe2O4 into the active Fe0@ZnO structure.  
  – A passivation step with 1% O₂/99% N₂ at room temperature for 2 hours is employed to prevent oxidation.

### Advantages and Limitations
• Comparative analysis reveals that while the co‑precipitation method is relatively simple, cost‐effective, and promotes fine dispersion of iron species, the reproducibility and scalability can be challenging due to the critical pH control and particle size distribution.  
• The incorporation of promoters (Na ≈1.7–1.8 wt% and Na₂S of 0.11 or 0.22 wt%) is achieved via incipient wetness impregnation after calcination, further influencing the final catalyst properties.

This step‐by‐step methodology, with precisely maintained reaction conditions (exact temperatures, durations, and reagent concentrations), ensures high reproducibility and consistent catalytic performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 and NaS‐Fe1Zn1 Series

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and 2.5 g Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (molar ratio Fe:Zn = 4:1); deionized water (50 mL); ammonia solution.
- **Conditions**: Stir at 700 rpm at room temperature; initial pH adjustment followed by controlled dropwise addition of ammonia to reach pH 9.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O in deionized water until a homogeneous solution is obtained.
  - Stir the solution for 1 hour, then adjust the pH to 9 by adding ammonia dropwise while monitoring continuously.
  - Continue stirring for an additional 30 minutes to ensure complete precipitation of intermediate hydroxides.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure the pH control and stirring rate are maintained to facilitate uniform precursor formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: The precursor suspension prepared above, plus additional Na₂CO₃ solution to introduce Na (targeting ≈1.7–1.8 wt%) and Na₂S solution (0.11–0.22 wt% depending on the desired formulation).
- **Conditions**: Co-precipitation under constant stirring at room temperature.
- **Process**:
  - Impregnate the precursor separately with Na₂CO₃ and, where required, Na₂S.
  - Mix thoroughly to ensure uniform distribution of promoters into the precursor matrix.
- **Key Steps**: Careful control of promoter concentrations is essential for reproducibility.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Centrifuge the reaction mixture at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes.
  - Wash the collected precipitate repeatedly with deionized water to remove unreacted species.
- **Key Steps**: Multiple washing cycles are important to eliminate impurities.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the precipitate in an oven at 100 °C for 12 hours.
- **Calcination**: Heat in a muffle furnace with a controlled ramp to 400 °C and hold for 4 hours in air, ensuring transformation of ZnFe₂O₄ spinel.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform drying ensures effective calcination.

##### Step 5: Activation
- **Process**:
  - Reduce the calcined catalyst in a fixed-bed reactor under a flow of H₂ at 450 °C for 5 hours to form the active Fe⁰@ZnO structure.
  - Cool the catalyst to 290 °C before feed introduction.
- **Key Steps**: Strict adherence to activation temperature and cooling protocols is vital to stabilize the active phases.

This detailed, stepwise workflow—from precursor preparation, catalyst assembly, and purification to drying, calcination, and final activation—ensures that the synthesis procedure is clear, reproducible, and aligned with the specific operational parameters reported in the study.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical step ensuring that the precursor ZnFe₂O₄ spinel is effectively transformed into the active Fe⁰@ZnO structure, which enhances catalyst dispersion and stability during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. The following parameters, extracted directly from the experimental protocol, define this pre-reaction treatment.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential to achieve complete reduction of ZnFe₂O₄, facilitating its conversion into metallic Fe⁰ and ZnO.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst was reduced at 450 °C in a fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** This high-temperature treatment promotes the formation of relatively large Fe⁰ particles (with accompanying nano-sized ZnO) that help inhibit sintering during the reaction.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure impacts the reduction kinetics by ensuring sufficient hydrogen availability during the reduction process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction was performed under a hydrogen pressure of 0.2 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at this pressure ensures effective and controlled reduction without premature sintering or structural degradation.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The hydrogen gas serves as the reducing agent needed for converting the spinel structure.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A flowing H₂ atmosphere was applied at a rate of 40 mL/min.
- **Observations:** The constant flow of H₂ under the specified conditions is fundamental to achieving uniform reduction and the subsequent formation of the Fe⁰@ZnO structure.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of catalyst activation is crucial in guaranteeing complete reduction while preventing over-exposure that could lead to sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst activation lasted for 5 hours.
- **Observations:** This time frame was optimized to ensure a reproducible transformation where ZnO effectively inhibits the aggregation of Fe⁰ particles.

*Note:* The activation parameters outlined here exclusively pertain to the catalyst pre-treatment for Fischer-Tropsch reactions and are distinctly separated from any procedures used solely for characterization purposes. The fixed-bed reactor, with an inner diameter of 1 cm and catalyst dilution with quartz powder, further supports the reproducibility of the reduction process.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS) reaction is conducted at a fixed temperature of 320 °C, a value directly extracted from the article. This temperature is critical for activating syngas and promoting the conversion of CO₂ to hydrocarbons. Under these conditions, the 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 catalyst exhibits high CO₂ conversion and favorable product selectivity toward lower olefins. Observations indicate that, despite an initial induction period, the catalyst maintains stability with minimal deactivation throughout the testing period.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
The reaction is maintained at 1 MPa, with the fixed-bed reactor ensuring that this pressure is steadily controlled by gradual adjustment during setup. The elevated pressure enhances syngas concentration, thereby shifting equilibrium conditions to favor CO transformation. This pressure setting is essential for balancing the CO dissociation and hydrogenation steps, which in turn supports optimal catalytic performance and product distribution.

#### 5.2.3 H₂/CO₂ Ratio
A H₂/CO₂ molar ratio of 3 is employed as specified in the article. This ratio plays a pivotal role in influencing the balance between CO₂ conversion and hydrocarbon selectivity. According to the reported findings, the chosen ratio improves the conversion efficiency and promotes the formation of C₂–C₅ light olefins, while concurrently suppressing CO selectivity. The study highlights that altering this ratio can significantly affect the hydrogenation pathways, thereby modulating the production of methane versus olefinic products.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity (GHSV)
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is set to 1200 mL⋅g_cat⁻¹⋅h⁻¹. This parameter directly impacts the contact time between the reactant gas stream and the catalyst. The article correlates a lower GHSV with enhanced CO₂ equilibrium conversion and a substantial decrease in the formation of undesired byproducts, such as CO and methane. Additionally, the controlled GHSV aids in maintaining catalyst stability over extended testing durations, proving effective in mitigating issues related to catalyst deactivation and carbon deposition.



## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** Crystal phase composition and transformations were analyzed using X-ray Diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy to elucidate the evolution of active phases during catalyst synthesis, reduction, and reaction. These phases are critical for Fischer-Tropsch activity and selectivity.
- **Findings:** Fresh catalysts exhibited ZnFe₂O₄ spinel (PDF#77–11) and a weak ZnO peak (PDF#79–208). After H₂ reduction at 450 °C, ZnFe₂O₄ was fully converted to metallic Fe⁰ (PDF#87–721) and ZnO, with Fe⁰ displaying higher crystallinity. In spent catalysts after CO₂-FTS, 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 contained Fe₃O₄ (PDF#82–1533) and χ‐Fe₅C₂ (PDF#89–2544), while S-doped samples (e.g., 1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1) showed Fe⁰, Fe₃C (PDF#23–1113), and χ‐Fe₅C₂, with Fe₃O₄ absent. Under CO-FTS, all spent catalysts exhibited χ‐Fe₅C₂ and ZnO, with no Fe₃O₄ detected, regardless of S content.
- **Observations:** Na₂S addition suppressed Fe₃O₄ formation and promoted Fe₃C, particularly under CO₂-FTS, correlating with reduced carburization and altered catalytic performance. XRD and Mössbauer data confirmed phase stability over extended operation (up to 1000 h).

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were determined by BET analysis (Micromeritics ASAP 2020) at 77 K, following degassing at 200 °C for 4 h. Surface area, pore volume, and average pore diameter are key for reactant diffusion and catalyst dispersion.
- **Findings:**  
  - 1.5Na-Fe: 37 m²/g, 0.74 cm³/g, 5.90 nm  
  - 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1: 47 m²/g, 0.49 cm³/g, 3.10 nm  
  - 1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1: 44 m²/g, 0.56 cm³/g, 3.80 nm  
  - 1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1: 45 m²/g, 0.51 cm³/g, 3.30 nm  
- **Observations:** Zn addition increased surface area and improved dispersion, while S addition had minimal effect on textural properties. Enhanced surface area and moderate pore sizes support improved reactant accessibility and catalytic efficiency.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphology and particle size distribution were examined by SEM (Hitachi Regulus 8100), TEM (FEI TE20), and HADDF-STEM, with EDX mapping for elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** SEM revealed block-like particles with surface protrusions in fresh catalysts, attributed to ZnFe₂O₄ and ZnO. TEM of reduced catalysts showed large Fe⁰ nanoparticles (260–290 nm) with smaller ZnO (60–70 nm) on their surfaces, forming Fe⁰@ZnO structures. In spent catalysts, S-free samples developed thick carbon shells (up to 28 nm) around Fe particles, while S-modified catalysts exhibited less carbon deposition and more uniform Fe and C distribution.
- **Observations:** ZnO inhibited Fe⁰ aggregation, and Na₂S addition suppressed carbon shell formation, enhancing catalyst stability during CO-FTS.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Phase Quantification
- **Description:** Elemental content was quantified by ICP-OES, and iron phase composition by Mössbauer spectroscopy.
- **Findings:** Actual Na and S contents closely matched theoretical values (Na ~1.7–1.8 wt%, S 0.11–0.22 wt%). After CO₂-FTS, 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 contained 32% Fe₃O₄ and 68% χ‐Fe₅C₂, while 1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1 had 30.7% Fe⁰, 28.0% Fe₃C, and 41.3% χ‐Fe₅C₂. After CO-FTS, both S-free and S-containing catalysts were dominated by χ‐Fe₅C₂ and Fe₃C, with minimal iron oxides.
- **Observations:** These results confirm that Na₂S modulates phase composition, particularly under CO₂-FTS, and that the catalysts maintain compositional stability during long-term operation.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  X_{\mathrm{CO}_2} = \frac{(A_{\mathrm{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\mathrm{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\mathrm{CO}_2})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\mathrm{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{(A_{\mathrm{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\mathrm{N}_2})_{\text{in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \(A_i\) denotes the GC peak area of component \(i\).
- **Experimental Data:** Under CO₂-FTS conditions (320 °C, 1 MPa, H₂/CO₂ = 3, GHSV = 1200 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹), the 1.5Na-Fe₁Zn₁ catalyst achieved a CO₂ conversion of 39.0%. Increasing the H₂/CO₂ ratio from 1 to 3 raised conversion from 17.5% to 39.0%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal Fe/Zn ratio (1:1) and Na loading (1.5 wt%) maximize conversion. Addition of Na₂S decreases CO₂ conversion, with higher S content further suppressing activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO Selectivity
- **Description:** CO selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO₂ that forms CO, primarily via the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  S_{\mathrm{CO}} = \frac{f_{\mathrm{CO}} \cdot (A_{\mathrm{CO}})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\mathrm{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{f_{\mathrm{CO}_2} \cdot [(A_{\mathrm{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\mathrm{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\mathrm{CO}_2})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\mathrm{N}_2})_{\text{out}}]} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under CO₂-FTS, S_CO values are: 1.5Na-Fe₁Zn₁: 15.6%; 1.5Na0.1S-Fe₁Zn₁: 42.6%; 1.5Na0.2S-Fe₁Zn₁: 71.3%.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO selectivity increases sharply with Na₂S addition, indicating a shift toward RWGS and away from hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO₂ yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  S_{\mathrm{CH}_4} = \frac{F_{\mathrm{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\mathrm{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\mathrm{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under CO₂-FTS: 1.5Na-Fe₁Zn₁: 12.7%; 1.5Na0.1S-Fe₁Zn₁: 55.9%; 1.5Na0.2S-Fe₁Zn₁: 58.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Na₂S addition dramatically increases CH₄ selectivity, reflecting enhanced hydrogenation and suppressed olefin formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO₂ forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbons:
  \[
  S_{C_3^+} = \frac{\text{Moles of } C_3^+ \text{ hydrocarbons}}{\text{Total moles of hydrocarbons}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** In CO-FTS, C₄⁺ olefin selectivity reaches ~65.4% with Na₂S addition. After ZSM-5 cracking, C₂–C₅ olefin selectivity increases to 64.6%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Na₂S reduces C₃⁺ selectivity in CO₂-FTS but enhances long-chain olefin selectivity in CO-FTS, especially after secondary catalytic cracking.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, indicates the probability of chain growth:
  \[
  \alpha = \frac{\text{Moles of C}_{n+1}}{\text{Moles of C}_n}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Specific α values are not directly reported, but trends are discussed.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α correlates with greater long-chain hydrocarbon formation. Na₂S addition lowers α in CO₂-FTS (favoring chain termination and light products), but maintains high α in CO-FTS, supporting long-chain olefin production.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate measures the decline in conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{X_{\text{initial}} - X_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For 1.5Na-Fe (without Zn), CO₂ conversion drops from 43.3% to 38.3% over 20 h. With Na₂S in CO-FTS, high CO conversion (~96%) and selectivity are sustained over 1000 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** Na₂S addition significantly improves stability in CO-FTS by suppressing carbon deposition, as confirmed by TEM and TG analyses.

**Summary:** The 1.5Na-Fe₁Zn₁ catalyst series demonstrates tunable performance via promoter modification. Na₂S addition shifts selectivity and stability profiles, with contrasting effects under CO₂-FTS and CO-FTS. Optimal promoter ratios and secondary catalytic cracking enable high selectivity for light and long-chain α-olefins, with robust long-term stability under CO-FTS conditions.
