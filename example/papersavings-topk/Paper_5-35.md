## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis of the Na‐FeC*x* Fischer–Tropsch catalyst and the preparation of zeolite promoters, ensuring reproducibility through a clearly defined sequence of procedures.

#### Na‐FeC*x* Catalyst Synthesis
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Reagents: 30.0 g of FeCl₃⋅6H₂O and 11.9 g of FeCl₂⋅4H₂O are dissolved in 200 mL deionized water along with 5.0 mL of aqueous HCl (12.0 mol L⁻¹).  
  – Equipment: Standard glass reactor with controlled stirring.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – A 2.0 mol L⁻¹ NaOH aqueous solution is added dropwise under vigorous stirring until the solution reaches a pH of 10.  
  – The mixture is stirred for an additional 1 hour before the solid is separated via centrifugation.

3. • Drying  
  – The separated solid is dried at 100 °C overnight to remove moisture.

4. • Calcination  
  – The dried material is heated at 550 °C for 4 hours in air to eliminate organic templates.

5. • Post‑Treatment Steps  
  – The catalyst is then pre‑reduced at 400 °C for 6 hours in a pure H₂ atmosphere.  
  – Carbonization follows by treating the reduced catalyst with syngas (CO/H₂) at 300 °C for 24 hours.

#### Zeolite Promoter Synthesis
• s‑ZSM‑5 is synthesized via a hydrothermal method using an autoclave at 180 °C for 2 days.  
• The s‑S1‑OH promoter is prepared using an organosilane‑assisted modification: mixing TPAOH with a silica source and organosilane, followed by crystallization at 180 °C for 3 days. Post‑crystallization, the product is collected, washed, dried, and finally calcined at 550 °C for 4 hours.

#### Catalyst Granule Integration
• The Na‐FeC*x* catalyst and zeolite promoters are physically mixed in granule form with mesh sizes ranging from 20 to 40. This granule mixing is critical to minimize sodium migration and to ensure optimal proximity between the active phase and the zeolite nanochannels.

This structured approach—with precise reagent concentrations, controlled pH adjustments, and clearly defined temperature and duration parameters—ensures that the synthesis is both reproducible and scalable.

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Na‐FeC*x*/s‐ZSM‐5

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 30.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 11.9 g FeCl₂·4H₂O, 200 mL deionized water, and 5.0 mL 12.0 M HCl.  
- **Conditions**: Dissolution at room temperature under continuous stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O and FeCl₂·4H₂O in the water and acid mixture to achieve a homogeneous solution.  
  - Adjust the pH to 10 by adding 2.0 M NaOH solution dropwise while stirring continuously for 0.5 hours, ensuring full precipitation of the metal hydroxide.

- **Process**:  
  - Separate the precipitate by centrifugation and wash it with deionized water multiple times (approximately six washes) to remove unreacted species.  
  - Dry the purified solid at 100 °C overnight in a convection oven.

##### Step 3: Calcination and Activation  
- **Drying & Calcination**:  
  - Calcine the dried precursor at 550 °C for 4 hours in air using a precision furnace; a controlled ramp rate is recommended though not explicitly specified.  
- **Activation**:  
  - Pre-reduce the calcined sample at 300 °C for 24 hours under a hydrogen atmosphere (H₂ flow at 10 mL/min) ensuring complete formation of the Na‐FeC*x* phase.

##### Step 4: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**: Use the activated Na‐FeC*x* and pre-prepared s‐ZSM‐5.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical preparatory step in the synthesis of the Na‐FeCₓ/zeolite Fischer–Tropsch catalysts. This activation, performed as a pre‐reaction treatment, ensures that the precursor ferric oxide is efficiently reduced to form the active metallic carbide phase, predominantly Fe₅C₂, while also maintaining the catalyst’s structural integrity with respect to sodium incorporation.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is the key thermal condition that drives the reduction of the iron oxide precursor, thereby enabling the subsequent formation of the active carbide phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 400 °C.
- **Observations:** Activation at 400 °C provides sufficient thermal energy for reduction; however, it may also promote sodium migration in systems with smaller granules or powder mixing, as evidenced by shifts in the Na 1s XPS peak. This emphasizes the need for careful control of granule size during synthesis.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Pressure during activation influences the kinetics of the reduction reaction by affecting gas diffusion and contact with the catalyst surface.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is carried out under atmospheric pressure (approximately 1 bar).
- **Observations:** Operating at atmospheric pressure ensures stable reduction kinetics without inducing catalyst agglomeration or undue structural stress.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas used during activation serves as the reducing agent, essential for stripping oxygen from the precursor and facilitating the transition to the active metallic phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A pure hydrogen (H₂) atmosphere is employed during the reduction process.
- **Observations:** Hydrogen effectively reduces the iron oxide to lower oxidation states, paving the way for the formation of the active Fe₅C₂ phase. Its use, however, requires precise management to balance reduction efficiency and control sodium migration.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Activation time is the duration for which the catalyst remains under the specified reduction conditions, ensuring complete transformation without prolonged exposure that could lead to detrimental effects.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under activation conditions for 6 hours.
- **Observations:** A 6‑hour reduction period is optimized to achieve full conversion to the active phase while minimizing the risk of extensive sodium migration that could compromise catalyst stability and performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was carried out in a stainless-steel fixed-bed reactor under rigorously controlled conditions to ensure reproducibility and reliable performance evaluation of the Na‐FeC*x*/s‐ZSM‐5 and Na‐FeC*x*/s‐S1‐OH catalysts.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
• Definition: The reaction temperature is critical for activating syngas (CO and H₂) and determining both catalyst activity and product selectivity.  
• Experimental Conditions: The reactor was maintained at 260 °C throughout the catalytic tests. Prior to reaction, the catalyst was pre‐reduced at 400 °C for 6 hours in a pure H₂ flow and then cooled to the designated reaction temperature of 260 °C.  
• Observations: At 260 °C, notable differences were observed between the bare and zeolite‐promoted catalysts. For instance, the bare Na‐FeC*x* exhibited only about 1.9% CO conversion while the promoted catalysts demonstrated significantly higher CO conversion and improved olefin selectivity under identical conditions.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
• Definition: Reaction pressure influences the effective concentration of syngas molecules in the reactor, thereby impacting the kinetics and overall conversion.  
• Experimental Conditions: The reaction was performed at a constant pressure of 2.0 MPa.  
• Observations: Operating at 2.0 MPa ensured that the syngas remained in close contact with the catalyst, contributing to efficient catalytic turnover and favorable hydrocarbon formation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio and Syngas Composition  
• Definition: The H₂/CO ratio plays a pivotal role in hydrocarbon chain growth and product distribution, while the overall syngas composition sets the stoichiometric balance for the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
• Experimental Conditions: The feed gas was composed of a CO/H₂/Ar ratio of 45/45/10, with adjustments to the H₂/CO ratio (e.g., elevated to 3 in selected tests for the Na‐FeC*x*/s‐S1‐OH variant) used to optimize CO conversion and product selectivity.  
• Observations: An increased H₂/CO ratio was correlated with enhanced CO conversion (up to 82.6% in some cases) and narrower product distributions dominated by C₁–C₁₀ hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity

• Observations: This value provided a balance between sufficient reactant contact and high catalyst stability, enabling a steady state to be achieved within the reactor.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases of the catalysts and zeolite promoters were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Rigaku D/MAX 2550 diffractometer (CuKα, λ = 1.5418 Å). XRD is essential for confirming the formation and stability of active phases under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that Fe₅C₂ is the dominant crystalline phase in both Na‐FeC*x*/s‐ZSM‐5 and Na‐FeC*x*/s‐S1‐OH catalysts after the LT-FTO reaction. The zeolite components retained their respective MFI structures, indicating structural integrity post-synthesis and reaction.
- **Observations:** The presence of Fe₅C₂, with negligible oxide phases, confirms effective carbonization and the formation of the catalytically active phase. The stability of the MFI structure in both s‐ZSM‐5 and s‐S1‐OH ensures consistent promoter function.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Sodium Migration
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi) was employed to determine elemental composition and to monitor sodium migration between Na‐FeC*x* and zeolite promoters. Shifts in Na 1s binding energy provide direct evidence of sodium redistribution.
- **Findings:** XPS analysis showed Na 1s peaks at 1071.8 eV for Na on FeC*x* and at 1073.3 eV for Na on zeolite. Intermediate binding energies were observed depending on the degree of mixing and treatment.
- **Observations:** Granule mixing (20–40 mesh) effectively minimized sodium migration, preserving sodium in the Na‐FeC*x* phase. Excessive migration, as seen in powder-mixed samples, led to sodium depletion and reduced catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen sorption isotherms (Micromeritics ASAP 2020M) were used to assess BET surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution. These parameters are critical for reactant diffusion and product desorption.
- **Findings:** s‐ZSM‐5 exhibited a surface area of 337–543 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.13–0.27 cm³/g. The b-axis thickness of 90–110 nm in s‐ZSM‐5 nanosheets resulted in slightly lower surface area but higher pore volume compared to conventional n‐ZSM‐5, indicating enhanced micropore accessibility. The introduction of silanol groups in s‐S1‐OH did not significantly alter pore volume.
- **Observations:** Rational design of zeolite morphology, particularly nanosheet thickness, optimizes pore accessibility and diffusion, supporting high catalytic efficiency.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Catalyst morphology and particle size were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU-8010/SU-1510). These techniques elucidate the physical structure and dispersion of active phases.
- **Findings:** SEM and TEM images confirmed that s‐ZSM‐5 and s‐S1‐OH possess sheet-like morphologies with controlled b-axis thickness (90–115 nm). Na‐FeC*x* displayed well-dispersed Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles. Granule mixing preserved the distinct morphologies of both catalyst and promoter.
- **Observations:** The nanosheet structure, achieved via organosilane-assisted synthesis, enhances accessibility to active sites. Granule mixing prevents excessive sodium migration and maintains optimal catalyst morphology.

#### 6.1.5 Olefin Adsorption/Desorption Dynamics
- **Description:** DRIFT (Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform) and TPD (Temperature-Programmed Desorption) studies were conducted to evaluate olefin adsorption and desorption on catalyst surfaces, providing insight into product desorption kinetics.
- **Findings:** Both s‐ZSM‐5 and s‐S1‐OH accelerated olefin desorption compared to Na‐FeC*x* alone or with n‐ZSM‐5. DRIFT showed rapid decrease in IR signal intensity for olefins, while TPD indicated lower desorption temperatures for nanosheet zeolites.
- **Observations:** Enhanced olefin desorption reduces re-adsorption and secondary reactions, directly contributing to higher olefin selectivity and productivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under LT-FTO conditions (260 °C, 2.0 MPa, GHSV 2400 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹, CO/H₂/Ar = 45/45/10), the bare Na‐FeCₓ* catalyst exhibited a low CO conversion of 1.9%. When physically mixed with s‐ZSM‐5 zeolite (granule size 20–40 mesh), CO conversion increased dramatically to 82.5%. The Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐S1‐OH catalyst achieved 66.5% CO conversion at H₂/CO = 1, which further increased to 82.6% at H₂/CO = 3.
- **Observations and Trends:** The integration of zeolite nanosheets as promoters significantly enhances CO conversion. Granule mixing and optimized H₂/CO ratios are critical for achieving high conversion rates.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift and oxidation reactions:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst maintained low CO₂ selectivity under LT-FTO conditions, with values not exceeding 10%. The Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐S1‐OH catalyst also exhibited low CO₂ selectivity, consistent with suppressed water-gas shift activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both zeolite-promoted catalysts minimize CO₂ formation, favoring hydrocarbon synthesis over oxidation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of methane among the hydrocarbon products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{\sum F_{\text{hydrocarbons}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5, CH₄ selectivity was below 3.8%. The Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐S1‐OH catalyst showed a slightly higher CH₄ selectivity of 6.0%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both catalysts suppress methane formation, with s‐ZSM‐5 being more effective, likely due to enhanced olefin desorption and minimized secondary hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of C₃ and higher hydrocarbons among total hydrocarbon products:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\sum F_{\text{C}_3 \text{ and higher}}}{\sum F_{\text{hydrocarbons}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst achieved C₂–C₁₀ olefin selectivity of 67.8–71.0%, with C₁₀⁺ selectivity below 6.3%. The product distribution was dominated by C₁–C₁₀ hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Zeolite nanosheet promotion narrows the product distribution, favoring light olefins and minimizing heavy hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination:
  \[
  \text{ASF:} \quad W_n = (1-\alpha)\alpha^{n-1}
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** The article describes α values being determined by fitting product distributions to the ASF model, with the zeolite-promoted catalysts exhibiting high α values consistent with selective C₂–C₁₀ olefin production.
- **Observations and Trends:** High α values correlate with increased selectivity toward longer-chain olefins, especially when sodium migration is minimized.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5, CO conversion remained stable (68.1% to 67.6%) over 600 hours on stream, indicating negligible deactivation. Granule mixing was essential for maintaining stability; powder mixing led to rapid deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Proper granule mixing and zeolite promotion ensure long-term catalyst durability with minimal loss in activity.

#### 6.2.7 Olefin Productivity and α-Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Olefin productivity is the amount of olefin produced per gram of catalyst per hour. α-Olefin selectivity is the fraction of α-olefins among C₄⁺ olefins.
- **Experimental Data:** Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 achieved an olefin productivity of 15.3 mmol·g⁻¹·h⁻¹. Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐S1‐OH reached 13.1 mmol·g⁻¹·h⁻¹ and an α-olefin selectivity of 81.7% (vs. 52.1% for s‐ZSM‐5).
- **Observations and Trends:** The absence of Brønsted acid sites in s‐S1‐OH suppresses olefin isomerization, maximizing α-olefin selectivity and enhancing product value.
