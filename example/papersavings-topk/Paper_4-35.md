## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe₃O₄@χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalysts is divided into a two‑stage process: the formation of Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals (in cubic or octahedral morphology) followed by a surface reconstruction to generate a conformal χ‑Fe₅C₂ shell.

#### Fe₃O₄ Nanocrystal Synthesis – Solution‑Phase Route
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Reagents: Iron(III) acetylacetonate (Fe(acac)₃) and 4‑biphenylcarboxylic acid dissolved in a high‑boiling solvent mixture of benzyl ether (BE) and oleic acid (OA).  
  – Conditions: Degas the solution at 120 °C for 30 min.  
2. • Heating Step  
  – Ramp: Increase temperature at 10 °C min⁻¹ until reaching 290 °C.  
  – Reaction: Maintain at 290 °C for 30 min under 300 rpm stirring, ensuring uniform nucleation and growth.  
3. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Process: Cool to room temperature, add ethanol to precipitate the nanocrystals, and wash three times with hexane before re‑dispersion in hexane.  

#### Surface Reconstruction to Form Fe₃O₄@χ‑Fe₅C₂
4. • Drying (Implicit in Transfer)  
  – The re‑dispersion in hexane is followed by drying under conditions that prepare the nanocrystals for further treatment.  
5. • Reduction  
  – Conditions: Treat the dried Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals under 1 bar H₂ at 270 °C for 10 h with a controlled heating ramp of 1 °C min⁻¹.  
  – Purpose: Remove surface organics and convert surface Fe₃O₄ to a metallic state.
6. • Carburization (Surface Reconstruction)  
  – Reactor Setup: Fixed‑bed reactor.  
  – Gas Composition: Expose the reduced catalyst to syngas composed of 64 vol% CO, 32 vol% H₂, and 4 vol% Ar.  
  – Conditions: 20 bar pressure, 270 °C, space velocity ~2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹, for 20 h.  
  – Outcome: Transformation of the metallic surface into a uniform χ‑Fe₅C₂ shell, with the exposed facets determined by the initial template shape.
7. • Post‑Treatment  
  – Handling: Switch to N₂ to protect the catalyst from air and transfer to an N₂‑filled glove box for storage.

This detailed, step‑by‑step synthesis protocol ensures both accuracy and reproducibility, with each parameter—reagent concentration, temperature, pressure, and duration—maintained as reported, facilitating a comparative analysis of synthesis complexity, scalability, and operational cost.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ Nanocubes

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 1.4 g Fe(acac)₃ (>99.9% purity), 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid, benzyl ether, and oleic acid.  
- **Conditions**: Degas the solution at 120 °C for 30 minutes under constant stirring (300 rpm); heat to 290 °C using a controlled ramp.  
- **Process**:  
  • Dissolve Fe(acac)₃ and 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid in benzyl ether and oleic acid to form a homogeneous solution.  
  • Degas the mixture at 120 °C for 30 minutes.  
  • Increase the temperature to 290 °C gradually while maintaining stirring to promote the formation of well-defined Fe₃O₄ nanocubes.  
- **Key Steps**: Focus on precise temperature control and uniform mixing to ensure reproducible nanoparticle shapes with exposed {202} facets after reconstruction.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly via Surface Reconstruction  
- **Reagents**: Preformed Fe₃O₄ nanocubes, H₂ (for reduction), and syngas comprising 32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar (for carburization).  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Reduction: 1 bar H₂ at 270 °C for 10 hours (heating ramp of 1 °C/min, gas flow rate 100 mL/min).  
  • Carburization: 20 bar syngas at 270 °C for 20 hours (space velocity ~2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹).  
- **Process**:  
  • Load the nanocubes into a fixed-bed reactor and reduce under H₂ to remove surface organics.  
  • Switch to a syngas atmosphere for the carburization step to form the χ-Fe₅C₂ shell.  
  • After reaction, protect the catalyst from air by switching to N₂ and transferring the reactor contents to a glove box.  
- **Key Steps**: Control gas composition and ramp conditions to ensure uniform shell formation.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  • Precipitate the catalyst using ethanol, followed by washing three times with hexane.  
  • Dry under inert conditions to avoid oxidation; while specific centrifugation speeds and drying temperatures are optimized for reproducibility, these steps are integral to obtaining the final catalyst structure.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Core–Shell Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy, which are essential for confirming the formation and stability of the core–shell architecture.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns identified Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) and χ-Fe₅C₂ (iron carbide) as the principal phases in Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes and octahedra. Quantitative analysis revealed that nanocubes contained approximately 63.4 wt% Fe₃O₄ and 33.2 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂, with minor Fe(II)/Fe(III) species (3.4 wt%). The coexistence of these phases confirms the successful formation of a well-defined core–shell structure. During carburization, XRD showed the progressive emergence of χ-Fe₅C₂ peaks and reduction of Fe₃O₄ peaks, indicating transformation at the surface.
- **Observations:** The presence of both Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂ is consistent with a core–shell morphology, which is critical for catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology, Facet Exposure, and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping were used to assess particle morphology, shell thickness, and facet exposure.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed nanocubes with an average size of 40.1 ± 3.8 nm and octahedra at 45.4 ± 3.5 nm. The shell thickness was uniform: ~2.0 nm for nanocubes and ~1.7 nm for octahedra. FFT analysis of HAADF-STEM images revealed that nanocubes predominantly expose the (202) facet of χ-Fe₅C₂, while octahedra expose the (112) facet. Lattice spacings of 0.21 nm (Fe₃O₄ (400)) and 0.22 nm (χ-Fe₅C₂ (202)) were observed, indicating good lattice matching.
- **Observations:** The uniform shell and facet exposure are directly linked to catalytic activity, with the {202} facet on nanocubes correlating with enhanced performance.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Phase Quantification
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy and STEM–EDX mapping provided quantitative phase composition and spatial distribution of elements.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra confirmed 63.4 wt% Fe₃O₄, 33.2 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂, and 3.4 wt% Fe(II)/Fe(III) in nanocubes. After 100 h on stream, χ-Fe₅C₂ content increased to 39.8%. EDX mapping showed Fe and O concentrated in the core, with Fe and C in the shell.
- **Observations:** The increase in χ-Fe₅C₂ after reaction suggests ongoing surface carburization, while the spatial distribution supports the core–shell model.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET) and BJH analyses were used to determine surface area and porosity, which influence reactant accessibility and catalyst stability.
- **Findings:** BET surface areas were 20.4 m²/g for nanocubes and 22.0 m²/g for octahedra. Both morphologies were initially nonporous. After 100 h on stream, octahedra developed mesopores (average diameter 16.0 nm), while nanocubes remained nonporous.
- **Observations:** The emergence of mesoporosity in octahedra is associated with catalyst deactivation, whereas the stable, nonporous surface of nanocubes correlates with sustained activity.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Reactivity and Carbon Deposition
- **Description:** In-situ DRIFTS and Raman spectroscopy were employed to probe CO adsorption/dissociation and carbon deposition, providing mechanistic insights.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS monitored the linearly adsorbed CO peak at 2058 cm⁻¹, enabling calculation of CO dissociation rates and activation energies for different facets. Raman spectra identified disordered (D band, 1330 cm⁻¹) and graphitic carbon (G band, 1592 cm⁻¹) after reaction, with heavier carbon deposition observed on octahedra.
- **Observations:** The {202} facet on nanocubes favors hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation with lower activation energy, while greater carbon buildup on octahedra contributes to deactivation.

#### 6.1.6 Metal Dispersion and Surface Fe Quantification
- **Description:** CO pulse chemisorption quantified surface Fe atoms, essential for calculating turnover frequency (TOF).
- **Findings:** Surface Fe atom concentrations were 24.1 µmol g⁻¹ for nanocubes and 19.5 µmol g⁻¹ for octahedra.
- **Observations:** Higher surface Fe dispersion in nanocubes supports their superior intrinsic catalytic activity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (20 bar, 270 °C, syngas: 64% H₂, 32% CO, 4% Ar, space velocity 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹), Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a CO conversion of 45.4%, while Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ octahedra/SiC reached 21.2%. When the space velocity for octahedra was reduced to 800 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹ to match conversion levels, performance differences in selectivity were further assessed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes, exposing the {202} facet of χ-Fe₅C₂, consistently outperformed octahedra ({112} facet) in CO conversion, highlighting the facet-dependent activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water-gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity was determined by online gas chromatography. The carbon balance, which includes CO₂, was similar for both nanocubes and octahedra, indicating comparable CO₂ selectivity under identical conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** No significant difference in CO₂ selectivity was observed between the two morphologies at matched conversion.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Methane selectivity was lower for Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC compared to octahedra/SiC and χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC (the latter showing 15.5 C% CH₄ selectivity). The nanocubes’ {202} facet suppressed CH₄ formation due to a higher surface CO/H₂ ratio.
- **Observations and Trends:** The {202} facet on nanocubes favors longer-chain hydrocarbon formation, reducing methane selectivity relative to the {112} facet on octahedra.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of long-chain hydrocarbon production.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC exhibited a C₅⁺ selectivity of 44.8 C%, while octahedra/SiC showed 27.6 C% under standard conditions and 39.6 C% at matched conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes consistently favored the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons, attributed to the exposed {202} facet.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) statistics, reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination:
  - α values range from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (infinite chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** The α values were 0.66 for nanocubes/SiC, 0.62 for octahedra/SiC, and 0.63 for χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC.
- **Observations and Trends:** While the facet effect strongly influences activity and selectivity, its impact on α is modest; similar α values were observed across morphologies at comparable conversion.

#### 6.2.6 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF quantifies the number of CO molecules converted per surface Fe atom per hour, calculated using CO pulse chemisorption to determine surface Fe:
  \[
  \text{TOF} = \frac{\text{CO conversion} \times \text{moles of CO in syngas} \times \text{gas flow rate}}{\text{moles of surface Fe atoms}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC exhibited a TOF of 645.9 h⁻¹, while octahedra/SiC showed 372.7 h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher TOF for nanocubes underscores the superior intrinsic activity of the {202} facet.

#### 6.2.7 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 100 h, nanocubes/SiC maintained CO conversion within 1% fluctuation, indicating high stability. Octahedra/SiC showed a decline from 20.1% to 11.7% CO conversion, reflecting significant deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes’ structural integrity and facet stability underpin their superior resistance to deactivation, while octahedra suffer from void formation and carbon deposition, accelerating deactivation.

#### 6.2.8 Olefin-to-Paraffin Ratio (C₂–C₄)
- **Description:** The olefin-to-paraffin (o/p) ratio among C₂–C₄ products is calculated as the ratio of total C₂–C₄ olefins to paraffins, indicating selectivity for valuable intermediates.
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC displayed a higher o/p ratio among C₂–C₄ products compared to octahedra/SiC.
- **Observations and Trends:** The {202} facet promotes olefin formation, enhancing the catalyst’s value for industrial applications.

---
**Summary:**  
Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC, with exposed {202} facets, demonstrate superior CO conversion, higher selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons, greater intrinsic activity (TOF), and enhanced stability compared to octahedra/SiC. These results underscore the critical role of facet engineering in optimizing Fischer–Tropsch catalyst performance.
