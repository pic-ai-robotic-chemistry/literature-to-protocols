## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis for 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 involves a two‐stage approach: an initial co‑precipitation to form the ZnFe2O4 spinel precursor and a subsequent incipient wetness impregnation for Na2S incorporation. Both methods are reported with clear, reproducible experimental steps.

#### Co‑precipitation Method

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O dissolved in deionized water.  
  – Equipment: Magnetic stirrer set at 700 rpm.  
  – Duration: Stir for 30 minutes.

2. • pH Adjustment and Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Slowly add ammonia to adjust the solution to pH 9.  
  – Continue stirring for 1 hour, ensuring homogeneous mixing and precipitate formation.  
  – Allow the suspension to age under static conditions for 2 hours to facilitate precipitation–deposition before proceeding.

3. • Drying  
  – Filter and wash the resulting precipitate with deionized water.  
  – Dry at 100 °C for 12 hours to remove residual solvents.

4. • Calcination  
  – Calcine in a muffle furnace at 400 °C for 4 hours, transforming the dried powder into the ZnFe2O4 spinel phase.

5. • Post‑synthesis Reduction  
  – Reduce the calcined precursor in a flowing H₂ atmosphere at 450 °C for 5 hours, converting ZnFe2O4 into the active Fe⁰@ZnO structure.  
  – Observations: Formation of larger Fe⁰ particles (260–290 nm) and smaller ZnO particles (60–70 nm).

#### Incipient Wetness Impregnation for Na2S Modification

1. • Impregnation  
  – Add Na2S (0.1–0.2 wt%) into the dried FeZn powder via incipient wetness impregnation, ensuring uniform distribution.

2. • Follow‑up Post‑treatment  
  – Repeat drying and, if necessary, a mild calcination step to finalize the incorporation of sulfur, thereby modulating the catalyst’s hydrogenation ability and carbon deposition.

This structured methodology, integrating precise steps, reagent concentrations, and operating conditions, ensures reproducibility and accuracy in synthesizing catalysts for CO₂/CO-Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 0.18 mol total of Fe(NO₃)₃⋅9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂⋅6H₂O dissolved in deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Stir at 700 rpm for 30 minutes; maintain temperature at ambient.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the iron and zinc nitrates in deionized water to ensure a homogeneous solution.
  - Slowly add ammonia dropwise to adjust the pH to 9, promoting uniform co‐precipitation.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure consistent stirring and controlled ammonia addition to achieve ideal precipitation and homogeneity.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: Following precursor formation, a Na₂CO₃ solution is added via incipient wetness impregnation to incorporate sodium.
- **Conditions**: Stirring is maintained to allow even distribution; use a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave when necessary.
- **Process**:
  - Introduce Na by mixing the Na₂CO₃ solution thoroughly with the freshly precipitated FeZn powders.
  - Allow the mixture to age under static conditions to complete precursor assembly.
- **Key Steps**: Emphasize uniform mixing for reproducible catalyst characteristics.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Filter the suspension and wash multiple times with deionized water to remove residual ions.
  - Utilize centrifugation to separate the catalyst solids efficiently.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough washing to eliminate impurities that may affect later steps.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the washed catalyst at 100 °C for 12 hours to remove moisture.
- **Calcination**: Calcine in a muffle furnace at 400 °C for 4 hours in air to form the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel phase.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain accurate temperature control and time for phase formation.

##### Step 5: Activation
- **Process**: Reduce the calcined catalyst under a flowing H₂ atmosphere at 450 °C for 5 hours to form the active Fe⁰@ZnO structure.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor gas flow (e.g., 30 mL/min) and temperature ramp settings to ensure reproducibility.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation process for the Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is carried out as a dedicated pre‐reaction treatment to convert the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel precursor into the active Fe⁰@ZnO phase. This step is distinct from any activation procedures used in subsequent catalyst characterization.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for reducing the precursor oxides to generate the active metallic phases.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is pre‐reduced at 450 °C.  
- **Observations:** Reduction at this temperature transforms the bulk ZnFe₂O₄ into larger Fe⁰ nanoparticles, with ZnO present to inhibit excessive sintering and promote metal dispersion.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The applied pressure during reduction influences the kinetics of the hydrogen-driven reduction process.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction is conducted at 0.2 MPa.  
- **Observations:** Operating at this pressure facilitates the efficient removal of oxygen from the catalyst precursor, thereby enabling a uniform reduction process without promoting catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The choice of activation gas is vital for effectively reducing the catalyst precursor and establishing the active phase.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Hydrogen gas is employed as the reducing agent during the activation process.  
- **Observations:** Hydrogen effectively strips oxygen from the iron oxide phases, ensuring that the precursor is transformed into the active metallic and carbide forms necessary for optimal Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The activation time ensures complete reduction and phase transformation of the catalyst precursor.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under hydrogen flow for 5 hours.  
- **Observations:** This duration allows for the full evolution of the active Fe⁰@ZnO structure, ensuring improved catalyst dispersion and stability while minimizing risks of sintering.

Following the reduction step, an in situ passivation is performed by cooling in the feed gas and treating with a 1% O₂/99% N₂ mixture to preserve the active phase during handling. This integrated activation protocol is essential for achieving the desired catalytic performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is the thermal condition at which the Fischer–Tropsch process is conducted, affecting both syngas activation and the formation of active iron carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** According to the article, the reaction temperature is maintained at 320 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 320 °C has been shown to facilitate the activation and dissociation of the syngas components, leading to enhanced CO conversion. Additionally, the 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 catalyst exhibits excellent catalytic stability at this temperature with optimal lower olefins production, whereas minor increases in temperature (e.g., to 340 °C) may increase methane and C₅+ formation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure is the overall system pressure during the process, which influences the concentration of reactants on the catalyst surface and the hydrocarbon product distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch evaluation was carried out at a reaction pressure of 1 MPa.
- **Observations:** The chosen pressure condition enhances CO adsorption on the catalyst, promoting improved conversion and steering the selectivity towards desired products. The article notes that higher pressures tend to favor the formation of heavier hydrocarbons and slightly impact the catalyst's hydrogenation behavior.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio defines the proportion of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed gas, crucial for managing the balance between hydrogenation and carburization during synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reported H₂/CO ratio used in these tests is 1.
- **Observations:** This stoichiometric ratio ensures a balanced supply of both reactants. The article highlights that deviations from this baseline, such as an increase in the H₂/CO ratio, would lead to higher methane selectivity due to enhanced hydrogenation of surface intermediates.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Gas Hourly Space Velocity (GHSV)
- **Definition:** GHSV quantifies the volume flow rate of the reactant gas per unit mass of the catalyst, influencing the residence time and overall conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The process is operated with a GHSV of 1200 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹.
- **Observations:** This GHSV value is optimal for balancing CO conversion efficiency and catalyst stability; lower values permit adequate contact time, thereby enhancing lower olefins production while maintaining a stable reaction profile over extended operating periods.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The evolution of crystal phases was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) to monitor transformations from precursor to active and spent catalyst states, providing insight into phase composition and stability under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of fresh catalysts revealed the presence of ZnFe₂O₄ spinel as the dominant phase, with a weak ZnO peak. Following H₂ reduction (450 °C, 5 h), ZnFe₂O₄ was fully converted to metallic Fe⁰ (PDF#87–721) and ZnO (PDF#79–208), forming a Fe⁰@ZnO structure. In spent catalysts after CO₂-FTS, Fe⁰ was further transformed into Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂, while ZnO remained. For S-modified catalysts (e.g., 1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1), XRD indicated the presence of Fe⁰, Fe₃C, and Fe₅C₂, but no Fe₃O₄.
- **Observations:** The formation of Fe⁰@ZnO is essential for catalytic activity. S addition suppresses Fe carburization and oxidation, stabilizing Fe⁰ and Fe₃C phases and inhibiting Fe₃O₄ formation, which correlates with altered catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Reduction Behavior (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** H₂-temperature programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was employed to assess the reducibility of catalyst precursors and the influence of Na₂S on reduction profiles.
- **Findings:** Three main reduction peaks were observed: ZnFe₂O₄ to Fe₃O₄ at ~335 °C, Fe₃O₄ to FeO at ~425 °C, and FeO to Fe at ~610 °C. Increasing Na₂S content shifted these peaks to lower temperatures.
- **Observations:** The shift to lower reduction temperatures with Na₂S addition indicates enhanced reducibility, attributed to the electron-withdrawing effect of Na₂S, which counteracts the electron-donating influence of Na.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were determined by N₂ adsorption-desorption using the BET method, providing surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution.
- **Findings:** BET analysis (Micromeritics ASAP 2020, 77 K) yielded the following values:
  - 1.5Na-Fe: 37 m²/g (surface area), 0.74 cm³/g (pore volume), 5.90 nm (average pore diameter)
  - 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1: 47 m²/g, 0.49 cm³/g, 3.10 nm
  - 1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1: 44 m²/g, 0.56 cm³/g, 3.80 nm
  - 1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1: 45 m²/g, 0.51 cm³/g, 3.30 nm
- **Observations:** The incorporation of Zn and S slightly increases surface area and maintains mesoporosity, which is beneficial for reactant diffusion and catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphological features and particle sizes were examined by SEM, TEM, and HADDF-STEM, with EDX mapping for elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** SEM images showed block-like ZnFe₂O₄ particles with ZnO protrusions. After reduction, TEM revealed large Fe⁰ nanoparticles (260–290 nm) decorated with smaller ZnO (60–70 nm), forming Fe⁰@ZnO structures. EDX mapping of spent 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 showed Fe-rich spheres (~210 nm) enveloped by a carbon shell (up to 28 nm thick), while S-modified catalysts exhibited more dispersed Fe and uniform C distribution without distinct carbon shells.
- **Observations:** ZnO inhibits Fe aggregation, enhancing dispersion. S addition reduces carbon shell formation, mitigating deactivation.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Deposition and Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Carbon deposition was quantified by thermogravimetric analysis (TG) and characterized by Raman spectroscopy; hydrophobicity was assessed via contact angle measurements.
- **Findings:** TG showed significant weight loss above 250 °C for 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1, indicating heavy carbon deposition, which was reduced in S-containing catalysts. Raman spectra displayed strong D (1313 cm⁻¹) and G (1600 cm⁻¹) bands for 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1, with decreased intensity upon S addition. Hydrophobic SiO₂ supports exhibited contact angles of 125.5°, decreasing after reaction, confirming hydrophobicity and its partial loss under high-pressure conditions.
- **Observations:** S addition effectively suppresses carbon deposition, and hydrophobic modification aids in water management, both contributing to improved catalyst longevity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion

- **Description:**  
  CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO molecules transformed by the catalyst relative to the total CO supplied. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  In this study, conversions are determined using GC peak areas with N₂ as an internal standard.

- **Experimental Data:**  
  For the Na₂S-modified catalyst (1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1) under CO-FTS conditions (320 °C, 1 MPa, H₂/CO = 1, GHSV = 1200 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹), CO conversion values are:
  - 88.0% at 0.5 MPa
  - 96.9% at 1.0 MPa
  - 97.5% at 1.5 MPa

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The introduction of Na₂S significantly enhances CO conversion, with values approaching 97% at elevated pressures. The catalyst maintains high conversion over extended time-on-stream, indicating robust activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO Selectivity

- **Description:**  
  CO selectivity (S_CO) reflects the proportion of converted CO₂ that is reduced to CO, calculated as:
  \[
  S_{\text{CO}} = \frac{f_{\text{CO}} \cdot (A_{\text{CO}})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{f_{\text{CO}_2} \cdot [(A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}]} \times 100
  \]

- **Experimental Data:**  
  Under CO₂-FTS, S_CO increases with Na₂S content:
  - 15.6% for 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1
  - 42.6% for 1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1
  - 71.3% for 1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Higher Na₂S loading markedly increases CO selectivity, indicating a shift toward the reverse water-gas shift pathway and suppressed hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity

- **Description:**  
  CH₄ selectivity (S_CH₄) is the fraction of converted carbon appearing as methane:
  \[
  S_{\text{CH}_4} = \frac{f_{\text{CH}_4} \cdot A_{\text{CH}_4}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{f_{\text{CO}_2} \cdot [(A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}]} \times 100
  \]

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - CO₂-FTS: 12.7% for 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1; 11.7% for hydrophobically modified catalyst.
  - CO-FTS: 35.6% for 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1; reduced to 5.8% (1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1) and 5.6% (1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1) with Na₂S.

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Na₂S addition dramatically lowers CH₄ selectivity in CO-FTS, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity

- **Description:**  
  C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  S_{\text{C}_5^+} = 100 - S_{\text{CH}_4} - S_{\text{C}_2^0-\text{C}_4^0} - S_{\text{C}_2^- - \text{C}_4^-}
  \]

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - CO-FTS: 49.6% for 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1; increased to 67.8% (1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1) and 63.8% (1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1) with Na₂S.

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Na₂S modification shifts product distribution toward long-chain hydrocarbons, enhancing C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)

- **Description:**  
  The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, indicates the probability of chain growth versus termination (0 < α < 1). Higher α favors long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

- **Experimental Data:**  
  While explicit α values are not tabulated, the data show that Na₂S increases α in CO-FTS, as evidenced by higher C₅⁺ selectivity and suppressed methane formation.

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Na₂S promotes chain growth, resulting in a product slate rich in long-chain α-olefins.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation

- **Description:**  
  Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring conversion and selectivity over time. Deactivation is linked to carbon deposition and water accumulation.

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1: Gradual deactivation observed (CO₂ conversion drops from 43.3% to 38.3% after 20 h).
  - 1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1: Maintains high CO conversion (>96%) and selectivity over extended periods (up to 1000 h in CO-FTS).

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Na₂S modification and hydrophobic supports significantly enhance catalyst longevity by suppressing carbon deposition and facilitating water removal.
