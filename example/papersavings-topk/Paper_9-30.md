## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis procedures were designed to ensure reproducibility and high performance via controlled reaction parameters and precise equipment usage. The following detailed experimental procedures summarize the three synthesis approaches reported:

#### • Fe@Si via Solvothermal and Stöber Methods  
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
 - Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ mixed with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) in an aqueous solution.  
 - Equipment: Teflon‑lined stainless steel autoclave.  
 - Conditions: Reaction set at 180 °C for 18 h for nanoparticle stabilization, with PVP preventing aggregation.  
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
 - Process: Tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) is added dropwise into a solution containing deionized water and ethanol under continuous stirring.  
 - Conditions: Room temperature stirring for 48 h to yield a uniform SiO₂ shell.  
3. Drying and Calcination  
 - Drying: 120 °C for 24 h.  
 - Calcination: 550 °C for 6 h in air to remove organic constituents and form stable iron oxide phases.  
4. Post‑treatment:  
 - Reduction is carried out at 350 °C for 24 h in syngas (H₂/CO = 2) to enhance active phase formation.

#### • Fe/Si via Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
1. Precursor Mixing  
 - Reagents: An ethanol solution of 2 M Fe(NO₃)₃ is used.  
 - Process: The mesoporous SiO₂ support is impregnated for 5 h to ensure even distribution of the iron precursor.  
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
 - Involves an autoclave treatment in a Teflon‑lined vessel at 180 °C for 18 h to facilitate strong interaction between the precursor and support.  
3. Drying and Calcination  
 - Drying: Conducted at 120 °C.  
 - Calcination: 550 °C for 6 h in air.

#### • Fe‑Si via Co‑precipitation  
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
 - Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol are simultaneously mixed.  
 - pH Control: NH₃⋅H₂O is added to maintain a pH of 9.0 ± 0.1 at 85 °C, ensuring homogeneous precipitation and observable solid formation.  
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
 - A brief deposition period follows immediately after mixing.  
3. Drying and Calcination  
 - Drying at 120 °C, followed by calcination at 550 °C for 6 h, solidifying the Fe–SiO₂ interaction.

This structured description, including equipment details and specific numerical conditions, allows for clear replication and comparative evaluation of the catalyst synthesis strategies.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe@Si Catalyst

##### 3.1.2.2 Fe/Si Catalyst  
- **Deionized water**: Transferred 100mL using a graduated cylinder to dissolve the iron precursor and promoter before impregnation.

##### 3.1.2.3 Fe‑Si Catalyst

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe@Si Catalyst

- **Fe precursor solution**: Stirred at 600rpm for 20 minutes in a conventional magnetic stirrer setup to secure uniform distribution on the m‑SiO₂ support.

##### 3.1.3.3 Fe‑Si Catalyst  
- **Fe–silica sol mixture**: Mixed at 600rpm for 20 minutes under an N₂ atmosphere at 85 °C to stabilize the pH and promote simultaneous precipitation, thus ensuring reproducible formation of the co‐precipitated material.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Si

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, appropriate amount of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and anhydrous ethanol.  
- **Conditions**: Dissolution and stabilization via a solvothermal treatment at 180 °C for 18 hours under continuous stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in anhydrous ethanol.  
  - Add PVP to prevent aggregation of forming Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.  
  - Maintain the reaction at 180 °C for 18 hours to ensure complete conversion to the iron precursor.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure homogeneous dissolution and controlled nanoparticle growth.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly via the Stöber Method  
- **Reagents**: 25 mL tetraethoxysilane (TEOS), 6 g cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), plus 1656 mL ethanol, 1276 mL deionized water, and 144 mL NH₃·H₂O (25%).  
- **Conditions**: Room temperature stirring for 48 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Hydrolyze TEOS in the presence of CTAB to form a mesoporous SiO₂ shell around the Fe₂O₃ core.  
  - Stir continuously to promote uniform shell formation.  
- **Key Steps**: Controlled addition of TEOS and CTAB is crucial for creating uniform porosity.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  - Filter and wash the composite with distilled water and anhydrous ethanol to remove residual reagents.  
  - Dry the sample at 120 °C for 24 hours.  
  - Calcine in air at 550 °C for 6 hours to remove organics and stabilize the catalyst structure.

- **Operational Description**: Measures textural properties such as BET surface area, pore volume, and pore size.
- **Instruments and Models**: ASAP 2420.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples degassed under vacuum at 90 °C for 120 minutes and at 350 °C for 480 minutes; measurement conducted at −196 °C.

- **Key Notes**: Reported values include Fe@Si (652 m²/g, 2.62 nm pore size), Fe/Si (642 m²/g, 2.55 nm), and Fe‑Si (295 m²/g, 6.28 nm).

- **Operational Description**: Analyzes functional groups and Fe–SiO₂ interactions.
- **Instruments and Models**: VERTEX 70 spectrophotometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples mixed with KBr (1/50 ratio), pressed into disks, spectra recorded from 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹ at 4 cm⁻¹ resolution.
- **Test Parameters**: Standardized disk preparation for reproducible spectral quality.
- **Key Notes**: Useful for identifying surface species and catalyst interactions.

### 4.4 X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: Determines crystalline phases present, such as hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) and iron carbides.

- **Test Parameters**: Data fitted using nonlinear least-squares routines.
- **Key Notes**: Provides detailed oxidation state and phase information.

### 4.6 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) with EDX Mapping
- **Operational Description**: Visualizes catalyst morphology and confirms elemental distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Talos F200X operating at 200 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples dispersed in ethanol for 20 minutes, deposited onto a carbon membrane.
- **Test Parameters**: Standard imaging and EDX mapping parameters to assess dispersion and particle size.
- **Key Notes**: Critical for evaluating anti-sintering properties.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for initiating the reduction of iron oxides into the desired active phases (e.g., iron carbides) without triggering excessive sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** All catalysts, including the Fe@Si variant, are activated at 350 °C in a fixed-bed reactor. This pre‐reaction thermal treatment is carried out prior to Fischer–Tropsch testing.
- **Observations:** Operating at 350 °C ensures a controlled reduction rate. For the Fe@Si catalyst, however, the protective silica shell leads to diffusion limitations for H₂O removal, resulting in a somewhat lower reduction degree, while still preserving particle dispersion.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure determines the gas–catalyst interaction dynamics, influencing the reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst pre‐treatment is conducted under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Atmospheric conditions promote moderate reduction kinetics that are sufficiently robust to generate active phases without causing excessive catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition used during activation drives both the reduction of the iron oxide species and the carburization necessary for forming active iron carbides.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with a H₂/CO ratio of 2 is employed.
- **Observations:** In this environment, hydrogen effectively reduces the iron oxides, while carbon monoxide contributes to carburization. This balance is especially important for maintaining performance consistency across different catalyst architectures.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) regulates how efficiently the activation gas contacts the catalyst particles.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹ is applied during the activation step.
- **Observations:** This flow rate minimizes the risk of over-reduction and ensures uniform exposure of all catalyst sites.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is essential to achieving complete precursor transformation and optimal active phase formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Catalyst pre‐treatment is maintained for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** The 24-hour period is sufficient to initiate the reduction process and prepare the catalyst for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction conditions, with performance variations attributed primarily to structural differences among the catalysts.

### 5.2 Reaction

This section delineates the reaction conditions utilized in the Fischer-Tropsch catalytic tests, ensuring that the conditions can be directly replicated and interpreted for further investigations.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter influencing the activation of syngas and overall catalyst performance. In the study, the Fischer-Tropsch tests were carried out at a constant temperature of 280 °C. Under this condition, the Fe@Si catalyst exhibited the highest CH₄ selectivity due to the confinement effect of the protective SiO₂ shell, while the Fe‑Si catalyst demonstrated enhanced C₅⁺ selectivity. These observations confirm that the chosen temperature effectively balances syngas conversion efficiency with the desired hydrocarbon distribution.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
The total system pressure plays a significant role in facilitating syngas adsorption and hydrogenation reactions. The experiments were conducted at 2.0 MPa. This pressure was selected to stabilize the reaction environment, optimize CO conversion, and promote favorable hydrocarbon selectivity. The use of 2.0 MPa has been instrumental in enhancing the reaction kinetics while ensuring a steady product distribution during the fixed-bed reactor operation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The stoichiometric balance of the input gases is paramount in dictating the pathway of the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. A H₂/CO molar ratio of 2 was employed across all tests. This ratio was found to be optimal, as it facilitated effective hydrogenation of CO, which in turn led to a balanced product slate. Specifically, while a higher ratio typically shifts selectivity towards methane, the selected value maintained a desirable equilibrium, favoring both light and heavy hydrocarbon production depending on the catalyst's inherent properties.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is defined as the ratio of syngas flow rate to the mass of catalyst used, and it determines the contact time between the reactants and the catalyst. In these experiments, a GHSV of 5000 mL∙h⁻¹∙g_cat⁻¹ was maintained. This condition ensured that the reactants were efficiently converted, and the performance trends over time could be reliably compared for the different catalyst configurations evaluated.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural features of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a D8 ADVANCE diffractometer. XRD analysis is essential for identifying the presence and crystallinity of iron oxide phases, which directly influence catalytic behavior in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** For the Fe@Si catalyst, XRD patterns revealed well-crystallized hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) as the only detectable crystalline phase, with characteristic peaks at 2θ = 38.7°, 41.7°, 63.9°, and 74.1°. Broad peaks at 2θ = 42° and 75° were attributed to amorphous iron oxides with small crystallite sizes. The Fe/Si catalyst exhibited similar hematite features, while Fe–Si showed broader peaks, indicating smaller or more amorphous iron oxide crystallites due to strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction.
- **Observations:** The presence of amorphous phases and small crystallite sizes in Fe@Si and Fe–Si suggests higher surface area and more active sites, which are beneficial for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties, including BET surface area and pore size distribution, were measured by N₂ physisorption using an ASAP 2420 analyzer. These parameters are critical for reactant diffusion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si exhibited a BET surface area of 652 m²/g and an average pore size of 2.62 nm. Fe/Si showed similar values (642 m²/g, 2.55 nm), while Fe–Si had a lower surface area (295 m²/g) and a larger average pore size (6.28 nm).
- **Observations:** The high surface areas and uniform mesopores in Fe@Si and Fe/Si are attributed to the use of CTAB as a structure-directing agent, whereas the co-precipitation method for Fe–Si led to larger, less uniform pores and reduced surface area.

#### 6.1.3 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was employed to assess the reducibility of iron species, with total H₂ consumption reflecting the extent of reduction.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts exhibited high total H₂ consumption (0.95 and 1.00 mol H₂/mol Fe, respectively), while Fe–Si showed significantly lower consumption (0.42 mol H₂/mol Fe).
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si slightly hindered reduction due to diffusion limitations, but both Fe@Si and Fe/Si achieved greater reduction than Fe–Si, where strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction stabilized iron oxides and suppressed reduction.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Talos F200X) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping were used to examine particle morphology and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si consisted of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles (average size ~50.4 nm) embedded within a porous SiO₂ shell, maintaining their size (~45.1 nm) after reaction. Fe/Si showed significant agglomeration, while Fe–Si displayed a more uniform distribution of iron species, with some aggregation observed post-reaction.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively prevented sintering, preserving nanoparticle size and dispersion, whereas the lack of a protective barrier in Fe/Si led to particle growth and aggregation.

#### 6.1.5 Phase Composition and Carburization
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy (MR-351) quantified the relative amounts of iron carbides, Fe₃O₄, and other iron species after activation and reaction, providing insight into active phase formation.
- **Findings:** After activation, Fe/Si had the highest iron carbide content (55.75%), followed by Fe@Si (52.01%) and Fe–Si (29.67%). The lower carbide content in Fe–Si was linked to strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction suppressing reduction and carburization.
- **Observations:** Higher iron carbide content correlated with higher initial catalytic activity, while oxidation of carbides during reaction contributed to deactivation in Fe@Si and Fe/Si. Fe–Si exhibited greater stability but lower activity due to limited carbide formation.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under identical reaction conditions (280 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹), the CO conversion values after 64 hours on stream are:
  - Fe@Si: 47.9%
  - Fe/Si: 55.7%
  - Fe–Si: 29.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe/Si and Fe@Si catalysts exhibit higher initial CO conversion (75.0% and 66.6%, respectively), but both show gradual deactivation over time. In contrast, Fe–Si starts with low activity (10.1%) but increases steadily, attributed to ongoing carburization and formation of active iron carbides.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Reported CO₂ selectivity values are:
  - Fe@Si: 24.3%
  - Fe/Si: 27.1%
  - Fe–Si: 46.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe–Si exhibits significantly higher CO₂ selectivity, indicating a stronger water–gas shift activity, likely due to its strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction and lower reduction degree.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The selectivity to CH₄ is:
  - Fe@Si: 24.4%
  - Fe/Si: 19.5%
  - Fe–Si: 17.9%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@Si shows the highest CH₄ selectivity, attributed to the confinement effect of the SiO₂ shell, which promotes secondary hydrogenation and hinders desorption of heavier hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The C₅⁺ selectivity values are:
  - Fe@Si: 30.2%
  - Fe/Si: 31.0%
  - Fe–Si: 25.1%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe/Si achieves the highest C₅⁺ selectivity, while Fe–Si, despite strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction, shows the lowest, likely due to increased CO₂ formation and less effective chain growth.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution. Higher α values favor longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not numerically provided, the article notes that Fe/Si exhibits higher α, correlating with its superior C₅⁺ selectivity, whereas Fe@Si has a lower α, consistent with higher CH₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The order of α factor aligns with Fe–SiO₂ interaction strength: Fe/Si > Fe@Si > Fe–Si, mirroring trends in C₅⁺ and CH₄ selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring the decline in CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si both show a gradual decrease in CO conversion, indicating deactivation primarily due to oxidation of iron carbides by H₂O. Fe–Si, conversely, displays increasing activity, attributed to progressive carburization.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively inhibits sintering, as confirmed by TEM, but also impedes H₂O removal, contributing to deactivation. Fe/Si suffers from aggregation, while Fe–Si benefits from strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction, enhancing long-term activity.
