## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details two synthesis protocols used to prepare Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, focusing on reproducibility and technical precision.

#### MIL‑101(Fe) Derived Fe@C Catalysts (Hydrothermal–Pyrolysis Route)
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Reagents: 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 ml nitric acid, and 70 ml DMF.  
   • Equipment: Teflon‑lined steel autoclave.  
   • Procedure: The reagents are mixed and stirred for 2 h to form a homogeneous solution before transferring into the autoclave.

2. **Hydrothermal Synthesis**  
   • Conditions: 120 °C for 48 h.  
   • Post‑processing: The synthesized MIL‑101(Fe) is washed repeatedly using DMF and ethanol, then dried at 80 °C overnight.

3. **Pyrolysis**  
   • Equipment: Tube furnace configured with a controlled gas flow of 2% C₂H₂/He.  
   • Conditions: Pyrolysis at 500 °C, 600 °C, or 700 °C for 1 h with a heating ramp of 5 °C/min.  
   • Observations: The temperature modulates both the iron phase (h‑Fe₃C) and the nature of the carbon shell (amorphous at 500 °C, graphene at 600 °C, graphite at 700 °C).

4. **Additional Post‑treatment**  
   • For Fe@C‑600‑H₂: A hydrogen reduction step at 400 °C with a 50 ml/min flow for 24 h further transforms Fe@C‑600.

*Comparative Note:* The hydrothermal method enables a high‑surface area MIL‑101(Fe) framework, although it is more complex and time‑consuming compared to the co‑precipitation technique.

#### Unsupported‑Fe Catalyst (Continuous Co‑precipitation Route)
1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   • Reagents: Aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ and ammonia solution.  
   • Procedure: Fe(NO₃)₃ is mixed with ammonia dropwise under stirring at 80 ± 1 °C to maintain a pH of 8.2–8.5, ensuring effective precipitation.
  
2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • Method: Dropwise addition supports uniform precipitation and deposition on forming particles.
  
3. **Drying and Calcination**  
   • Drying: Conducted at 120 °C for 12 h.
   • Calcination: Carried out in static air at 500 °C for 5 h.
  
4. **Final Reduction**  
   • Conditions: Reduction in hydrogen at 350 °C for 24 h.

*Advantages vs. Disadvantages:*  
– Hydrothermal synthesis provides well‐defined structures and higher surface area, yet requires longer processing time.  
– In contrast, the co‑precipitation method is simpler and easier to control for pH but may offer lower structural definition.

This structured approach, complete with specific reagents, equipment, temperature profiles, and post‑treatment procedures, ensures that another researcher could replicate the methodology with high accuracy.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@C Catalysts (Fe@C-500, Fe@C-600, Fe@C-700, and Fe@C-600-H₂)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation (Synthesis of MIL-101(Fe))
- **Reagents**: 166 mg of terephthalic acid, 606 mg of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 mL nitric acid, and 70 mL N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF).
- **Conditions**: Mix these reagents in a Teflon-lined autoclave and conduct hydrothermal synthesis at 120 °C for 48 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve terephthalic acid and Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O in DMF with nitric acid.
  - Stir for 2 hours at room temperature to ensure a homogeneous solution.
  - Transfer the solution into the autoclave, synthesize MIL-101(Fe) at 120 °C for 48 hours.
  - Wash the product three times with DMF and ethanol before drying at 80 °C overnight.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure uniform mixing and controlled hydrothermal conditions for reproducibility.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Pyrolysis
- **Reagents**: The as-prepared MIL-101(Fe) without any additional reagents.
- **Conditions**: Pyrolysis is performed in a tube furnace under a 2% C₂H₂/He flow with a heating rate of 5 °C/min.
- **Process**:
  - Subject MIL-101(Fe) to pyrolysis at final temperatures of 500, 600, and 700 °C for 1 hour respectively.
  - Note that at 500 °C an amorphous carbon shell forms, while at 600 °C a 3–5 layer graphene shell results; at 700 °C, a thicker graphite shell is developed.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain controlled heating and gas flow to provide precise carbon shell structures.

##### Step 3: Activation
- **For Fe@C-600-H₂**:
  - **Conditions**: Reduce Fe@C-600 under hydrogen at 50 mL/min, 400 °C for 24 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor the reduction process carefully to achieve the desired transformation of h‑Fe₃C.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Unsupported-Fe Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Formation via Coprecipitation
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃ solution and ammonia solution.
- **Conditions**: Add reagents dropwise at 80 ± 1 °C, adjusting pH to 8.2–8.5.
- **Process**:
  - Mix under stirring; after aging for 2 hours, wash and filter the precipitate.
  - Dry at 120 °C for 12 hours.
  
##### Step 2: Calcination and Reduction
- **Conditions**: Calcine at 500 °C for 5 hours in static air, then reduce at 350 °C in hydrogen for 24 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete conversion and avoid aggregation through controlled temperature programs.

This detailed workflow ensures that all primary precursors are synthesized prior to intermediate steps, enabling clear reproducibility of the catalyst synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the Fe@C catalysts were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES), providing insight into the formation and distribution of active iron phases relevant to Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS).
- **Findings:** XRD patterns reveal that Fe@C-500 contains FeO and weak, undetermined lines, indicating incomplete reduction and carburization. Fe@C-600 displays distinct diffraction lines for h-Fe₃C (hexagonal cementite), α-Fe, and graphite carbon, while Fe@C-700 exhibits sharper, narrower peaks for h-Fe₃C and graphite carbon, reflecting increased crystallinity and graphitization at higher pyrolysis temperatures. MES quantifies the iron phases: h-Fe₃C content increases with pyrolysis temperature—68.2% in Fe@C-500, 87.2% in Fe@C-600, and 95.5% in Fe@C-700. α-Fe is present in Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂.
- **Observations:** The dominance of h-Fe₃C, especially at higher pyrolysis temperatures, is directly correlated with enhanced FTS activity and selectivity. The presence of α-Fe encapsulated within h-Fe₃C may further stabilize the active phase during reaction.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were employed to examine nanoparticle morphology, particle size distribution, and the nature of carbon shells encapsulating the iron phases.
- **Findings:** Fe@C-600 features uniformly distributed iron nanoparticles with an average size of 5.9 nm, encapsulated by concentric graphene shells (3–5 layers, interplanar spacing ~0.346 nm). Fe@C-500 exhibits amorphous carbon shells, while Fe@C-700 displays thick, well-oriented graphite shells with larger particle sizes (~40 nm). After long-term FTS, Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ maintain nanoparticle dispersion and shell integrity, whereas Fe@C-500 and unsupported-Fe show significant aggregation.
- **Observations:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 effectively inhibits nanoparticle sintering and oxidation, contributing to superior catalyst stability and performance.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET analysis) was conducted to determine surface area, pore volume, and average pore size, parameters critical for reactant accessibility and catalytic efficiency.
- **Findings:** Fe@C-600 exhibits a BET surface area of 280 m²/g, a pore volume of 0.27 cm³/g, and an average pore size of 3.8 nm. These values indicate a mesoporous structure with high surface area, favorable for FTS.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and mesoporosity of Fe@C-600 facilitate efficient reactant diffusion and exposure of active sites, supporting its high catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.4 Carbon Shell Structure
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy was utilized to characterize the degree of graphitization and structural features of the carbon shells surrounding the iron nanoparticles.
- **Findings:** Fe@C-500 shows a weak, single 2D peak at 2700 cm⁻¹, indicative of amorphous carbon. Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ display broad 2D bands (2500–3200 cm⁻¹) fitted to four peaks, characteristic of 3–5 layer graphene shells. Fe@C-700 presents two 2D peaks, consistent with a graphite shell. The ID/IG ratios are 0.79 for Fe@C-600 and 0.91 for Fe@C-700, reflecting increasing graphitization with temperature.
- **Observations:** The formation of a well-defined graphene shell at 600 °C is linked to enhanced catalyst stability and resistance to aggregation, while excessive graphitization at 700 °C leads to thicker shells and reduced catalytic performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS), calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (300 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.0, WGSV 8000 ml/g/h), Fe@C-600 exhibited an initial CO conversion of 65.0% at 24 h, increasing to 74.5% at 184 h. Fe@C-600-H₂ achieved a stable CO conversion of 78.7%. Unsupported-Fe, tested at a lower WGSV (3000 ml/g/h), showed 75.3% conversion at 24 h but suffered rapid deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ maintained high and stable CO conversion over extended operation, attributed to their graphene shell structure and iron phase composition. Unsupported-Fe, lacking a protective shell, deactivated rapidly.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-600 showed CO₂ selectivity of 33.3% at 24 h and 33.7% at 184 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The stable CO₂ selectivity suggests consistent water–gas shift activity, with no significant increase over time.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the percentage of methane among total hydrocarbon products:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-x catalysts (500–700 °C) exhibited CH₄ selectivity of 10.5%–12.4% at 24 h. Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe showed higher values of 23.4% and 28.7%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower CH₄ selectivity in Fe@C-x correlates with higher h-Fe₃C content, favoring long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms among total hydrocarbons, calculated by summing the mass of C₅⁺ products and dividing by total hydrocarbon mass.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-600 achieved C₅⁺ selectivity of 60.4% at 24 h, while Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe showed 38.6% and 14.7%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** High C₅⁺ selectivity in Fe@C-600 is linked to the dominance of h-Fe₃C and the presence of a graphene shell, both promoting chain growth.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution:
\[
\alpha = \text{slope of } \ln(W_n/n) \text{ vs. } n
\]
where \( W_n \) is the mass fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-x catalysts exhibited α values of 0.67–0.69 at 24 h. Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe had lower α values of 0.49 and 0.30, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values in Fe@C-x correspond to enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon formation, consistent with high C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate (\%)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 196 h, Fe@C-500 and Fe@C-700 showed deactivation rates of 13.1% and 20.1%, respectively. Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ exhibited negligible deactivation, while unsupported-Fe deactivated by 47.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ effectively prevents nanoparticle aggregation, ensuring superior long-term stability compared to unsupported-Fe.

#### 6.2.7 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY quantifies catalyst activity as moles of CO converted per gram of iron per second:
\[
\text{FTY} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted}}{\text{g Fe} \cdot \text{s}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-600-H₂ achieved an FTY of 203.0 μmol CO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹ at 24 h and 210.5 μmol CO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹ at 184 h. Unsupported-Fe showed 39.5 μmol CO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high FTY of Fe@C-600-H₂ demonstrates its superior intrinsic activity, attributed to the α-Fe phase and graphene shell protection.

---
**Summary:**  
Fe@C core–shell catalysts, especially Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂, exhibit high CO conversion, low CH₄ selectivity, high C₅⁺ selectivity, elevated α factors, and outstanding stability, outperforming unsupported-Fe. The synergy between h-Fe₃C phase and graphene shell is critical for optimal Fischer–Tropsch synthesis performance.
