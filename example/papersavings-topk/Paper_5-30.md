## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines the catalyst synthesis methods with an emphasis on reproducibility and clarity. Two primary synthesis routes are employed: one for the sodium‑doped ferric carbide (Na‑FeC*x*) via co‑precipitation and subsequent processing, and another for the zeolite promoter using hydrothermal or organosilane‑assisted synthesis.

#### Co‑precipitation Synthesis of Na‑FeC*x*

1. • Precursor Mixing  
  – Dissolve 30.0 g FeCl₃⋅6H₂O and 11.9 g FeCl₂⋅4H₂O in deionized water with added HCl.  
  – Equipment: Conventional glassware with pH monitoring.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Add 2.0 M NaOH aqueous solution dropwise until the pH reaches 10 under vigorous stirring (duration: 1 h).  
  – Observations: Formation of a visible precipitate and color change indicate successful co‑precipitation.

3. • Separation and Washing  
  – Isolate the solid phase via centrifugation and wash with deionized water repeatedly (six times).

4. • Drying  
  – Dry the washed precipitate at 100 °C overnight.

5. • Calcination  
  – Calcine the dried precursor at 550 °C for 4 h to ensure the removal of impurities and achieve the desired phase structure.

6. • Post‑treatment  
  – Treat the calcined Na‑Fe₃O₄ sample with syngas at 300 °C for 24 h to form Na‑FeC*x*.  
  – Note: This step integrates sodium doping to modify the electronic environment and catalyst performance.

#### Zeolite Promoter Synthesis

• Hydrothermal Method for s‑ZSM‑5  
  – Mix tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH) with deionized water, then add tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS) and aluminum isopropoxide.  
  – Transfer the mixture into a Teflon‑lined autoclave and crystallize at 180 °C for 2 days, followed by calcination.

• Organosilane‑Assisted Method for s‑S1‑OH  
  – Dissolve TPAOH in deionized water; subsequently add TEOS and diethoxydimethylsilane (DEMS) (molar ratio 1:10), and stir for controlled deposition of silanol groups.  
  – Crystallize in an autoclave at 180 °C for 4 days and finalize with calcination to achieve tailored microporosity.

### Comparative Analysis

The co‑precipitation method enables precise control over precipitate formation and is scalable due to its straightforward reagent handling and pH control. Conversely, the dual approaches for zeolite promoters offer flexibility: the hydrothermal method is conventional with established reproducibility, while the organosilane‑assisted synthesis provides enhanced control over morphology and micropore distribution. Proper granule mixing (20–40 or 40–60 mesh) of the Na‑FeC*x* and zeolite components is critical to optimizing the composite catalyst’s performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation 1: Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 30.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O and 11.9 g FeCl₂·4H₂O are dissolved in 200 mL deionized water with the addition of 5.0 mL aqueous HCl. A sodium precursor (e.g., NaNO₃) is included at a controlled amount for doping.
- **Conditions**: Ambient temperature; vigorous stirring; pH is monitored and adjusted to 10 using a dropwise addition of NaOH solution.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the iron salts completely in the acidic water mixture.
  - Under continuous stirring, add NaOH until the pH reaches 10, ensuring a uniform precipitate of iron species.
  - Maintain stirring for an additional 60 minutes to guarantee complete precursor formation.
- **Key Steps**: Accurate pH control and ensuring full dissolution of reagents are critical.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: The prepared iron precursor, the sodium dopant, and hydrothermally synthesized s‑ZSM‑5 nanosheets.
- **Conditions**: Ambient conditions; granule size controlled within the 20–40 mesh range.
- **Process**:
  - Mechanically mix the Fe precursor with the sodium dopant and s‑ZSM‑5 nanosheets in a typical 3:1 weight ratio.
  - Ensure homogeneous mixing to minimize sodium migration between granules.
- **Key Steps**: Consistent granule size and uniform component distribution are essential.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Reagents**: Abundant deionized water for washing.
- **Conditions**: Centrifugation at moderate speeds for solid separation; drying at 100 °C overnight.
- **Process**:
  - Wash the solid repeatedly (approximately six times) to remove unreacted precursors.
  - Dry the washed material thoroughly at 100 °C, then calcine at 550 °C for 4 hours in air with a controlled ramp rate.
- **Key Steps**: Precise temperature control during calcination safeguards the integrity of both the active phase and the zeolite support.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Conditions**: Pre-reduction at 400 °C for 6 hours under a pure H₂ environment.
- **Process**: Flow hydrogen steadily through the catalyst bed to convert the precursor into the active Fe₅C₂ phase.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The pre‐reduction activation step is fundamental for transforming the precursor into the catalytically active sodium‐doped ferric carbide phase (Na‑FeCₓ), which is essential for effective Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is crucial for inducing the reduction of iron oxides to the active carbide phase while avoiding excessive sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is heated to 400 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 400 °C allows for complete conversion to the dominant Fe₅C₂ phase under controlled conditions.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process is important to ensure a thorough reduction without compromising the catalyst integrity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The pre‐reduction is carried out for 6 hours.
- **Observations:** A 6‐hour treatment ensures full transformation of the precursor while maintaining the stability of active sites.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The reducing gas provides the necessary chemical environment for the conversion of the iron oxide precursor.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen (H₂) is used during the activation.
- **Observations:** The use of pure H₂ creates an efficiently reducing atmosphere, which is critical for forming the desired Na‑FeCₓ phase.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The pressure during the activation influences the reduction kinetics and overall catalyst morphology.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Atmospheric pressure conditions are maintained to support steady and controlled reduction without causing catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Gas Flow
- **Definition:** Controlled gas flow is necessary to ensure uniform exposure of the catalyst particles to the reducing atmosphere.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A constant pure H₂ flow at 10 mL/min is applied.
- **Observations:** This moderate flow rate guarantees that the reducing gas uniformly interacts with the catalyst, thereby optimizing the reduction efficiency without inducing localized over‐reduction.

These activation parameters—temperature, time, gas composition, pressure, and flow rate—are carefully chosen to transition the catalyst precursor to its active state. This optimized activation not only generates the Fe₅C₂ phase but also prepares the catalyst for improved CO conversion and enhanced olefin selectivity in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction tests.

### 5.2 Reaction

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases of the catalysts were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Rigaku D/MAX 2550 diffractometer (CuKα, λ = 1.5418 Å). XRD is essential for confirming the formation and stability of active phases, particularly Fe₅C₂, which is critical for Fischer–Tropsch activity.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of Na‑FeCₓ* catalysts after low-temperature Fischer–Tropsch operation display sole signals corresponding to ferric carbide (Fe₅C₂), with oxide phases being nearly undetectable. The Fe₅C₂ phase is confirmed by matching diffraction peaks to the standard pattern (PDF # 20-0509). The presence of zeolite promoters (e.g., s‑ZSM‑5, s‑S1‑OH) does not alter the dominance of Fe₅C₂ after reaction.
- **Observations:** The persistence of Fe₅C₂ as the main phase, regardless of zeolite addition, indicates robust phase stability and effective carbonization under reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Sodium Migration
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi, Al Kα 1486.6 eV) was used to analyze surface elemental composition and monitor sodium migration between Na‑FeCₓ* and zeolite. Calibration was performed using Au4f₇/₂ (83.96 eV), Ag3d₅/₂ (368.21 eV), and Cu2p₃/₂ (932.62 eV).
- **Findings:** The Na1s XPS peak for sodium on FeCₓ* appears at 1071.8 eV, while sodium on zeolite is detected at 1073.3 eV. Granule-mixed catalysts with larger granules retain sodium predominantly on FeCₓ* (1071.8 eV), whereas powder-mixed samples show significant sodium migration to the zeolite (peak shift toward 1073.3 eV).
- **Observations:** Controlled granule mixing is crucial to minimize sodium migration, preserving the optimal sodium content on FeCₓ* and maintaining catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen sorption isotherms (BET method, Micromeritics ASAP 2020M) were employed to determine surface area and pore volume, which influence reactant accessibility and product diffusion.
- **Findings:** Representative values for zeolite promoters are:  
  - n‑ZSM‑5: 388.7 m²/g, 0.13 cm³/g  
  - s‑ZSM‑5: 337.3 m²/g, 0.21 cm³/g  
  - SSZ‑13: 542.9 m²/g, 0.27 cm³/g  
  - Beta: 471.1 m²/g, 0.18 cm³/g  
  - MOR: 502.7 m²/g, 0.21 cm³/g  
  The b-axis thickness of s‑ZSM‑5 and s‑S1‑OH nanosheets is 90–110 nm.
- **Observations:** The higher pore volume of s‑ZSM‑5 compared to n‑ZSM‑5, despite a slightly lower surface area, reflects the tailored nanosheet morphology, which facilitates rapid olefin desorption and diffusion.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Microstructure
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU‑8010/SU‑1510) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM‑2100F) were used to examine catalyst and zeolite morphology, particle size, and structural integrity.
- **Findings:** SEM and TEM images confirm that s‑ZSM‑5 and s‑S1‑OH possess nanosheet morphologies with controlled b-axis thickness (90–110 nm). After reaction, both Na‑FeCₓ* and zeolite retain their distinct granular and nanosheet structures, indicating morphological stability.
- **Observations:** The uniform and stable morphology of both catalyst and promoter supports efficient physical mixing and sustained catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Acidity and Framework Structure
- **Description:** NH₃-TPD (BELCAT II) and ²⁹Si MAS NMR (Bruker AVANCE-III) were used to assess surface acidity and silicon environments in zeolites.
- **Findings:** NH₃-TPD profiles confirm the presence of acid sites in ZSM‑5-type zeolites, while ²⁹Si MAS NMR spectra verify the integrity of the MFI framework and the presence of silanol groups in s‑S1‑OH.
- **Observations:** The controlled acidity and framework structure of the zeolite promoters are essential for modulating product selectivity and facilitating olefin desorption.

#### 6.1.6 Olefin Adsorption/Desorption Properties
- **Description:** DRIFTS/FTIR (Perkin‑Elmer Frontier) and TPD were used to study olefin adsorption and desorption kinetics.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS/FTIR spectra show that s‑ZSM‑5 accelerates olefin desorption compared to n‑ZSM‑5 and Na‑FeCₓ* alone. TPD profiles corroborate these findings, with desorption rates following the order: Na‑FeCₓ*/s‑ZSM‑5 > Na‑FeCₓ* > Na‑FeCₓ*/n‑ZSM‑5.
- **Observations:** Enhanced olefin desorption from s‑ZSM‑5 promotes rapid product removal, suppressing deep hydrogenation and favoring high olefin selectivity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Na‑FeCₓ alone at 260 °C: 1.9%  
  - Na‑FeCₓ alone at 300 °C: 25.6%  
  - Na‑FeCₓ/s‑ZSM‑5 at 260 °C: 82.5%  
  - Na‑FeCₓ/s‑S1‑OH at 260 °C: 66.5% (up to 82.6% at H₂/CO = 3)
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The incorporation of MFI zeolite nanosheets (s‑ZSM‑5, s‑S1‑OH) dramatically increases CO conversion at low temperature (260 °C), with s‑ZSM‑5 achieving the highest value. The baseline Na‑FeCₓ catalyst requires higher temperature for moderate conversion, highlighting the promotional effect of zeolite nanosheets.

#### 6.2.2 Olefin Selectivity and Distribution
- **Description:** Olefin selectivity is the percentage of hydrocarbons produced as olefins, particularly C₂–C₁₀, and is a key indicator of catalyst performance in Fischer–Tropsch to olefins (FTO) processes.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Na‑FeCₓ/s‑ZSM‑5: ~72% total olefin selectivity, olefin productivity 15.3 mmol/g FeCₓ/h  
  - Na‑FeCₓ/s‑S1‑OH: 81.7% α-olefin selectivity among C₄⁺ olefins, o/a ratio ~3.8, narrow C₂–C₁₀ distribution
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The s‑S1‑OH promoter yields higher α-olefin selectivity and a narrower product range compared to s‑ZSM‑5, attributed to the absence of Brønsted acid sites and enhanced olefin desorption. Both zeolite-promoted catalysts outperform Na‑FeCₓ alone, which produces a broad C₁–C₂₀⁺ distribution.

#### 6.2.3 Methane and Heavy Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** Methane (CH₄) and C₁₀⁺ selectivities reflect the extent of undesired hydrogenation and chain growth, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Na‑FeCₓ/s‑S1‑OH: CH₄ selectivity 6.0%, C₁₀⁺ selectivity 6.3%  
  - Na‑FeCₓ/s‑ZSM‑5: Higher CH₄ and C₁₀⁺ selectivities than s‑S1‑OH
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The controlled microporous environment of s‑S1‑OH minimizes methane and heavy hydrocarbon formation, favoring light olefins.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, represents the probability of chain growth versus termination. Higher α values favor long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - While explicit α values are not numerically reported, catalysts with zeolite promoters exhibit lower α, resulting in a narrow C₂–C₁₀ product range.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Rapid olefin desorption promoted by zeolite nanosheets reduces α, enhancing selectivity for light olefins.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over extended operation. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Na‑FeCₓ/s‑ZSM‑5: CO conversion remains stable (68.1% at 50 h, 67.6% at 600 h); C₂–C₁₀ olefin selectivity stable (67.8–71.0%), o/a ratio 4.2–5.2
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The composite catalysts exhibit exceptional long-term stability, with negligible deactivation over 600 h. Proper granule mixing is critical to prevent sodium migration and maintain performance.

#### 6.2.6 Additional Performance Indicators
- **Olefin Productivity:** Na‑FeCₓ/s‑ZSM‑5 achieves 15.3 mmol/g FeCₓ/h.
- **Effect of Zeolite Structure:** MFI nanosheets (s‑ZSM‑5, s‑S1‑OH) outperform other zeolites due to enhanced olefin desorption and minimized side reactions.

**Summary:**  
The strategic combination of Na‑FeCₓ with MFI zeolite nanosheets yields high CO conversion, superior olefin selectivity, low methane and heavy hydrocarbon formation, and outstanding catalyst stability under low-temperature Fischer–Tropsch conditions.
