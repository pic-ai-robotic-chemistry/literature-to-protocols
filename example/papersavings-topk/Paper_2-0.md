## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis is centered on preparing phase‐pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ from a Raney iron precursor, with variants including unpromoted χ‑Fe₅C₂ and Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂. The procedure involves the following sequential steps:

#### 1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation
• Reagent: An iron–aluminium alloy is treated with an 8 moll⁻¹ KOH solution.  
• Reaction: The KOH solution dissolves aluminium from the alloy, thereby generating a porous Raney iron precursor that enhances surface area.  
• Process: Following dissolution, the precursor is extensively washed with deionized water and ethanol. This washing step removes K⁺ and AlO₂⁻ ions, ensuring high purity.

#### 2. Precipitation–Deposition (Promoter Incorporation)
• For promoted catalysts, a wet impregnation step is employed immediately after the precursor is prepared and washed.  
• Reagent: A dropwise addition of a promoter solution, either Mn(NO₃)₂ for Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ variants or KNO₃ for potassium-promoted systems, introduces the desired dopant.  
• Conditions: The mixture is stirred for 1 hour to achieve uniform distribution of the promoter within the porous structure.

#### 3. Drying
• Equipment: The catalyst is dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature.  
• Duration: The drying process runs for approximately 12 hours, ensuring the complete removal of solvents.

#### 4. Passivation
• Process: The dried precursor is passivated by exposure to a flow of 1% O₂ in helium at room temperature.  
• Duration: Passivation is maintained for 20 hours to stabilize the surface and prevent unwanted oxidation.

#### 5. Carburization (Post‑processing)
• Environment: The passivated catalyst is subjected to in situ carburization under a syngas mixture with the composition H₂/CO/He = 100/3.2/21.8.  
• Temperature: The reactor is gradually heated at approximately 1°C min⁻¹ to reach 350°C, followed by a dwell time of 6 hours.  
• Observation: This controlled heating ensures complete conversion to phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂, avoiding the formation of residual iron oxides and thereby minimizing undesired CO₂ generation.

This structured synthesis method—detailing reagent concentrations, reaction conditions, equipment use, and key observations—ensures reproducibility and scalability for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst preparation.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Phase‐pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ Catalyst (Unpromoted and Mn‑Promoted)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • Iron–aluminium alloy powder (50:50 by weight)  
  • 8 mol/L KOH solution (sufficient to submerge precursor)
- **Conditions**:  
  • Temperature: 70°C  
  • Stirring: Continuous magnetic stirring to ensure homogeneity  
- **Process**:  
  1. Add the iron–aluminium alloy powder to an 8 mol/L KOH solution in a suitable reaction vessel.  
  2. Heat the mixture to 70°C while stirring to selectively dissolve aluminium and form the Raney iron precursor.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  Maintain uniform temperature and stirring to avoid incomplete dissolution.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly (Promotion)
- **Reagents**:  
  • For Mn‐promotion: Mn(NO₃)₂ solution (0.5–12.5 wt%)  
  • For K‐promotion: KNO₃ solution (~1 wt%)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Incorporate immediately after aluminium dissolution  
- **Process**:  
  1. For manganese promotion, add the Mn(NO₃)₂ solution dropwise under stirring. For potassium promotion, add the KNO₃ solution similarly.  
  2. Allow the impregnation to proceed until uniform dispersion is achieved.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Carefully control dopant ratios for targeted catalytic performance.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:  
  1. Wash the impregnated precursor with deionized water ten times to remove K⁺ and AlO₂⁻ ions.  
  2. Follow with seven washes using ethanol to aid in solvent removal.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:  
  • Dry the washed precursor in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 hours.  
- **Passivation**:  
  • Expose to a 1% O₂ in He flow at room temperature for 20 hours to form a protective oxide layer before carburization.

##### Step 5: Activation via In Situ Carburization
- **Conditions**:  
  • Syngas composition: H₂/CO/He = 100/3.2/21.8  
  • Temperature: Increase gradually to 350°C at ~1°C/min  
  • Dwell Time: Maintain 350°C for 6 hours  
- **Process**:  
  1. Introduce the passivated precursor into the reactor.  
  2. Initiate a controlled temperature ramp while exposing the catalyst to the syngas mixture.  
  3. Dwell at 350°C to ensure complete carburization, forming the active χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase.

**Key Caution Points:**  
• Monitor temperature ramp and gas flow rates closely to prevent incomplete carburization or residual iron oxides.  
• Ensure uniform dopant incorporation to optimize CO conversion and selectivity toward linear α‑olefins.

This systematic workflow—from raw precursor synthesis through assembly, purification, drying, passivation, and controlled carburization—ensures reproducibility and high catalyst performance as evidenced by in situ XRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy validations.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

This section details the catalyst activation process for the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis experiments, emphasizing the conversion of a Raney iron precursor into phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ through a controlled carburization step.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for driving the carburization reaction necessary for converting the precursor to the active χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is gradually heated at approximately 1°C/min until reaching 350 °C, at which the process is maintained.
- **Observations:** Heating to 350 °C ensures complete and selective carburization that promotes phase purity while minimizing the formation of undesired phases.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Sufficient activation time is essential for ensuring complete transformation of the catalyst precursor.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Once 350 °C is reached, the catalyst is maintained at this temperature for a dwell time of 6 hours.
- **Observations:** This extended dwell facilitates uniform carbon insertion and stabilizes the active phase, contributing to sustained catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation governs the reduction and carburization dynamics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The in situ carburization is performed using a syngas mixture with the composition H₂/CO/He = 100/3.2/21.8.
- **Observations:** The strongly reducing environment provided by hydrogen, paired with a calibrated CO concentration and inert helium, ensures a controlled carburization process and minimizes over-carburization.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Pressure and Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences gas‐solid interactions, and space-time velocity (SV) controls the exposure rate of the catalyst to the reactive gas.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Carburization is performed under ambient pressure (approximately 0.1 MPa). Additionally, during catalytic testing, various SV values (e.g., 5,000 mL/(g_cat·h)) are applied to ensure differential conditions.
- **Observations:** Operating at ambient pressure aids in uniform gas diffusion, while controlled SV prevents over‐reduction and maintains catalyst integrity.

#### 5.1.5 Pre-Activation Passivation
- **Definition:** The passivation step stabilizes the Raney iron precursor by forming a thin, uniform oxide layer before carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The precursor is passivated by exposure to a flow of 1% O₂ in He at room temperature for 20 hours.
- **Observations:** This mild oxidation prevents excessive oxidation during subsequent carburization and facilitates a uniform conversion to phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂.

These activation procedures, with their tightly controlled parameters, ensure that catalysts reproducibly achieve high activity and stability during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and evolution of the catalysts were monitored using in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are critical for confirming the formation, purity, and stability of the active χ‑Fe₅C₂ (Hägg carbide) phase, which is essential for optimal Fischer–Tropsch performance.
- **Findings:** In situ XRD (Rigaku D/Max-2600/PC) revealed that the transformation from the Raney iron precursor to χ‑Fe₅C₂ begins at 300°C and is completed after 6 hours at 350°C under a syngas flow (H₂/CO/He = 100/3.2/21.8). Rietveld refinement of the XRD data confirmed the exclusive presence of χ‑Fe₅C₂, with no detectable secondary phases. The refinement yielded a goodness of fit (χ² = 7.58%, Rp = 18.7%, Rwp = 15.3%, Rexp = 5.6%), and the lattice parameters matched published values for χ‑Fe₅C₂. In situ Mössbauer spectroscopy further corroborated these results, showing only the χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase present after carburization and during prolonged operation under FT conditions, for both unpromoted and Mn-promoted catalysts.
- **Observations:** The combined use of XRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy demonstrates that the synthesis protocol reliably yields phase-pure χ‑Fe₅C₂, which remains stable under reaction conditions. The absence of iron oxides or other carbide phases is crucial for suppressing unwanted side reactions, such as the water–gas shift.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Microstructure
- **Description:** High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and environmental TEM (ETEM) were employed to visualize the morphological evolution and lattice structure of the catalysts during carburization and operation. These methods provide nanoscale insight into particle uniformity and phase distribution.
- **Findings:** HRTEM (FEI Titan ETEM G2, 300 kV) images captured the transformation of passivated Raney iron (initially crystalline iron with an amorphous oxide shell) into χ‑Fe₅C₂ grains. The conversion initiates at the inner layers and propagates outward, with the final χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase imaged along the (311) direction, exhibiting a lattice spacing of ~2.7 Å. ETEM confirmed the spatial localization of χ‑Fe₅C₂ and the absence of carbon deposition or manganese migration in Mn-promoted samples.
- **Observations:** The uniform formation of χ‑Fe₅C₂ grains and the lack of detectable carbon buildup or promoter segregation indicate effective control over synthesis and operational stability, supporting high catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Phase Purity
- **Description:** Elemental composition was controlled during synthesis via wet impregnation for Mn and K promoters, while phase purity and iron speciation were assessed by Mössbauer spectroscopy.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra confirmed that, after carburization, only the χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase was present in both unpromoted and Mn-promoted catalysts, with no signals from Fe⁰, Fe³⁺, or other iron carbides or oxides. The manganese content (0.5–12.5 wt%) did not introduce additional crystalline phases but enhanced catalyst stability and selectivity.
- **Observations:** The exclusive presence of χ‑Fe₅C₂, regardless of promoter addition, underscores the robustness of the synthesis method and the critical role of phase purity in achieving high Fischer–Tropsch performance.

#### 6.1.4 Porosity and Surface Area
- **Description:** The porous structure of the catalyst is a result of the precursor preparation (aluminum leaching from iron–aluminum alloy), which is essential for facilitating carburization and reactant access.
- **Findings:** While explicit BET surface area or pore size data are not reported, the synthesis protocol (alkaline leaching and thorough washing) yields a highly porous iron precursor, as evidenced by morphological analysis.
- **Observations:** The resulting porous structure is directly linked to the effectiveness of carburization and the accessibility of active sites, contributing to the catalyst’s high activity and selectivity in syngas conversion.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet molar flow rates of CO, respectively, determined via online gas chromatography.
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 70.7% at 290°C, 2.5 MPa, and a space velocity (SV) of 30,000 ml g_cat⁻¹ h⁻¹. Across a range of conditions (250–320°C, 2.3–3.0 MPa, SV 5,000–60,000 ml g_cat⁻¹ h⁻¹), high conversions were consistently observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese promotion significantly enhances CO conversion compared to unpromoted χ‑Fe₅C₂. The catalyst maintains high conversion even at elevated SVs, indicating robust activity under industrially relevant conditions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂, CO₂ selectivity ranged from 9.3–9.4% at 250°C and 3.0 MPa to 21.9% at 290°C and 2.5 MPa.
- **Observations and Trends:** The low CO₂ selectivity reflects effective suppression of the water–gas shift reaction, attributed to the phase purity of χ‑Fe₅C₂ and the presence of Mn, which inhibits CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalyst exhibited low CH₄ selectivity, consistently below that predicted by the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution.
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese promotion suppresses secondary hydrogenation, minimizing methane formation and favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\sum_{x \geq 3} x \cdot F_{C_xH_y,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 250°C, 3.0 MPa, and SV 5,000 ml g_cat⁻¹ h⁻¹, the Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalyst achieved a C₂–C₁₀ linear α‑olefin selectivity of 48.5% (carbon-based), with total C₂–C₁₀ hydrocarbon fractions exceeding 80% at optimal α values.
- **Observations and Trends:** The product distribution closely follows ASF theory, with high selectivity toward valuable linear α‑olefins and minimal light hydrocarbon byproducts.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon growth:
  \[
  \alpha = \frac{r_p}{r_p + r_t}
  \]
  where \( r_p \) and \( r_t \) are the rates of propagation and termination, respectively. Experimentally, α is extracted from the ASF distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalyst exhibited α values of 0.61 at 250°C and 0.63 at 290°C, corresponding to C₂–C₁₀ fractions of 83.1% and 81.5%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** These α values are near-optimal for maximizing C₂–C₁₀ olefin production, confirming efficient chain growth and product selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over extended operation. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 225 h of continuous operation at 320–325°C, 2.3 MPa, and SV 100,000 ml syngas g_cat⁻¹ h⁻¹, the Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalyst maintained stable CO conversion and selectivity, with no significant deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** In situ characterization confirmed the retention of phase-pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ and absence of carbon deposition or Mn migration, demonstrating exceptional catalyst robustness.
