## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis methods are organized into two main procedures: the hydrothermal preparation of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles followed by Mn deposition, and the modified Stöber process for SiO₂ coating—with an additional hydrophobic modification step where required.

#### Hydrothermal Synthesis and Calcination of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  - Dissolve 7.5 g of FeCl₃·6H₂O in 110 mL of water.  
  - Add 12.4 g of sodium acetate trihydrate and 3.8 g of hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide.  
  - Stir for 0.5 h, then introduce 26 mL of ethylenediamine and stir for an additional hour.

2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  - During hydrothermal treatment, the formation of the iron precursor occurs, implicitly incorporating precipitation phenomena.

3. • Hydrothermal Treatment  
  - Transfer the mixture into a Teflon‑lined autoclave and treat at 200 °C for 10 h.

4. • Drying and Calcination  
  - Wash and dry the product at 100 °C for 12 h.  
  - Finally, calcine at 500 °C for 5 h in air, ensuring the formation of high‐purity Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.

#### Mn Deposition and SiO₂ Coating Via the Modified Stöber Process

1. • Mn Deposition (Deposition–Precipitation)  
  - Disperse 0.5 g of the Fe₂O₃ in 300 mL of ethanol.  
  - Add dropwise 5 mL of an aqueous solution containing 0.078 g of KMnO₄ at 30 °C with stirring for 2 h, allowing uniform Mn deposition.

2. • SiO₂ Shell Formation (Modified Stöber Process)  
  - Disperse the Mn-promoted Fe₂O₃ in 150 mL of ethanol using ultrasonication.  
  - Add 2.5 mL of 25–28% ammonia solution and 10 mL of water.  
  - Introduce TEOS (0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL, as per the targeted shell thickness) while stirring vigorously at 30 °C for 3 h followed by an additional 4 h.

3. • Drying and Hydrophobic Modification  
  - Dry the Fe@Mn@xSi catalyst in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for 10 h.  
  - For hydrophobic conversion (Fe@Mn@xSi-c), disperse the dried catalyst in 70 mL n‑hexane, add 0.5 mL chlorotrimethylsilane, and subject the suspension to ultrasonic treatment at room temperature for 1 h.

### Comparative Analysis

The hydrothermal method, though complex and equipment-intensive, ensures high-purity nanoparticle formation. In contrast, the modified Stöber method provides precise control over the SiO₂ shell thickness, which is critical for catalyst stability and performance. Each method’s detailed steps with specific reagent concentrations, temperatures, and durations ensure high reproducibility for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis applications.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Mn@xSi (Hydrophilic) and Fe@Mn@xSi-c (Hydrophobic)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 
  - 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 12.4 g sodium acetate trihydrate, 3.8 g hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide, and 26 mL ethylenediamine.
- **Conditions**: 
  - Dissolution in deionized water followed by hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 10 hours.
  - Drying at 100 °C for 12 hours post-synthesis.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the precursors to form a homogeneous solution.
  - Transfer to a hydrothermal reactor; conduct the treatment ensuring controlled temperature and pressure.
- **Key Steps**:
  - Ensure complete precursor dissolution and maintain accurate temperature profiles to achieve uniform Fe₂O₃ nanoparticle formation.

##### Step 2: Mn Promoter Deposition
- **Reagents**:
  - KMnO₄ (0.078 g dissolved in 5 mL water) with 0.5 g of the prepared Fe₂O₃ dispersed in 300 mL ethanol.
- **Conditions**: 
  - Stir at 30 °C for 2 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Add the KMnO₄ solution dropwise while stirring, then centrifuge, wash with water, and dry at 100 °C for 12 hours.
- **Key Steps**: 
  - Dropwise addition ensures uniform Mn deposition on the Fe₂O₃ support.

##### Step 3: Catalyst Assembly via Modified Stöber Process
- **Reagents**:
  - TEOS (x mL for varied shell thickness: 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8), 2.5 mL ammonia solution (25–28%), and 10 mL water in ethanol.
- **Conditions**: 
  - Stir at 30 °C under a basic environment for 3 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Add TEOS to the dispersion; allow hydrolysis and condensation to form an amorphous SiO₂ shell.
- **Key Steps**: 
  - Precisely control TEOS volume and pH to tailor the shell thickness.

##### Step 4: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Process**:
  - Centrifuge and wash the catalyst with n-hexane.
  - Dry in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 6 hours.
  - Calcine at 500 °C for 5 hours in an air atmosphere (controlled ramp rate).
- **Key Steps**: 
  - Uniform drying and calcination are crucial to preserving shell integrity.

##### Step 5: Activation and Hydrophobic Modification (Fe@Mn@xSi-c)
- **Reagents**:
  - Chlorotrimethylsilane in n-hexane.
- **Conditions**: 
  - Ultrasonic treatment at room temperature for 1 hour.
- **Process**:
  - Immerse the dried catalyst in the n-hexane/chlorotrimethylsilane mixture to modify surface properties.
- **Key Steps**:
  - The hydrophobic layer minimizes residual Cl contact, enhancing catalyst performance as evidenced by reduced CH₄ selectivity and improved olefin formation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation protocol is designed to prepare the iron-based Fischer–Tropsch catalyst (Fe@Mn@xSi and its hydrophobized variant Fe@Mn@xSi-c) for optimal performance by ensuring complete reduction and subsequent carbide formation. This process involves two distinct steps executed under precisely controlled conditions.

#### 5.1.1 Reduction in Pure H₂
In the initial reduction step, the catalyst is subjected to pure H₂ at 350 °C under an applied pressure of 0.1 MPa for a duration of 20 hours. This step is critical for transforming Fe₂O₃ into intermediate reduced forms, setting the stage for complete activation. The prolonged exposure to hydrogen ensures that the iron oxide is fully reduced, thereby minimizing the presence of undesired oxidic species. The use of a controlled reducing atmosphere and precise temperature control facilitates the formation of a uniform metallic phase, which is essential for subsequent carburization.

#### 5.1.2 Syngas Activation
Following the reduction phase, the catalyst is activated by exposure to a syngas mixture with a H₂/CO ratio of 2. The activation step is performed at 320 °C and 0.1 MPa for 5 hours. This tailored gas composition plays a pivotal role in promoting the sequential carbonization where the reduced iron species are converted into active carbide phases, such as Fe₅C₂, which are vital for effective CO activation and C–C coupling. The transition to a syngas atmosphere aids in balancing the degree of carburization while preventing excessive sintering. Moreover, the procedure is integrated into the fixed-bed reactor setup where a space-time velocity of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹ is maintained during subsequent reaction tests. This high flow rate ensures continuous and uniform gas exposure, which is crucial both for activation efficiency and reproducibility.

Overall, the careful orchestration of these two steps—each with rigorously specified temperature, pressure, gas composition, and activation time—ensures that the catalyst achieves a stable, carbide-rich phase that translates directly into high catalytic activity and operational stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Evolution
- **Description:** The crystal phases present in the catalysts were identified and monitored using X-ray diffraction (XRD), in situ XRD, and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are critical for determining the active and inactive iron phases that govern Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that, under syngas conversion conditions, hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts form a mixture of iron carbide (Fe₅C₂) and Fe₃O₄ phases. In contrast, the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst predominantly exhibits iron carbide phases, specifically 74.1% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ-Fe₃C, with no detectable Fe₃O₄. Characteristic diffraction peaks at 44.2° (2θ) correspond to Fe₅C₂, while peaks at 35.4°, 57.0°, and 62.5° (2θ) indicate Fe₃O₄. In situ XRD under FTS conditions confirms that hydrophobic modification stabilizes the iron carbide phase and suppresses Fe₃O₄ formation.
- **Observations:** The preservation of iron carbide phases in hydrophobic catalysts is attributed to inhibited water adsorption, which prevents oxidation of the active phase. Increasing SiO₂ shell thickness (via higher TEOS dosage) reduces the formation of FexC phases and promotes Fe₃O₄, as confirmed by both XRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Shell Uniformity
- **Description:** Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) analysis and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were employed to determine elemental composition and verify the uniformity of the SiO₂ shell.
- **Findings:** ICP analysis confirmed that the Fe, Mn, and Si contents in the catalysts matched the intended synthesis ratios, indicating successful and uniform SiO₂ shell formation. EDS mapping further demonstrated homogeneous distribution of Si around the FeMn core.
- **Observations:** Uniform SiO₂ coating is essential for protecting the active core, ensuring catalyst stability, and controlling reactant diffusion.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Water-droplet contact angle measurements were used to assess the surface hydrophobicity of the catalysts, which influences water adsorption and catalyst stability during FTS.
- **Findings:** Hydrophobic modification with chlorotrimethylsilane significantly increased the contact angle, confirming enhanced surface hydrophobicity in Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalysts compared to their hydrophilic counterparts.
- **Observations:** Enhanced hydrophobicity effectively inhibits water ingress, stabilizing the iron carbide phase and improving catalyst selectivity and longevity.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Structural Integrity
- **Description:** Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and EDS mapping were utilized to examine catalyst morphology, shell integrity, and core evolution after reaction.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed that Fe@Mn nanoparticles are uniformly encapsulated by an amorphous SiO₂ shell. After FTS, the FeMn core in Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c exhibited shrinkage, reflecting phase transformation, but remained well-protected by the shell.
- **Observations:** The robust and uniform SiO₂ shell prevents sintering and aggregation, maintaining catalyst dispersion and structural integrity throughout reaction cycles.

#### 6.1.5 CO Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** In situ Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was used to probe CO adsorption behavior, which is critical for FTS activity.
- **Findings:** For chlorine-free Fe@Mn@0.05Si, DRIFTS spectra displayed strong carbonate bands (1700–1200 cm⁻¹) and gaseous CO doublets (2172, 2117 cm⁻¹), indicating effective CO adsorption and redox activity. In contrast, chlorine-containing Fe@Mn@0.05Si-c showed only gaseous CO bands, with no carbonate formation, signifying inhibited CO adsorption.
- **Observations:** Residual chlorine in thin-shell catalysts impedes CO adsorption, underscoring the importance of adequate shell thickness and chlorine removal for optimal catalytic performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during reaction, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{n_{\text{CO,in}} - n_{\text{CO,out}}}{n_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( n_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( n_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar amounts of CO entering and exiting the reactor, respectively. Calculations are based on carbon balance at steady state after 12 h.
- **Experimental Data:** For hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts, CO conversion decreases from 53.8% (Fe@Mn@0.05Si) to 24.3% (Fe@Mn@2.8Si) as SiO₂ shell thickness increases. Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c and Fe@Mn@0.05Si-c exhibit CO conversion below 2% in CO + H₂O model experiments, while hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si achieves ~30% under the same conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Thicker SiO₂ shells hinder CO access and carburization, reducing CO conversion. Hydrophobic modification restricts water entry, suppressing the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction and lowering CO conversion in the presence of external H₂O, but stabilizes performance under standard syngas conditions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of WGS side reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{n_{\text{CO}_2,\text{product}}}{n_{\text{CO,in}} - n_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts show CO₂ selectivity around 40%. For Fe@Mn@0.2Si, CO conversion to CO₂ is 28.9%. Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c reduces this to 7.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic surfaces suppress WGS, significantly lowering CO₂ selectivity. Thicker SiO₂ shells on hydrophilic catalysts facilitate water participation, increasing CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{n_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{n_{\text{CO,in}} - n_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity decreases from 21.5% (Fe@Mn@0.2Si) to 14.1% (Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c) upon hydrophobic modification.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic modification suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbons:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{n_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + n_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2n_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2n_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2n_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{n_{\text{CO,in}} - n_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** C₃⁺ selectivity decreases with increasing SiO₂ shell thickness. Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c exhibits higher C₃⁺ and olefin selectivity (olefin selectivity increases from 49.8% to 64.3%) compared to hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal shell thickness and hydrophobicity enhance C₃⁺ and olefin selectivity, while excessive shell thickness or chlorine contamination reduces performance.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended operation. Deactivation rate is inferred from the decline in CO conversion during continuous reaction.
- **Experimental Data:** Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c maintains stable CO conversion over 110 h, indicating low deactivation. In contrast, Fe@Mn@0.05Si-c (with residual chlorine) rapidly deactivates, with CO conversion dropping below 2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Proper SiO₂ shell thickness prevents chlorine poisoning and preserves the active iron carbide phase, ensuring long-term stability. Chlorine contamination or insufficient shell thickness leads to rapid deactivation.

---
**Summary:** The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst, with optimized SiO₂ shell thickness, demonstrates superior performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis: high CO conversion and C₃⁺/olefin selectivity, low CO₂ and CH₄ selectivity, and excellent long-term stability. These results underscore the critical role of surface hydrophobization and shell engineering in tuning catalyst activity and selectivity.
