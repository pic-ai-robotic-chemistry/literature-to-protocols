## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ catalysts involves a surface reconstruction approach using well-defined Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals as precursors. Two distinct morphologies are produced—nanocubes and octahedra—which subsequently form core–shell structures when processed over a SiC support.

#### • Precursor Preparation and Mixing
1. Precursor Synthesis:  
   - Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals (cubes with an average size of ≈40–45 nm or octahedra) are synthesized from Fe-based precursors under controlled mixing conditions in a multi-neck flask.  
   - Reagents include Fe precursors, with precise concentrations ensuring uniform nanocrystal formation.  
   - The support material, SiC, is introduced to provide enhanced dispersion and structural stability in later processing.

2. Precipitation–Deposition:  
   - In this synthesis workflow, the conventional precipitation–deposition step is integrated within the controlled precursor mixing. The formation and deposition of the Fe₃O₄ phase occur under conditions that obviate the need for a discrete post-mixing precipitation step.

#### • Step-by-step Experimental Procedures
3. Reduction:  
   - The prepared Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals are subjected to a reduction process in a fixed-bed reactor.  
   - Conditions: 270 °C, 1 bar H₂, for a duration of 10 hours.  
   - Outcome: The reduction of Fe₃O₄ to metallic Fe is confirmed by the disappearance of surface organics and the uniform transformation visibly noted by appropriate changes in the catalyst appearance.

4. Surface Reconstruction (Fixed‑Bed Treatment):  
   - The metallic Fe is further treated under a fixed-bed reactor environment using a mixture of syngas (CO, H₂, and a small fraction of Ar).  
   - Conditions: 20 bar pressure at 270 °C for 20 hours.  
   - Outcome: During this step, simultaneous carburization and mild oxidation yield the desired core–shell structure, forming an outer χ-Fe₅C₂ layer that exposes specific facets—{202} for nanocubes and {112} for octahedra.  
   - No additional promoters or additives are employed.

#### • Comparative Analysis
- Advantages: The surface reconstruction process allows controlled facet exposure and reproducible core–shell formation, essential for tailored catalytic performance.  
- Disadvantages: The integration of multiple high-pressure and high-temperature steps may present challenges in synthesis scalability compared to simpler wet‐chemical routes that yield pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles but lack surface control.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ Nanocubes/SiC

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Synthesis of Fe₃O₄ Nanocubes  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 1.4 g Fe(acac)₃  
  • 1.0 g 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid  
  • 20.0 mL butyl ether (BE)  
  • 2.5 mL oleic acid (OA)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Heat the reaction solution to 290 °C for 30 minutes under vigorous stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  • Dissolve the reagents in the solvent mixture ensuring complete homogenization.  
  • Degas the solution to remove air before initiating nucleation.  
  • Maintain the set temperature to promote the formation of uniform nanocubes.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Accurate temperature control and thorough mixing are critical for obtaining defined cube morphology.

##### Step 2: Purification and Post-Processing  
- **Process**:  
  • Precipitate the nanocubes by adding ethanol, then wash three times with hexane to remove residual organics.  
  • Isolate the product by gentle filtration, ensuring minimal disturbance of the particle size.

##### Step 3: Drying, Reduction & Surface Reconstruction  
- **Drying & Activation**:  
  • The purified nanocubes are dried under mild conditions before being subjected to reduction at 270 °C for 10 hours in H₂ (1 bar, 100 mL/min).  
- **Catalyst Assembly**:  
  • After reduction, load the nanocubes onto SiC support.  
  • In a fixed-bed reactor (inner diameter 9 mm), expose the catalyst to 20 bar syngas (composition: 64 vol% H₂, 32 vol% CO, 4 vol% Ar) at 270 °C for 20 hours.  
  • This treatment promotes selective carburization and oxidation to form the χ-Fe₅C₂ shell primarily exposing the {202} facet.  
- **Caution**:  
  • Upon reaction completion, switch feed to N₂ to protect the catalyst from atmospheric oxidation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is executed in two sequential stages to transform the Fe₃O₄ precursor into the active core–shell Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ structure required for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.1 Hydrogen Reduction Stage

In the initial activation step, the Fe₃O₄ precursor is reduced in pure H₂ at 270 °C under atmospheric pressure (1 bar) for 10 hours. This temperature is judiciously selected as it is sufficiently high to remove surface organics and fully reduce the iron oxide to metallic Fe, while preserving the nanocube morphology. The use of 1 bar H₂ provides a controlled, mild reducing environment that prevents undesirable structural changes, ensuring the proper formation of metallic Fe nanocubes that serve as a precursor for subsequent transformations.

#### 5.1.2 Fixed-Bed Reactor Treatment Stage

Following reduction, the catalyst is transferred to a fixed-bed reactor for syngas activation. Here, the catalyst is exposed to a syngas mixture composed of 64 vol% H₂, 32 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar at 270 °C under a pressure of 20 bar for 20 hours. The elevated pressure significantly increases the concentration of reactive species, facilitating the carburization process that transforms the reduced metallic Fe into a uniform χ-Fe₅C₂ shell while concurrently enabling controlled oxidation of the inner core to Fe₃O₄. The space-time velocity during this step is maintained at approximately 2400 mL/(g_cat·h), ensuring uniform exposure of the catalyst surface to the syngas mixture. This balanced environment is critical for achieving the targeted core–shell morphology and maintaining high catalyst stability and performance during long-term Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

Observations from the procedure indicate that the two-stage activation—first under pure H₂ and then under high-pressure syngas—not only ensures complete reduction and subsequent carburization but also supports consistent morphological features. The precise control over temperature, pressure, gas composition, space-time velocity, and duration is essential for replicability and optimal catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical factor influencing catalyst activity, syngas activation, and the CO dissociation pathway. In these experiments the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was carried out at 270 °C. This temperature has been shown to facilitate a hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation process on the {202} facets of the Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes and is essential for activating syngas effectively. Under these conditions, the Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes exhibited outstanding thermal stability; CO conversion remained stable (fluctuating within 1% over 100 hours) and the catalyst’s morphology retained its integrity with an observed increase in the iron carbide shell thickness from 2.0 nm to 2.6 nm.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure directly impacts syngas concentration and the thermodynamic favorability of hydrocarbon chain formation. The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was conducted in a fixed-bed reactor at a controlled pressure of 20 bar. This elevated pressure increases the partial pressures of the reactants, thereby promoting enhanced conversion rates. Notably, high syngas pressure at 20 bar has been linked with favorable conditions for the formation of longer-chain (C₅⁺) hydrocarbons, as evidenced by high CO conversion efficiencies observed with the nanocube catalysts.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The hydrogen-to-carbon monoxide ratio is pivotal in steering reaction selectivity and dictating the distribution of hydrocarbon products. The process employed a H₂/CO ratio of 2:1, specified as 64 vol% H₂ and 32 vol% CO. This balance was critical for minimizing excess methane formation and enhancing the production of olefins as well as C₅⁺ species. The optimized ratio contributed to a favorable reaction environment, supporting efficient catalytic performance.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
Space-time velocity, commonly expressed as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), determines the contact time between the syngas and the catalyst. In these tests, a GHSV of 2400 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹ was maintained, ensuring effective reactant-catalyst interaction. This condition was instrumental in achieving efficient CO conversion while preserving catalyst stability throughout the reaction duration.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy, which are essential for identifying active phases and confirming the core–shell architecture.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed the coexistence of Fe₃O₄ (magnetite) and χ-Fe₅C₂ (iron carbide) as the principal phases in both nanocube and octahedral catalysts. After syngas treatment (20 bar, 270 °C), new diffraction peaks corresponding to χ-Fe₅C₂ appeared, confirming the formation of a crystalline carbide shell. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the phase fractions: Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes contained approximately 63.4 wt% Fe₃O₄ and 33.2 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂, while octahedra had 65.1 wt% Fe₃O₄ and 29.5 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The presence and stability of the χ-Fe₅C₂ shell are directly linked to catalytic activity, with the {202} facet in nanocubes and {112} facet in octahedra confirmed by Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) analysis of TEM images.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Spatial Distribution
- **Description:** STEM-EDX and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) were employed to map the spatial distribution of elements, providing insight into the core–shell configuration.
- **Findings:** Elemental mapping showed Fe and O concentrated in the core (Fe₃O₄), while Fe and C were localized in the shell (χ-Fe₅C₂). Fe was distributed throughout, but O was confined to the core and C to the shell.
- **Observations:** This spatial separation confirms the successful synthesis of a Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ core–shell structure.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were measured by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, which are critical for understanding reactant accessibility and diffusion.
- **Findings:** BET analysis yielded surface areas of 20.4 m²/g for nanocubes and 22.0 m²/g for octahedra, with both exhibiting nonporous surface layers in the fresh state. After 100 h on stream, octahedra developed mesopores (average diameter 16.0 nm, BJH method), while nanocubes remained nonporous.
- **Observations:** The moderate surface areas and nonporous shells indicate that catalytic reactions occur primarily at the external surface. The formation of mesopores in spent octahedra correlates with structural degradation and reduced stability.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Facet Exposure
- **Description:** High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and TEM were used to assess particle morphology, shell thickness, and facet exposure.
- **Findings:** Both nanocubes (average size 40.5 ± 3.9 nm) and octahedra (45.4 ± 3.5 nm) displayed uniform, well-defined shapes. The χ-Fe₅C₂ shell thickness was ~2.0 nm for nanocubes and ~1.7 nm for octahedra. FFT analysis confirmed that nanocubes predominantly expose the {202} facet, while octahedra expose the {112} facet.
- **Observations:** Nanocubes retained their morphology and shell integrity after prolonged reaction, whereas octahedra developed mesopores and showed signs of structural collapse, explaining their lower catalytic stability.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and CO Activation Pathways
- **Description:** In-situ diffuse reflection infrared spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was conducted to probe CO dissociation mechanisms and activation energies under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** For nanocubes, DRIFTS detected vibrational peaks at 2955 cm⁻¹ (CH₃*), 2919 and 2849 cm⁻¹ (CH₂*, CHO*), and 1741 cm⁻¹ (C=O in CHO*), indicating a hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway. Octahedra showed only direct CO dissociation products. The apparent activation energy for CO dissociation was 68.6 kJ/mol for nanocubes (hydrogen-assisted) and 103.7 kJ/mol for octahedra (direct).
- **Observations:** The lower activation energy and presence of hydrogen-assisted intermediates on nanocubes correlate with higher catalytic activity and selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (20 bar, 270 °C, space velocity 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹, H₂/CO = 2:1), Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a CO conversion of 45.4%. The octahedral counterpart exhibited an initial CO conversion of 21.2%, which declined to 11.7% after 100 hours on stream.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes consistently outperformed the octahedra, attributed to the exposure of the {202} facet, which facilitates a more efficient hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water-gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** While specific numerical values for CO₂ selectivity are not detailed for each catalyst, the article reports that all catalysts maintained a carbon balance above 95%, ensuring reliable selectivity calculations.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes exhibited lower CO₂ selectivity compared to octahedra, consistent with their higher hydrocarbon productivity and more favorable reaction pathway.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC, CH₄ selectivity is reported as 15.5 C%. For nanocubes and octahedra, CH₄ selectivity can be inferred from the product distribution, with nanocubes showing lower CH₄ selectivity due to higher selectivity toward longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower CH₄ selectivity in nanocubes correlates with enhanced chain growth and higher C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\sum_{n=5}^{\infty} n \cdot F_{\text{C}_n\text{H}_m,\text{out}}}{\sum_{i} i \cdot F_{\text{C}_i\text{H}_m,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a C₅⁺ selectivity of 54.2 C%, while octahedra/SiC reached 47.1 C%. The χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC reference showed a C₅⁺ selectivity of 49.3 C%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes’ higher C₅⁺ selectivity indicates superior chain growth capability, aligning with their higher α factor.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
  - α = probability of chain growth (0 < α < 1).
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values are 0.66 for nanocubes/SiC, 0.62 for octahedra/SiC, and 0.63 for χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher α for nanocubes is consistent with their enhanced selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF measures the intrinsic activity, defined as moles of CO converted per mole of surface Fe atoms per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC exhibited a TOF of 645.9 h⁻¹, while octahedra/SiC showed 372.7 h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior TOF of nanocubes is attributed to the favorable {202} facet and efficient hydrogen-assisted CO activation.

#### 6.2.7 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Octahedra/SiC showed a decline in CO conversion from 20.1% to 11.7% over 100 hours, indicating significant deactivation. Nanocubes/SiC maintained stable performance.
- **Observations and Trends:** The rapid deactivation of octahedra is linked to structural collapse and carbon deposition, while nanocubes retain their core–shell structure and activity.
