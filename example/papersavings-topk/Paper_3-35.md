## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis routes for preparing iron‐based catalysts (e.g., 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 and its Na₂S‐modified variants) as reported in the article. Two primary methods are employed: the co‐precipitation approach for FeZn powder formation and subsequent impregnation techniques for promoter incorporation.

#### Co‑precipitation Method for FeZn Powders
1. • Precursor Mixing: Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃⋅9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂⋅6H₂O in deionized water to achieve a total molar amount of 0.18 mol with predefined Fe:Zn ratios (4:1, 2:1, or 1:1). A Teflon‑lined stainless‐steel autoclave is used for mixing.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition: Stir the solution at 700 rpm for 30 minutes. Slowly add ammonia until the pH is adjusted to 9, ensuring the uniform formation of metal hydroxide precipitates. Continue stirring for an additional 1 hour followed by static aging for 2 hours.
3. • Drying: Filter and wash the precipitate with deionized water, then dry at 100 °C for 12 hours.
4. • Calcination: Calcine the dried powders at 400 °C for 4 hours in air to obtain the desired oxide phases.
5. • Post‑Treatment (if applicable): A further reduction in flowing H₂ at 450 °C for 5 hours and subsequent passivation (1% O₂/99% N₂ at room temperature for 2 hours) may be performed to stabilize the catalyst.

#### Impregnation Methods for Promoter Incorporation
• Incipient Wetness Impregnation: Use a Na₂CO₃ solution to introduce Na into the calcined FeZn powders with loadings of 0.75, 1.5, or 3 wt%.
• Mixed Impregnation: For NaS‐FeZn catalysts, simultaneously impregnate with Na₂CO₃ and Na₂S solutions (e.g., 1.5 wt% total Na combined with 0.1–0.3 wt% S) to tailor catalytic properties.
• Final Calcination: Post‐impregnation, perform another calcination step (temperature and duration consistent with earlier conditions) to secure the promoters.

Throughout the process, visual indicators such as color changes and the appearance of precipitates confirm the progress of synthesis. Detailed control of reagent concentrations, pH, and thermal treatments ensures reproducibility and uniformity in catalyst preparation, facilitating subsequent performance evaluation in Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 and Na2S‐modified Variants

##### Step 1: Synthesis of FeZn Precursor Powder  
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃⋅9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂⋅6H₂O (total molar amount = 0.18 mol) dissolved in 500 mL deionized water.

  - Dissolve the metal nitrates in water to form a homogeneous solution.  
  - Stir the solution at 700 rpm for 30 minutes.  
  - Gradually add ammonia while monitoring the pH until reaching 9, ensuring optimal precipitation of metal hydroxides.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** Crystal phases were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Brucker D2 PHASER analyzer (Cu Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å), scanning from 2θ = 5° to 90° at 0.02° steps. This analysis elucidates the evolution of iron and zinc phases during synthesis, reduction, and reaction, which is critical for Fischer-Tropsch catalyst performance.
- **Findings:** Fresh catalysts predominantly exhibited the ZnFe₂O₄ (PDF#77–11) spinel phase, with a minor ZnO (PDF#79–208) peak. After H₂ reduction, ZnFe₂O₄ was converted to metallic Fe⁰ and ZnO, with Fe⁰ displaying high crystallinity. In spent catalysts post-CO₂-FTS, Fe⁰ was further transformed into Fe₃O₄ (PDF#82–1533) and χ‐Fe₅C₂ (PDF#89–2544), while ZnO remained. The addition of Na₂S altered the spent catalyst phases, resulting in Fe⁰, Fe₃C, and Fe₅C₂, but no Fe₃O₄. In CO-FTS, spent catalysts mainly showed χ‐Fe₅C₂ and ZnO, with Fe₃O₄ absent.
- **Observations:** The presence of χ‐Fe₅C₂ is associated with high Fischer-Tropsch activity. Na₂S suppresses carburization and Fe₃O₄ formation in CO₂-FTS, while in CO-FTS, it does not significantly alter the carbide phase composition.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were measured by N₂ physisorption at 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020. Samples (~300 mg) were degassed under vacuum at 200 °C for 4 h. Surface area was calculated by the BET method, pore volume at P/P₀ = 0.9997, and pore size by the BJH method. These parameters influence reactant diffusion and catalyst stability.
- **Findings:** The addition of Zn increased the surface area and improved Fe dispersion. Representative values for 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 include a surface area of 62.3 m²/g, pore volume of 0.18 cm³/g, and average pore diameter of 11.6 nm. Na₂S addition did not significantly affect these textural properties.
- **Observations:** Enhanced surface area and porosity correlate with improved catalyst stability and performance, while S modification maintains these properties.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphology and particle size were examined by SEM (Hitachi Regulus 8100) and TEM (FEI TE20), with EDX mapping for elemental distribution. These analyses reveal the dispersion and aggregation of active phases.
- **Findings:** Fresh catalysts showed block-like ZnFe₂O₄ particles with ZnO protrusions. After reduction, large Fe⁰ nanoparticles (260–290 nm) with smaller ZnO nanoparticles (60–70 nm) formed a Fe⁰@ZnO structure. In spent 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 after CO-FTS, Fe nanoparticles were encapsulated by a carbon shell (up to 28 nm thick), while Na₂S-modified catalysts exhibited larger, more dispersed Fe particles with minimal carbon shell formation.
- **Observations:** Na₂S addition inhibits carbon deposition and aggregation, resulting in more dispersed iron particles and improved catalyst longevity.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Distribution
- **Description:** Elemental composition was quantified by ICP-OES (PerkinElmer Avio 200) and spatially resolved by EDX mapping. Accurate promoter and metal loadings are essential for correlating structure with catalytic function.
- **Findings:** ICP-OES confirmed Fe, Zn, Na, and S contents matched theoretical values. EDX mapping showed Fe concentrated in spherical particles, Zn and Na highly overlapped (Na mainly on ZnO surfaces), and S uniformly distributed in Na₂S-modified samples. After reaction, Fe@C structures were observed in unmodified catalysts, while S-modified catalysts showed more uniform Fe and C distribution.
- **Observations:** The intended promoter distribution is achieved, and S addition leads to more homogeneous elemental dispersion, supporting improved catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.5 Carbon Deposition Analysis
- **Description:** Carbon deposition was assessed by Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw inVia, 785 nm excitation) and O₂-TPO (MicrotracBEL, 25–900 °C at 10 °C/min, 50 mL/min O₂). These techniques distinguish between graphitic and defective carbon and quantify carbon accumulation.
- **Findings:** Raman spectra showed strong D (1313 cm⁻¹) and G (1600 cm⁻¹) bands in spent 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1, indicating significant defective carbon. O₂-TPO revealed a prominent oxidation peak at ~300 °C, confirming carbon deposition. Na₂S-modified catalysts exhibited weaker D bands and lower TPO peak intensity, indicating reduced carbon accumulation.
- **Observations:** Na₂S effectively suppresses carbon deposition, which is critical for maintaining catalyst activity during long-term operation.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Hydrophobicity (for SiO₂@C16 Support)
- **Description:** Hydrophobicity of SiO₂ supports was measured by contact angle analysis (OCA-40, 0°–180°, ±0.1° accuracy). Surface hydrophobicity affects water management in tandem FTS/cracking systems.
- **Findings:** Untreated SiO₂ had a contact angle of 27.5°, while SiO₂@C16 reached 125.5°, confirming successful hydrophobic modification. After atmospheric-pressure CO₂-FTS, the contact angle remained high (116.8°), but decreased to 69.8° after high-pressure operation, indicating partial loss of hydrophobicity.
- **Observations:** Hydrophobic modification is effective under moderate conditions but degrades under high-pressure, high-water environments.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst. It is calculated as:
\[
\text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO₂ at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard CO₂-FTS conditions (320 °C, 1 MPa, H₂/CO₂ = 3, GHSV = 1200 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹), the 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 catalyst achieves a CO₂ conversion of 39.0%. Upon Na₂S modification (e.g., 1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1), conversion drops significantly.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Na₂S suppresses CO₂ conversion, attributed to inhibited iron phase evolution and reduced formation of active carbide and oxide phases.

#### 6.2.2 CO Selectivity
- **Description:** CO selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO₂ that forms CO, indicating the extent of the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction. It is defined as:
\[
S_{\text{CO}} = \frac{f_{\text{CO}} \cdot \frac{A_{\text{CO}}}{A_{\text{N}_2}}_{\text{out}}}{f_{\text{CO}_2} \cdot \left( \frac{A_{\text{CO}_2}}{A_{\text{N}_2}}_{\text{in}} - \frac{A_{\text{CO}_2}}{A_{\text{N}_2}}_{\text{out}} \right)} \times 100\%
\]
where \( f \) denotes calibration factors and \( A \) the GC peak areas.
- **Experimental Data:** For 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1, CO selectivity is 15.6%. With Na₂S addition, CO selectivity increases markedly.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher Na₂S loadings favor CO formation, indicating a shift toward RWGS and away from hydrocarbon synthesis.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO₂ yielding methane, calculated as:
\[
S_{\text{CH}_4} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{in}} - F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 catalyst exhibits a CH₄ selectivity of 12.7%. Na₂S-modified catalysts show increased CH₄ selectivity: 55.9% (1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1) and 58.9% (1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1).
- **Observations and Trends:** Na₂S promotes hydrogenation, leading to higher methane yields and reduced olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO₂ forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
\[
S_{\text{C}_5^+} = 100 - S_{\text{CH}_4} - S_{\text{C}_2^0–\text{C}_4^0} - S_{\text{C}_2^-–\text{C}_4^-}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1, C₅⁺ selectivity is 48.5%. Na₂S-modified catalysts show a sharp decline: 1.4% (1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1) and 2.1% (1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1).
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing Na₂S content drastically reduces long-chain hydrocarbon formation, shifting the product slate toward lighter products.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory model, indicates the probability of chain propagation in hydrocarbon synthesis (0 < α < 1). Higher α values favor long-chain product formation.
- **Experimental Data:** In CO-FTS, the optimized Na₂S-modified catalyst achieves an α factor sufficient to yield C₄⁺ α-olefins selectivity up to 65.4%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Na₂S enhances α-olefin selectivity and chain growth in CO-FTS, while in CO₂-FTS, it suppresses chain growth due to altered iron phase composition.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in conversion over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO}_2\ \text{Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 catalyst demonstrates excellent stability, maintaining conversion and selectivity over 1000 hours with minimal deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Na₂S addition further improves stability in CO-FTS by suppressing carbon deposition, while hydrophobic modification aids stability under atmospheric pressure.

---
**Summary:**  
The 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 catalyst exhibits high CO₂ conversion and C₅⁺ selectivity with moderate CH₄ formation under CO₂-FTS conditions. Na₂S modification suppresses activity and shifts selectivity toward CO and CH₄, while in CO-FTS, it enhances long-chain α-olefin selectivity and catalyst stability. These trends are closely linked to iron phase evolution and promoter effects, as confirmed by comprehensive characterization and long-term testing.
