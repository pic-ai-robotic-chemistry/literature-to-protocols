## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe@Mn@xSi and Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalysts involves a sequence of well‐defined procedures that ensure reproducibility and clarity in catalyst preparation. The following outlines the step-by-step methodology as reported:

#### Hydrothermal Synthesis and Calcination of Fe₂O₃
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O dissolved in 110 mL of water, with sodium acetate trihydrate (pH buffer), hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (surfactant), and ethylenediamine (reducing agent).  
   - Equipment: Standard laboratory glassware with stirring.  
2. • Hydrothermal Treatment  
   - Conditions: Heating at 200 °C for 10 hours in a hydrothermal reactor.  
3. • Drying and Calcination  
   - Drying: The washed precipitate is dried at 100 °C.  
   - Calcination: Performed at 500 °C for 5 hours in a furnace to enhance crystallinity and stabilize the Fe₂O₃ phase.

#### Mn Deposition via Precipitation–Deposition
1. • Precursor Mixing (for Mn deposition)  
   - The calcined Fe₂O₃ (typically 0.5 g) is dispersed in an appropriate volume of ethanol.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Reagent: A KMnO₄ aqueous solution (0.078 g in solution) is added dropwise using a peristaltic pump.  
   - Conditions: Stirring at 30 °C for 2 hours to facilitate uniform Mn deposition.
3. • Drying  
   - The deposited product is dried at 100 °C for 12 hours, preparing it for further treatments.

#### SiO₂ Coating and Hydrophobic Modification
1. • SiO₂ Deposition by Modified Stöber Method  
   - Process: Disperse the Fe@Mn catalyst in ethanol (e.g., 150 mL) via ultrasonication.  
   - Add a specified volume of TEOS (x = 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL) and stir at 30 °C.  
   - Then, add 2.5 mL of ammonia solution (25–28%) and 10 mL water; continue stirring for 4 additional hours.  
   - Following reaction, centrifuge, wash with ethanol, and dry.
2. • Hydrophobic Modification (for Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalysts)  
   - Post‑treatment: Subject the SiO₂‑coated catalyst to a silanization treatment by adding chlorotrimethylsilane in n‑hexane with ultrasonic assistance to tailor surface hydrophobicity.

Each procedural block includes precisely measured reagents, controlled temperatures, and specified reaction durations, ensuring that every synthesis step—from precursor mixing, precipitation–deposition, drying, and calcination to post‑processing—is meticulously documented for reproducibility and scalability.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

The following sections detail the catalyst‑specific procedures for reagent preparation of the Fe@Mn@xSi and Fe@Mn@xSi-c systems. Each catalyst undergoes precise weighing of solids, controlled liquid transfer, and standardized mixing to assure full reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Catalyst Fe@Mn@xSi
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O**: Weighed 7.5 g.
- **Sodium Acetate Trihydrate**: Weighed 12.4 g.
- **Hexadecyl Trimethyl Ammonium Bromide**: Weighed 3.8 g.

##### 3.1.1.2 Catalyst Fe@Mn@xSi-c
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O**: Weighed 7.5 g.
- **Sodium Acetate Trihydrate**: Weighed 12.4 g.
- **Hexadecyl Trimethyl Ammonium Bromide**: Weighed 3.8 g.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Catalyst Fe@Mn@xSi
- **Ethylenediamine**: Poured 26 mL into the stirred solution following initial reagent dissolution.
- **Aqueous KMnO₄ solution**: Added 5 mL dropwise using a peristaltic pump to facilitate deposition–precipitation for Mn introduction.
- **Tetraethyl Orthosilicate (TEOS)**: Transferred (e.g., 0.2 mL as a representative dosage from a series [0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.8 mL]) into the ethanol dispersion after initial stirring.
- **Ammonia solution (25–28%)**: Added 2.5 mL into the mixture to initiate SiO₂ coating.

##### 3.1.2.2 Catalyst Fe@Mn@xSi-c
- **Tetraethyl Orthosilicate (TEOS)**: Transferred in the same manner and dosage as for Fe@Mn@xSi.
- **n‑Hexane**: Poured 70 mL into the dried catalyst precursor to prepare for hydrophobic modification.
- **Chlorotrimethylsilane**: Transferred 0.5 mL into the n‑hexane dispersion to enable surface silanization.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Catalyst Fe@Mn@xSi
- **Core and promoter solution**: Mixed by stirring at 30 °C for 3 hours to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneous precursor formation.
- **Post‑TEOS addition mixture**: Continued stirring at 30 °C for an additional 4 hours, ensuring thorough incorporation of the SiO₂ precursor and promoter.

##### 3.1.3.2 Catalyst Fe@Mn@xSi-c
- **SiO₂‑coated precursor**: Stirred at 30 °C for 4 hours after the ammonia and water addition to achieve uniform shell deposition.
- **Hydrophobic modification mixture**: Following the addition of n‑hexane and chlorotrimethylsilane, the mixture was ultrasonically treated at room temperature for 1 hour to enhance surface modification.

These carefully defined steps—including the sequence of solid weighing, liquid transfer by specified methods and volumes, and controlled mixing conditions (stirring speeds, durations, and ultrasonic treatment)—are essential for achieving reproducibility in catalyst synthesis and ensuring that both hydrophilic and hydrophobic versions of the iron‑based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts are prepared with consistent quality.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Mn@xSi

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Synthesis of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles
- **Reagents**: 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 12.4 g sodium acetate trihydrate, 3.8 g hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide, and 26 mL ethylenediamine dissolved in 110 mL deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Stir the mixture for 0.5 hours at room temperature; perform hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 10 hours; subsequently calcine at 500 °C for 5 hours in air.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve all precursors to obtain a homogeneous solution.
  - Transfer to a sealed reactor and conduct the hydrothermal reaction.
  - Recover the product, then calcine to form crystalline Fe₂O₃.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and control of temperature during hydrothermal treatment to avoid incomplete precipitation.

##### Step 2: Promoter Deposition – Mn Addition via Deposition–Precipitation
- **Reagents**: 0.5 g of the synthesized Fe₂O₃; 5 mL aqueous solution containing 0.078 g KMnO₄; and 300 mL ethanol.
- **Conditions**: Stir at 30 °C for 2 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Disperse Fe₂O₃ in ethanol.
  - Add KMnO₄ solution dropwise under continuous stirring.
  - Dry the resultant product at 100 °C for 12 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform dropwise addition and steady stirring ensure even Mn deposition.

##### Step 3: Catalyst Assembly – SiO₂ Shell Coating
- **Reagents**: Varying dosages of TEOS (0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL), 2.5 mL ammonia solution (25–28%), and 10 mL water in ethanol.
- **Conditions**: Stir at 30 °C for 3 hours after TEOS addition, followed by an additional 4 hours after adding ammonia and water.
- **Process**:
  - Disperse Fe@Mn in ethanol, add TEOS, and ensure controlled SiO₂ deposition.
  - After coating, dry in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for 10 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Precise TEOS dosage is essential to control the shell thickness and maintain catalyst activity.

##### Step 4: Activation – Reduction and Carburization
- **Conditions**: Reduce in H₂ at 350 °C for 20 hours; follow with carburization at 320 °C for 20 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Controlled temperature staging maintains conversion of Fe₂O₃ to active iron carbide phases, critical for reproducibility.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe@Mn@xSi-c

##### Step 5: Surface Hydrophobization via Silanization
- **Reagents**: Treated hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi, chlorotrimethylsilane, and n-hexane.
- **Process**:
  - Disperse the dried SiO₂-coated catalyst in n-hexane.
  - Add chlorotrimethylsilane under ultrasonic treatment to achieve silanization.
- **Key Steps**: This modification inhibits water-induced oxidation, preserving the active iron carbide phase and enhancing catalyst stability.

This step-by-step workflow—from raw precursor synthesis to final activation—ensures precise control of pH, temperature, and reagent addition, thereby enabling reproducible preparation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic iron-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to determine the catalyst’s crystal phases and track phase evolution during reduction/carburization. The catalyst was first reduced in pure H₂ at 350 °C for 20 hours, then carbonized in a mixture of 31.6% CO and 68.4% N₂ at 320 °C for 20 hours, and subsequently exposed to a CO + H₂O atmosphere while collecting in situ XRD patterns.
- **Instruments and Models**: Ultima IV‑185 diffractometer (for in situ analysis) and XPert Pro (for ex situ analysis).
- **Characterization Conditions**: For XPert Pro, Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA; 2θ scan range of 25°–65° with a step size of 0.02° and scanning rate of 10°/min. In situ analysis was conducted in a 2θ range of 29°–79°.
- **Test Parameters**: Precise temperature settings during pre-treatment and gas compositions as noted.
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment is critical to avoid diffraction errors.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: A quartz tube reactor was used to assess the reduction behavior. The 50 mg catalyst sample was pre-treated in N₂ at 350 °C for 1 hour, cooled to 80 °C, and then exposed to a gas mixture of 5% H₂/95% N₂ while ramping to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micro fixed‐bed reactor coupled with a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Characterization Conditions and Test Parameters**: Gas flow rate of 30 mL/min.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and controlled heating ensure reliable reduction profiles.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Following a similar pre-treatment as in H₂‑TPR (N₂ at 350 °C for 1 hour, cooling to 80 °C), the sample was exposed to 5% CO/95% N₂ and heated to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Instruments and Models**: DAS‑7000 chemical adsorption instrument.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Flow rate maintained at 30 mL/min.
- **Key Notes**: Direct comparison with H₂‑TPR highlights differences in reduction behavior.

### 4.4 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS)
- **Operational Description**: The catalyst powder was dispersed on an appropriate substrate without additional tablet formation to examine morphology and elemental distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Talos F200x.
- **Key Notes**: Ensuring minimal sample agglomeration aids in accurate mapping.

### 4.5 Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS)/FTIR
- **Operational Description**: The powder sample, placed in an infrared cell, was first pre-treated in Ar at 320 °C for 120 minutes, then exposed to CO at the same temperature, followed by an Ar purge.
- **Instruments and Models**: NICOLET iS50 FTIR Spectrometer.
- **Key Notes**: The sequential atmospheric changes allow insight into surface chemical interactions.

### 4.6 Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) Analysis
- **Operational Description**: ICP was utilized for elemental quantification of catalyst components.
- **Instruments and Models**: Agilent 5110.
- **Key Notes**: Accurate elemental analysis supports catalyst composition verification.

### 4.7 Water‑droplet Contact Angle Measurements
- **Operational Description**: Surface hydrophobicity was assessed by measuring the contact angle using a controlled water droplet.
- **Instruments and Models**: Kruss DSA100.
- **Key Notes**: This measurement corroborates the intended surface modification effects.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for effecting complete reduction of the iron oxide precursor and for initiating carburization to form the active iron carbide phase.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are initially subjected to a reduction step in pure hydrogen (H₂) at 350 °C. This is followed by a second activation step using syngas at 320 °C.  
- **Observations:** The well-calibrated temperatures ensure sufficient thermal energy to transform Fe₂O₃ into metallic iron and then into catalytic carbide phases while minimizing the risk of sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
- **Definition:** Pressure during activation influences gas–catalyst interactions and reduction kinetics.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Both the hydrogen reduction and the subsequent syngas activation are performed at 0.1 MPa.  
- **Observations:** The controlled low pressure allows a gentle yet effective reduction process, ensuring that the formation of active phases occurs without aggressive conditions that might lead to particle agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
- **Definition:** The activation gas composition is essential for determining the chemical environment during reduction and carburization.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** In the first stage, a pure H₂ atmosphere is used to reduce the iron oxide to metallic iron. In the second stage, the catalyst is exposed to syngas—comprising H₂ and CO at a H₂/CO ratio of 2—to facilitate carburization.  
- **Observations:** This sequential gas introduction not only ensures effective reduction but also promotes the formation of desired iron carbide phases, as evidenced by improved catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** The space‑time velocity controls the gas flow per unit catalyst weight, influencing the extent of catalyst exposure to the reactive gases.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Although a flow rate of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·gcat⁻¹ is specified for the catalytic reaction step, no explicit flow rate is provided for the activation process.  
- **Observations:** The reactor setup ensures uniform gas distribution, thereby effectively supporting the activation process.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
- **Definition:** Adequate activation time is essential to drive complete phase transformation while preventing excessive sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts undergo a 20-hour reduction in pure H₂, followed by 5 hours of activation in syngas.  
- **Observations:** This duration is optimized to ensure full conversion of the precursor phases into the active carbide forms, as confirmed by the improved phase evolution and catalyst stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is crucial for achieving optimal kinetics and maintaining the active iron carbide phase during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic reaction was performed at 320 °C.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 320 °C facilitates effective CO conversion and stabilization of the iron carbide species, especially in hydrophobic catalysts where the phase is preserved without oxidation to Fe₃O₄.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the syngas concentration and overall reaction rate, thereby affecting product distribution.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were carried out at a pressure of 2.0 MPa using a stainless fixed-bed reactor.  
- **Observations:** This elevated pressure improves reaction kinetics, promoting higher conversion rates of CO to hydrocarbons. It also supports the maintenance of the active phase, contributing to enhanced catalytic performance.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is a critical parameter that determines the balance between hydrogenation reactions and chain growth during synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A H₂/CO ratio of 2 was maintained throughout the experiments.  
- **Observations:** This specific ratio optimizes the syngas conversion process, ensuring sufficient hydrogen availability for hydrocarbon formation. However, an increased hydrogen concentration would favor methane production over olefins, a trend carefully managed under these controlled conditions.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, expressed as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), defines the contact time between the reactants and the catalyst.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A GHSV of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹ was used during the reaction tests.  
- **Observations:** The relatively high GHSV indicates a shorter residence time, which can influence the selectivity of the catalyst. The controlled flow rate, in combination with the established temperature and pressure, was instrumental in preserving the active iron carbide phase, particularly in hydrophobic catalysts, thus ensuring long-term stability and reproducibility of results.

Overall, these parameters collectively ensure that the Fischer–Tropsch reaction proceeds under conditions that maximize catalytic performance and product selectivity, while maintaining high reproducibility across extended reaction periods.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and their evolution were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), including in situ XRD, to monitor real-time changes under reduction, carburization, and reaction conditions. This analysis is critical for identifying active phases and understanding catalyst stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that the as-prepared Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles are transformed into metallic iron and subsequently into iron carbide phases (χ-Fe₅C₂ and θ-Fe₃C) after reduction and carburization. In situ XRD demonstrated that, under syngas conditions, hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalysts undergo partial oxidation of iron carbide to Fe₃O₄, as indicated by the emergence of Fe₃O₄ peaks (35.4° 2θ) and the attenuation of Fe₅C₂ peaks (44.2° 2θ). In contrast, hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalysts maintain strong iron carbide peaks with no detectable Fe₃O₄ formation.
- **Observations:** The retention of iron carbide phases in hydrophobic catalysts correlates with enhanced catalytic stability and selectivity, while the formation of Fe₃O₄ in hydrophilic catalysts is associated with deactivation and increased methane and CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Phase Composition by Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to quantitatively determine the distribution of iron species after reaction, providing complementary information to XRD regarding the chemical environment and phase composition.
- **Findings:** For the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst, Mössbauer spectra indicated a mixture of 59.7% χ-Fe₅C₂, 34.3% Fe₃O₄, and 6.0% Fe³⁺(spm). The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst exhibited 74.1% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ-Fe₃C, with no Fe₃O₄ detected.
- **Observations:** The data confirm that surface hydrophobization effectively inhibits water-induced oxidation of iron carbide, preserving the active phase and supporting long-term catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping were used to examine particle morphology and the spatial distribution of Fe, Mn, and Si within the catalysts.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed that Fe@Mn nanoparticles are uniformly coated with an amorphous SiO₂ shell. EDS mapping confirmed homogeneous distribution of Fe (core), Mn (promoter), and Si (shell) in both hydrophilic and hydrophobic catalysts. The shell thickness was tunable via TEOS dosage, and hydrophobic modification did not disrupt the core–shell architecture.
- **Observations:** The uniform coating and elemental distribution are essential for controlling phase evolution and ensuring effective protection against water-induced deactivation.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction and Carburization Behavior
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and CO temperature-programmed reduction (CO-TPR) were conducted to assess the reducibility and carburization of iron species, which are critical for activating the catalyst.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR profiles showed that increasing SiO₂ shell thickness shifted reduction peaks to higher temperatures and reduced peak areas, indicating decreased accessibility of internal iron species. CO-TPR experiments revealed that thicker shells also hindered carburization, as evidenced by diminished CO consumption.
- **Observations:** These results highlight the importance of shell thickness and surface properties in modulating the activation and stability of the catalyst.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemical Properties and Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS/FTIR) was used to probe CO adsorption and surface functional groups, while water contact angle measurements assessed surface hydrophobicity.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS spectra identified characteristic CO adsorption bands, confirming the presence of active surface sites. Contact angle measurements demonstrated a significant increase in hydrophobicity after silanization, with hydrophobic catalysts exhibiting much higher water contact angles compared to their hydrophilic counterparts.
- **Observations:** Enhanced hydrophobicity effectively suppresses water adsorption, thereby stabilizing the iron carbide phase and improving catalyst performance under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated on a carbon-atom basis as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (320 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst achieved a CO conversion of approximately 30% in the CO + H₂O model experiment. In contrast, the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst exhibited a markedly lower CO conversion (<2%) under the same conditions in the presence of H₂O, indicating strong resistance to water-induced oxidation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic surface modification effectively inhibits water access and preserves the active iron carbide phase, resulting in stable CO conversion during long-term operation. Increasing SiO₂ shell thickness in hydrophilic catalysts reduces CO conversion due to restricted reactant accessibility.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water-gas shift (WGS) side reaction:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst exhibited a CO₂ selectivity of 28.9%, while the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst showed a significantly lower value of 7.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophilic catalysts facilitate H₂O diffusion, promoting the WGS reaction and increasing CO₂ formation. Hydrophobic modification suppresses this pathway, resulting in lower CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated on a carbon-atom basis:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst produced CH₄ with a selectivity of 21.5%, whereas the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst achieved a lower CH₄ selectivity of 14.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic surface treatment reduces methane formation by stabilizing the iron carbide phase and enhancing C–C coupling, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity denotes the proportion of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms, determined from product distribution by gas chromatography.
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst demonstrated a higher selectivity for long-chain olefins (C₃⁺), with olefin selectivity reaching 64.3%, compared to 49.8% for the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic modification enhances C₃⁺ hydrocarbon formation, while increasing SiO₂ shell thickness in hydrophilic catalysts shifts the product distribution toward lighter hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination, influencing the distribution of hydrocarbon products. Higher α values indicate a preference for longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** While specific α values are not numerically reported, the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst exhibits a higher α factor, as inferred from increased C₃⁺ selectivity and reduced CH₄ formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Stabilization of the iron carbide phase by hydrophobic modification correlates with enhanced chain growth, as evidenced by the product distribution.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over time. The deactivation rate is typically expressed as the decline in CO conversion per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst maintained stable performance over 110 hours on stream, with negligible loss in CO conversion or selectivity. The hydrophilic catalyst showed more rapid deactivation due to phase oxidation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic surface modification significantly improves catalyst longevity by preventing water-induced oxidation of the active phase, ensuring sustained activity and selectivity during prolonged operation.
