## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis for the Fe–Zn based systems (e.g., 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 and NaS‐Fe1Zn1) is executed with precise protocols to ensure reproducibility and effective performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. Two primary methods are employed: the co‑precipitation method and the impregnation method, with an additional hydrophobic modification step when required.

#### Co‑precipitation Method
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃⋅9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂⋅6H₂O are dissolved in 500 mL deionized water (total molar amount 0.18 mol, with Fe:Zn ratios such as 4:1, 2:1, or 1:1).  
   • pH Control: Ammonia is added gradually to adjust the pH to 9, monitored using a pH meter.  
   • Observations: The solution forms a homogeneous suspension with initial precipitate development.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   • A support precursor is added dropwise and stirred for 1 hour to ensure uniform deposition, enhancing the metal oxide distribution.
  
3. Drying  
   • Conditions: The suspension is dried at 100 °C for 12 hours to remove moisture.

4. Calcination  
   • Parameters: The dried precursor is calcined at 400 °C for 4 hours in air, solidifying the formation of the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel structure.

5. Post‑Treatment  
   • A reduction step in a flowing H₂ atmosphere is carried out at 450 °C and 0.2 MPa for 5 hours, followed by passivation (1% O₂/99% N₂) at room temperature for 2 hours to stabilize the metal phases.

#### Impregnation Method
1. Catalyst Support Soaking  
   • Dried FeZn powders are subjected to incipient wetness impregnation by soaking in a Na₂CO₃ solution to introduce Na uniformly.

2. Drying and Calcination  
   • The impregnated material is dried at 80 °C overnight and then calcined at around 400 °C for 3–4 hours.

#### Additional Hydrophobic Modification
Conducted via autoclave synthesis with SiO₂@C16, this step is performed at 70 °C for 72 hours, followed by drying and calcination, to enhance water management on the catalyst surface.

This structured approach, integrating precise reagent concentrations, controlled pH, and sequential processing steps, ensures that the synthesis is both clear and reproducible—facilitating robust catalytic performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the step‐by‐step reagent preparation for the two catalyst systems—the 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 catalyst and the NaS‐Fe1Zn1 catalyst—to ensure full reproducibility and proper grouping of operations.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed to contribute to a total molar amount of 0.18 mol for the synthesis, using an analytical balance.
- **Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed in proportion to the iron nitrate, ensuring the correct Fe:Zn ratio.
- **Na2CO3**: Weighed according to the target loading for the sodium promoter (approximately 1.5 wt% Na) to be used in the subsequent incipient wetness impregnation step.

##### 3.1.1.2 NaS‐Fe1Zn1
- **Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O**: Weighed under the same conditions as for the 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 catalyst.
- **Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O**: Weighed to maintain the required stoichiometry with the iron precursor.
- **Na₂S**: Weighed carefully to achieve the desired sulfur doping level (e.g., around 0.1 wt% S) in the promoter package.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1
- **Deionized Water**: Poured 500 mL into a beaker using a graduated cylinder to dissolve the metal nitrates.
- **Na2CO3 Solution**: Transferred via pipetting into the dried FeZn powder during the incipient wetness impregnation step, ensuring controlled addition.

##### 3.1.2.2 NaS‐Fe1Zn1
- **Deionized Water**: Poured 500 mL into the reaction vessel, ensuring complete dissolution of the metal precursors.
- **Na2S Solution**: Poured gently into the metal nitrate solution using standard laboratory glassware to reach the specified sulfur content.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1
- **Metal Salt Solution**: Mixed at 700 rpm for 30 minutes at room temperature to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneity.
- **Post-Ammonia Addition Mixture**: Stirred for an additional 1 hour after the addition of ammonia (to adjust pH to ~9), ensuring optimal co‐precipitation.

##### 3.1.3.2 NaS‐Fe1Zn1
- **Metal Salt and Na₂S Mixture**: Mixed at 700 rpm for 30 minutes at room temperature and then aged under static conditions for 2 hours to facilitate uniform incorporation of the sulfur promoter.

This systematic approach—detailing the weighing of solid precursors, precise liquid transfer, and rigorous mixing conditions—ensures that each catalyst’s preparation steps are clearly defined and reproducible, aligning with the objectives and detailed QA information provided in the study.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (total 0.18 mol with Fe:Zn ratios of 4:1, 2:1, or 1:1) in 500 mL deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Stir the solution at 700 rpm for 30 minutes. Slowly add ammonia to reach a pH of 9.
- **Process**:
  • Ensure complete dissolution and homogeneity before adding ammonia.  
  • After ammonia addition, allow the suspension to age for 2 hours for complete precipitation.
  • Filter and wash the precipitate with deionized water.
  • Dry the resulting FeZn powders at 100 °C for 12 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Constant stirring and controlled pH adjustment are critical for uniform precursor formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: Utilize the dried FeZn powders and prepare a Na₂CO₃ solution for incipient wetness impregnation.
- **Conditions**: Impregnate uniformly, then dry at 100 °C for 12 hours.
- **Process**:
  • Add the Na₂CO₃ solution dropwise to the powders ensuring total Na load matches the targeted composition.
  • Achieve even distribution of the promoter through adequate mixing.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Conditions**: Calcine at 400 °C for 4 hours in air with a controlled ramp rate.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor the temperature closely to ensure the complete formation of the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel structure.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Process**: Reduce the catalyst under a flowing H₂ atmosphere (40 mL/min) at 450 °C and 0.2 MPa for 5 hours, then allow cooling to 290 °C prior to use.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: NaS‐Fe1Zn1 Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Note**: Follow the identical procedure as for the 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 catalyst.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: In addition to Na₂CO₃, prepare a Na₂S solution (with doping levels in the range of 0.1–0.3 wt% S) for simultaneous impregnation.
- **Conditions & Process**:
  • Mix the dried FeZn powders with the combined Na₂CO₃/Na₂S solution ensuring even incorporation.  
  • Dry the impregnated powders at 100 °C for 12 hours before subjecting them to calcination.
  
##### Post-Processing: Hydrophobic Modification
- **Process**: Separately modify the catalyst with SiO₂@C16 via silanization.  
  • Conduct the reaction in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 70 °C for 72 hours, followed by drying and a short calcination step.
- **Key Steps**: Maintaining precursor ratios and controlled addition of promoters (Na₂CO₃ and Na₂S) are essential for reproducibility and desired iron phase distribution.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is performed as a pre‐reaction reduction step to convert the freshly prepared ZnFe₂O₄ spinel phase into active species comprising metallic Fe and ZnO. The procedure exclusively addresses the reduction process required for catalyst synthesis rather than any milder activation employed for characterization.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is crucial for driving the reduction of ZnFe₂O₄, where sufficient thermal energy facilitates the removal of oxygen, leading to the formation of metallic Fe that is prone to carburization into iron carbide phases during subsequent reactions.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced in a flowing hydrogen atmosphere at 450 °C.  
- **Observations:** This temperature ensures complete conversion of the precursor while favoring enhanced metal dispersion without triggering pronounced sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
- **Definition:** The pressure during activation governs the kinetics of hydrogen reduction and influences the interaction between the gas and catalyst surface.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed at 0.2 MPa.  
- **Observations:** Operating under 0.2 MPa provides a controlled environment for efficient reduction, which is critical for maintaining the catalyst’s structural integrity and dispersion.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
- **Definition:** The chosen gas, pure hydrogen, acts as a reducing agent by donating electrons necessary to remove oxygen from the metal oxides.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure H₂ is employed during the activation step.  
- **Observations:** H₂ ensures the systematic reduction of ZnFe₂O₄ to metallic Fe and ZnO, setting the stage for subsequent formation of the desired iron carbide phases.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity determines the volumetric flow of reducing gas per unit mass of catalyst, influencing the uniformity and rate of reduction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Based on the experimental setup (2 g catalyst with a 40 mL/min H₂ flow), the space-time velocity is calculated to be approximately 1,200 mL/(g_cat·h).  
- **Observations:** This flow rate facilitates efficient gas–solid interactions while minimizing the risk of incomplete activation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
- **Definition:** The duration of activation is critical to ensure complete phase transformation without overexposure that could lead to sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced for 5 hours.  
- **Observations:** A 5-hour activation period is sufficient to achieve a stable, well-dispersed active phase configuration, thereby enhancing long-term catalyst stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is defined as the operational heat that activates syngas molecules, facilitates CO dissociation, and promotes hydrogenation on the catalyst surface. In the reported experiments, the Fischer–Tropsch reaction was conducted at 320 °C. This temperature was selected because it significantly enhances CO conversion—with CO conversion reaching up to 96.9%—and facilitates the carburization of the iron phase, leading to the formation of active iron carbide species. The elevated temperature also increases the concentration of CHₓ* intermediates, thus favoring carbon chain growth without rapid catalyst deactivation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure, which influences the concentration of syngas and the adsorption of reactants on the catalyst surface, was maintained at 1 MPa under standard conditions. The study also included high-pressure tests at 2 MPa to observe its influence on catalytic performance. Under elevated pressures, a slow increase in CO selectivity was noted while overall catalytic activity showed a decline, possibly due to enhanced steam formation and its interaction with the catalyst surface. These observations underscore the delicate balance between pressure and catalyst stability in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio is critical for controlling hydrogenation and determining the hydrocarbon product distribution. For CO₂-FTS experiments, a ratio of approximately 3 was maintained to ensure effective CO₂ conversion and to enhance the generation of lower olefins. In contrast, during CO-FTS tests, a stoichiometric ratio of 1 was applied. This lower ratio helps to balance methane formation with olefin production, promoting the formation of long-chain C₄⁺ α-olefins while minimizing excessive hydrogenation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) determines the contact time between reactants and the catalyst and was set to 1200 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹. A lower GHSV, corresponding to a longer residence time, has been associated with higher overall conversion due to prolonged interaction with active sites. This standardized parameter aids in achieving reproducible results and ensures that the influence of temperature, pressure, and gas composition on product distribution is consistent across experiments.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase evolution of the catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy, providing insights into active phase formation and transformations under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** Fresh catalysts exhibit the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel phase (PDF#77–11), with a minor ZnO peak (PDF#79–208). After H₂ reduction, ZnFe₂O₄ is fully converted to metallic Fe⁰ (PDF#87–721) and ZnO, as evidenced by the disappearance of ZnFe₂O₄ peaks and the emergence of Fe⁰ peaks with higher intensity, indicating superior crystallinity. In spent catalysts, XRD reveals the presence of Fe₃O₄ and χ‐Fe₅C₂ for unmodified samples, while Na₂S-modified catalysts display Fe⁰, Fe₃C (PDF#23–1113), and χ‐Fe₅C₂, with Fe₃O₄ absent.
- **Observations:** The addition of Na₂S suppresses χ‐Fe₅C₂ formation and promotes Fe⁰ and Fe₃C phases, correlating with altered catalytic selectivity and stability.

#### 6.1.2 Iron Phase Distribution (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy quantifies the distribution of iron phases, crucial for understanding catalytic activity and deactivation pathways.
- **Findings:** After CO₂-FTS, the unmodified catalyst contains 32% Fe₃O₄ and 68% χ‐Fe₅C₂. With Na₂S addition, the phase composition shifts to 30.7% Fe⁰, 28% Fe₃C, and 41.3% χ‐Fe₅C₂, with Fe₃O₄ undetected.
- **Observations:** Na₂S addition inhibits both carburization and oxidation, reducing χ‐Fe₅C₂ and Fe₃O₄ content while increasing Fe⁰ and Fe₃C, which impacts product selectivity and catalyst longevity.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** BET surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were measured by N₂ physisorption (Micromeritics ASAP 2020), with pore size calculated via the BJH method.
- **Findings:** Zn addition increases surface area from 37 m²/g (1.5Na-Fe) to 47 m²/g (1.5Na-Fe1Zn1), enhancing dispersion. S addition does not significantly alter surface area. Pore volume and average pore size are determined at P/P₀ = 0.9997.
- **Observations:** Improved surface area and optimized pore structure facilitate reactant diffusion and catalyst stability, supporting higher activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** SEM (Hitachi Regulus 8100) and TEM (FEI TE20) analyses, complemented by EDX mapping, elucidate particle morphology, size, and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** SEM reveals block-like morphologies with dense protrusions. TEM shows large Fe nanoparticles (260–290 nm) with smaller ZnO nanoparticles (60–70 nm) attached, forming Fe@ZnO structures. In spent catalysts, carbon shells up to 28 nm thick are observed on unmodified samples, while S-modified catalysts exhibit more dispersed Fe and uniform C distribution.
- **Observations:** The Fe@ZnO configuration and reduced carbon deposition in S-modified catalysts are linked to enhanced stability and altered deactivation behavior.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Raman, FTIR, and contact angle measurements assess surface carbon species and hydrophobic modification effects.
- **Findings:** Raman spectra show increased D-band intensity after long-term reaction, indicating more defective carbon. Na₂S addition reduces carbon deposition. Contact angle increases from 27.5° (hydrophilic) to 125.5° (hydrophobic) after SiO₂@C16 modification, confirming successful hydrophobic treatment.
- **Observations:** Hydrophobic modification accelerates water desorption, mitigating H₂O accumulation and improving catalyst durability, especially under atmospheric-pressure CO₂-FTS.

#### 6.1.6 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** ICP-OES provides quantitative analysis of Fe, Zn, Na, and S content in fresh and spent catalysts.
- **Findings:** Elemental compositions align with theoretical loadings, confirming successful promoter incorporation and retention after reaction.
- **Observations:** Elemental data support phase analysis and explain performance trends observed with varying promoter levels.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under CO-FTS conditions, the 1.5Na0.1S-Fe1Zn1 catalyst exhibited a CO conversion increasing from 24.0% at 280 °C to 96.9% at 320 °C. The addition of Na₂S maintained high CO conversion (~96%) under optimal conditions. In CO₂-FTS, Na-containing catalysts achieved CO₂ conversion around 39%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Na₂S addition enhances CO conversion stability in CO-FTS, while in CO₂-FTS, it suppresses activity. Higher reaction temperatures and optimized promoter levels favor increased conversion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For Na-promoted catalysts, CO₂ selectivity remains low due to suppressed WGS activity. Upon catalyst deactivation, CO₂ selectivity increases. Na₂S addition does not significantly affect CO₂ selectivity in CO-FTS but increases it during deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Stable catalysts maintain low CO₂ selectivity, while increased CO₂ selectivity signals deactivation or enhanced WGS.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity increases with higher H₂/CO ratios and pressure. For 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1, CH₄ selectivity is minimized by Na and Zn promoters but rises with catalyst deactivation or higher water partial pressure. Na₂S addition does not significantly alter CH₄ selectivity in CO-FTS but increases it in CO₂-FTS.
- **Observations and Trends:** Effective promoter combinations suppress methane formation, while deactivation or water accumulation increases CH₄ selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ (Long-Chain Hydrocarbon) Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Na and Zn promoters increase C₅⁺ selectivity and stability. In CO-FTS, Na₂S addition enhances long-chain α-olefins (C₄⁺) selectivity up to ~65.4%. In CO₂-FTS, Na₂S shifts selectivity toward light alkanes and CO, reducing C₅⁺ and olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter tuning enables control over hydrocarbon distribution, with Na₂S favoring long-chain olefins in CO-FTS but suppressing them in CO₂-FTS.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination, influencing product distribution according to the Anderson-Schulz-Flory (ASF) law. Values range from 0 (only methane) to 1 (very long chains).
- **Experimental Data:** Na‐Fe1Zn1 catalysts exhibit higher α values, favoring long-chain hydrocarbons. Na₂S addition decreases α in CO₂-FTS (favoring light alkanes) but maintains or slightly increases α in CO-FTS, enhancing lower olefins selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Adjusting promoter levels modulates α, enabling tailored product distributions.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring conversion decline over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 catalyst maintained stable CO₂ conversion and selectivity over 1000 h, with iron phase stability (∼70% χ‐Fe₅C₂). Na₂S addition in CO-FTS suppressed carbon deposition, further enhancing stability. Hydrophobic SiO₂@C16 support improved water management and durability, especially under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalyst longevity is linked to iron phase stability, minimized carbon deposition, and effective water removal.
