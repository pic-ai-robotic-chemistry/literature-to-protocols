## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the two main synthesis routes employed for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts: the MOF‑derived (hydrothermal) method for Fe@C catalysts and the continuous co‑precipitation approach for Unsupported‑Fe. Both techniques are outlined with step‐by‐step experimental procedures to ensure reproducibility.

#### Hydrothermal Method for Fe@C Catalysts
• **Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques:**  
  - The hydrothermal method utilizes a MIL‑101(Fe) precursor synthesized from terephthalic acid, Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, nitric acid, and DMF in a Teflon‑lined autoclave. This method enables controlled formation of an iron carbide core (h‑Fe₃C) enveloped by a carbon shell whose structure varies with the pyrolysis temperature.  

• **Step-by-Step Experimental Procedures:**
  1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation:**  
     - Mix 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 ml nitric acid, and 70 ml DMF with stirring to obtain a homogeneous solution.
  2. **Hydrothermal Treatment:**  
     - Transfer the solution into a Teflon‑lined autoclave and heat at 120 °C for 48 h.
  3. **Washing and Drying:**  
     - Wash the resultant MIL‑101(Fe) and dry at 80 °C (overnight).
  4. **Pyrolysis:**  
     - Pyrolyze the dried precursor in a 2% C₂H₂/He flow with a heating rate of 5 °C/min for 1 h at target temperatures (500 °C for Fe@C‑500, 600 °C for Fe@C‑600, and 700 °C for Fe@C‑700).  
  5. **Precipitation–Deposition (When Applicable):**  
     - For a deposition-based modification, add a support precursor dropwise and stir for 1 h prior to drying and calcination.
  6. **Post‑Treatment:**  
     - For Fe@C‑600‑H₂, conduct an additional reduction under H₂ (50 ml/min) at 400 °C for 24 h, converting h‑Fe₃C to α‑Fe while preserving the carbon shell.

• **Observations & Equipment:**  
  - Reactor: Teflon‑lined autoclave for hydrothermal synthesis.  
  - Notable phenomena include the formation of an iron carbide core and distinct carbon shells (amorphous at lower temperatures; graphene or graphite at higher temperatures).

#### Co‑precipitation Method for Unsupported‑Fe
• **Overview and Comparative Analysis:**  
  - This simplified technique involves the continuous addition of an aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ solution with ammonia (adjusted to pH 8.2–8.5) leading to uniform iron precipitation.
  
• **Step-by-Step Experimental Procedures:**
  1. **Precursor Mixing and Precipitation:**  
     - Mix Fe(NO₃)₃ and adjust pH using a 3 mol/L ammonia solution until reaching pH 8.2–8.5, enabling precipitation–deposition.
  2. **Drying:**  
     - Dry the precipitate at 100 °C for 6 h.
  3. **Calcination:**  
     - Calcine at 500 °C for 5 h in static air.
  4. **Reduction:**  
     - Reduce the calcined sample in H₂ (100 ml/min) at 350 °C for 24 h to form α‑Fe.

Together, these detailed procedures provide a comprehensive and reproducible approach to synthesizing Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, with clear guidance on reagent concentrations, reaction conditions, equipment, and post‑treatment steps.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@C Series (Fe@C-500, Fe@C-600, Fe@C-700, and Fe@C-600-H₂)

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation – MIL-101(Fe)
- **Reagents**: 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 mL nitric acid, and 70 mL DMF.
- **Conditions**: Stir the mixture for 2 hours at room temperature; transfer to a Teflon-lined autoclave; perform hydrothermal synthesis at 120 °C for 48 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the reagents in DMF to obtain a homogeneous solution.
  - Seal the solution in the autoclave and heat under controlled conditions.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and maintain precise hydrothermal parameters to guarantee reproducibility.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly & Pyrolysis
- **Reagents**: MIL-101(Fe) precursor.
- **Conditions**: Utilize a 2% C₂H₂/He gas mixture; heat with a ramp rate of 5 °C/min until reaching the target temperature; hold for 1 hour.
- **Process**:
  - For Fe@C-500: Pyrolyze at 500 °C to yield an amorphous carbon shell.
  - For Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂: Pyrolyze at 600 °C to form a 3–5 layer graphene shell.
  - For Fe@C-700: Pyrolyze at 700 °C to develop a well-oriented graphite shell.
- **Key Steps**: Strictly control the ramp rate and final temperature, as these influence both the iron phase (predominantly h‑Fe₃C) and the carbon shell’s morphology.

##### Step [3]: Catalyst Activation (Fe@C-600-H₂ only)
- **Reagents**: Fe@C-600 sample.
- **Conditions**: Reduce under a hydrogen atmosphere (50 mL/min) at 400 °C for 24 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Place the Fe@C-600 catalyst into the reduction furnace.
  - Expose it to H₂ to convert h‑Fe₃C partially into α‑Fe while largely preserving the carbon framework.
- **Key Steps**: Monitor reduction conditions closely to prevent carbon shell degradation.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Unsupported-Fe

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation – Co-precipitation
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃ aqueous solution (1 mol/L) and ammonia solution (3 mol/L).
- **Conditions**: Stir at 80 ± 1 °C while adding ammonia dropwise, maintaining pH at 8.2–8.5; age for 2 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Constant pH control is crucial for uniform precipitation.

##### Step [2]: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Process**:
  - Wash and filter the precipitate repeatedly with distilled water.
  - Dry for 12 hours at 120 °C.
  - Calcine in static air at 500 °C for 5 hours, then reduce in H₂ (100 mL/min) at 350 °C for 24 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough washing and precise thermal treatment to achieve the desired active iron phase.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pretreatment step performed during catalyst synthesis to ensure that the optimal active phase is generated before the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. In this work, two types of catalysts undergo hydrogen reduction under controlled conditions to achieve full phase transformation while preserving the protective carbon shell.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
Activation temperature plays a central role in driving the chemical conversion of precursor phases.  
• For the Fe@C-600-H₂ catalyst, the hydrogen reduction is performed at 400 °C. This temperature is specifically chosen to fully convert the pyrolyzed h‑Fe₃C phase into the more catalytically active α‑Fe phase.  
• In contrast, the Unsupported‑Fe catalyst employs a milder hydrogen reduction at 350 °C. The lower temperature here is intended to provide gentle conditions that facilitate reduction while minimizing potential sintering or over-reduction effects.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time
The duration of the activation process is essential to ensure complete transformation of the precursor.  
• Both catalysts are subjected to an extended hydrogen reduction for 24 hours.  
This prolonged treatment is necessary to allow sufficient interaction between hydrogen and the catalyst particles, ensuring that phase conversion is uniform and stable over time, which is critical for maintaining catalyst performance during reaction conditions.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas and Flow Rate
The choice and control of the activating gas directly affect the reduction efficiency.  
• Hydrogen is used as the activation gas in all cases, providing a reducing environment that enables the effective removal of oxygen from the catalyst precursor.  
• For the Fe@C-600-H₂ catalyst, a controlled hydrogen flow rate of 50 ml/min is adopted. This specific flow rate is optimized to offer a steady reduction environment that promotes thorough phase conversion without disturbing the encapsulating carbon shell.  
• The Unsupported‑Fe catalyst is also reduced in hydrogen, albeit with a higher flow rate to suit its different physical characteristics.

#### 5.1.4 Catalyst Phase Transformation
By carefully controlling activation temperature, time, and hydrogen flow rate, the process effectively converts h‑Fe₃C into α‑Fe. This phase conversion is crucial, as it directly influences the catalyst’s CO conversion efficiency and iron time yield during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, while the intact carbon shell prevents particle sintering and aggregation.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is critical for activating syngas and dictating catalyst dynamics. In this study, the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis was conducted at a fixed reactor temperature of 300 °C. At this temperature, the h‑Fe₃C phase within the Fe@C catalysts exhibits high activity, contributing to elevated CO conversion rates and a favorable long-chain hydrocarbon (C₅⁺) distribution. The stability of the catalyst is maintained under these conditions, with minimal deactivation observed over extended time-on-stream periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The system pressure directly influences the concentration of reactants in the reactor and thereby affects the kinetics of syngas conversion. Here, a pressure of 2.0 MPa was maintained in the fixed-bed reactor. This elevated pressure enhances the overall reaction rate by ensuring an increased presence of reactant molecules, which is crucial for achieving high conversion efficiency and steering the product selectivity towards longer hydrocarbon chains.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio plays a defining role in the product spectrum of Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. A stoichiometric ratio of 1.0 was employed, which balances the hydrogen and carbon monoxide feed to optimize the formation of desired hydrocarbons. This ratio is particularly important as it mitigates excessive methane formation while promoting the assembly of long-chain molecules, as evidenced by the superior selectivity seen in the Fe@C catalysts.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
Space-time velocity, expressed as weight hourly space velocity (WHSV), determines the residence time of syngas over the catalyst. Two distinct values were used: 8000 ml/(gcat·h) for the MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts and 3000 ml/(gcat·h) for the Unsupported‑Fe catalyst. The higher WHSV for MOF-derived catalysts promotes rapid syngas flow, enhancing reaction kinetics and maintaining high CO conversion, while the lower value for Unsupported‑Fe provides longer contact times, albeit with comparatively different conversion efficiencies.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 diffractometer with Co Kα radiation (λ = 0.179 nm), operated at 35 kV and 40 mA, with a scan step of 0.03° over a 2θ range of 10–90°. This enabled precise identification of FeO, h‑Fe₃C, α‑Fe, and graphite-carbon phases.
- **Findings:** Fe@C-500 exhibited FeO diffraction lines, Fe@C-600 showed h‑Fe₃C, graphite-carbon, and α‑Fe, while Fe@C-700 displayed dominant h‑Fe₃C and graphite-carbon peaks. The sharpening and narrowing of diffraction lines at higher pyrolysis temperatures indicated increased crystallinity and graphitization. Mössbauer spectroscopy further quantified the iron phases, revealing up to 95.5% h‑Fe₃C in Fe@C-700.
- **Observations:** The transition from FeO to h‑Fe₃C and α‑Fe, along with the evolution of the carbon shell from amorphous to graphitic, was directly correlated with increasing pyrolysis temperature. The dominance of h‑Fe₃C at higher temperatures is associated with enhanced Fischer–Tropsch activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Core–Shell Structure
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM/HRTEM) and elemental mapping were employed to visualize the core–shell architecture, particle size, and carbon shell morphology. TEM was performed on an FEI Talos 200A at 200 kV.
- **Findings:** HRTEM images revealed that Fe@C-500 possessed an amorphous carbon shell, Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ featured concentric graphene shells (3–5 layers), and Fe@C-700 exhibited a well-oriented graphite shell. Elemental mapping confirmed iron nanoparticles encapsulated within carbon shells. The average particle size for Fe@C-600 was 6.5 nm.
- **Observations:** The carbon shell structure evolved from amorphous to graphene to graphite with increasing pyrolysis temperature, providing enhanced protection against sintering and oxidation. The preservation of the graphene shell after H₂ reduction (Fe@C-600-H₂) was confirmed by HRTEM.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET) analysis measured specific surface area (SBET), total pore volume (Vtot), and average pore size. Measurements were conducted at −196 °C after vacuum evacuation (160 °C for MIL-101(Fe), 300 °C for catalysts).
- **Findings:** MIL-101(Fe) exhibited SBET of 1632 m²/g, Vtot of 0.83 cm³/g, and average pore size of 1.9 nm. MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts showed reduced SBET (135–291 m²/g) and Vtot (0.23–0.28 cm³/g), reflecting structural collapse during pyrolysis.
- **Observations:** The significant decrease in surface area and pore volume upon conversion from MIL-101(Fe) to Fe@C catalysts indicates the formation of dense carbon shells and loss of the original MOF porosity, which impacts reactant diffusion and catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Carbon Shell Structure and Graphitization
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy characterized the degree of graphitization and shell structure by analyzing D, G, and 2D bands.
- **Findings:** Fe@C-500 showed a weak, single 2D peak (amorphous carbon), Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ displayed four 2D peaks (graphene shell), and Fe@C-700 exhibited two 2D peaks (graphite shell). The ID/IG ratio decreased from 1.10 (Fe@C-500) to 0.79 (Fe@C-600) and 0.91 (Fe@C-700), indicating increased graphitization.
- **Observations:** Higher pyrolysis temperatures promoted the formation of more ordered carbon shells, with the graphene and graphite shells providing enhanced stability and electronic properties beneficial for Fischer–Tropsch catalysis.

#### 6.1.5 Thermal Stability and Composition
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) assessed decomposition behavior and carbon content. Samples were heated to 1000 °C at 10 °C/min under inert or 2% C₂H₂/He atmospheres.
- **Findings:** TGA confirmed the thermal stability of the carbon shells and quantified the residual iron content after pyrolysis.
- **Observations:** The robust carbon shells, especially at higher pyrolysis temperatures, contributed to improved thermal stability and resistance to oxidation, supporting catalyst durability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion represents the percentage of carbon monoxide molecules transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, calculated as the ratio of reacted CO to the total CO fed into the reactor.
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (300 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.0), the CO conversion at 24 hours on stream was:
  - Fe@C-500: 64.1%
  - Fe@C-600: 65.0%
  - Fe@C-700: 73.2%
  - Fe@C-600-H₂: 78.7%
  - At 184 hours, Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ reached 74.5% and 81.5%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@C-600-H₂ exhibited the highest CO conversion, with Fe@C-700 also performing strongly. The graphene-encapsulated Fe@C-600 maintained high conversion and stability over extended operation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity quantifies the fraction of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift reaction.
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 hours:
  - Fe@C-500: 35.2%
  - Fe@C-600: 33.3%
  - Fe@C-700: 33.9%
- **Observations and Trends:** All Fe@C catalysts demonstrated moderate CO₂ selectivity, with minimal variation across pyrolysis temperatures.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the percentage of methane among total hydrocarbon products, indicating the tendency toward undesired chain termination.
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 hours:
  - Fe@C-500: 11.5%
  - Fe@C-600: 10.5%
  - Fe@C-700: 12.4%
  - Fe@C-600-H₂: 23.4%
  - Unsupported-Fe: 28.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** The h‑Fe₃C-dominated Fe@C catalysts exhibited significantly lower methane selectivity compared to Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe, highlighting the beneficial effect of the carbide phase and carbon shell.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the proportion of hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of long-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 hours:
  - Fe@C-500: 88.5%
  - Fe@C-600: 89.5%
  - Fe@C-700: 87.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe@C catalysts achieved high C₃⁺ selectivity, correlating with their low methane production and high α values.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** 
  - Fe@C-500: 0.68–0.61
  - Fe@C-600: 0.69–0.68
  - Fe@C-700: 0.67–0.64
  - Fe@C-600-H₂: 0.49–0.50
  - Unsupported-Fe: 0.30–0.21
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values in Fe@C catalysts correspond to enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity and suppressed methane formation. The graphene shell (Fe@C-600) optimizes α, supporting superior product distribution.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time, indicating catalyst stability.
- **Experimental Data:** 
  - Fe@C-600: 0% (CO conversion increased from 65.0% to 74.5%)
  - Fe@C-600-H₂: Stable (78.7% to 81.5%)
  - Fe@C-500: 13.1%
  - Fe@C-700: 20.1%
  - Unsupported-Fe: 47.9%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ demonstrated exceptional stability, attributed to the protective carbon shell, especially graphene, which inhibits nanoparticle aggregation and deactivation.

---
**Summary:**  
The Fe@C core–shell catalysts, particularly those with a graphene shell and h‑Fe₃C core (Fe@C-600), deliver high CO conversion, superior long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity, low methane formation, and outstanding stability. The nature of the carbon shell and iron phase are critical determinants of Fischer–Tropsch performance.
