## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts are synthesized using a continuous coprecipitation method, which enables precise control of composition and structure. The following step-by-step procedure details the reported synthesis:

#### 1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
• Reagents: An aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ is continuously mixed with a silica sol to form the binary Fe/SiO₂ system, targeting an iron loading of approximately 50.9 wt%.  
• pH Control: A flowing ammonia solution is introduced to adjust and maintain the pH between 8.2 and 8.5.  
• Reaction Conditions: The mixture is maintained at 80 ± 1 °C, ensuring optimal precipitation kinetics.  
• Equipment: Temperature-controlled mixing apparatus with pH monitoring devices facilitate the process.  
• Observation: Successful mixing leads to the formation of a homogenous and visible precipitate.

#### 2. Precipitation–Deposition  
• Process: Immediately following mixing, the precipitate forms via deposition as ammonia induces the coprecipitation of iron and silica.  
• Note: This step solidifies the interaction between the metal precursor and support, setting the stage for subsequent processing.

#### 3. Drying  
• Conditions: The washed and filtered precipitate is dried at 120 °C for 12 hours.  
• Purpose: Drying removes residual moisture without triggering premature phase transformations.

#### 4. Calcination  
• Parameters: The dried precursor is calcined in static air at 500 °C for 5 hours.  
• Impact: This step induces the formation of iron oxide phases vital for later carburization.

#### 5. Post‑calcination Treatment  
• Reactor Setup: The calcined material undergoes controlled pretreatment in a fixed-bed reactor.  
• Conditions: The catalyst is exposed to different gas atmospheres—pure CO, pure H₂, or H₂/CO syngas—at ≈300 °C for 20 hours with a gas hourly space velocity of ~4000 mL/(g_cat·h).  
• Result: Varying gas environments yield distinct iron carbide phases (e.g., CO treatment produces Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂; H₂ promotes ε‑Fe₂C formation; syngas mainly forms χ‑Fe₅C₂), which directly influence Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### Comparative Analysis  
The continuous coprecipitation method offers major advantages in reproducibility and scalability. Its simplicity relative to batch processes and the precise control of pH and temperature make it suitable for large-scale catalyst synthesis, though the requirement for specific post‑calcination treatments adds a layer of complexity in tailoring the final active phases.

- **Operational Description**: MES is conducted to quantify the iron carbide phases and monitor phase transformations. Both in situ and ex situ measurements are performed.
- **Instruments and Models**: MR-351 constant-acceleration Mössbauer spectrometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: In situ experiments are carried out at 11 K to fully magnetically block the iron oxides and carbides, while reactor cooling to 300 K is used for data collection following pretreatment or FTS operation.
- **Test Parameters**: The velocity calibration is done using α‑Fe foil, and spectra are modeled via a Lorentzian line profile including quadruple doublets and magnetic sextets.
- **Key Notes**: MES distinguishes between phases effectively despite potential modifications due to high gas hourly space velocities during synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation step for the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is a critical pretreatment process performed in a fixed‐bed reactor. Following calcination, the catalyst precursor is subjected to controlled pretreatment to promote the formation of desired carbide phases that directly correlate with catalytic performance.

#### Activation Temperature
The catalyst is heated to 300 °C during the activation. This temperature is selected to ensure complete removal of adsorbed water and to facilitate the controlled reduction and carburization of the iron oxide precursor. The moderate 300 °C setting is optimal for exposing active sites while minimizing sintering, thereby maintaining high metal dispersion.

#### Activation Time
The pretreatment is maintained for 20 hours, which allows sufficient time for the gas–solid reactions to fully convert the precursor into its active carbide phases. This extended activation period is critical for achieving reproducible formation of phases such as Fe₇C₃, χ‑Fe₅C₂, or ε‑Fe₂C, depending on the gas environment employed.

#### Activation Gas
Three specific gas atmospheres are utilized in the activation step: pure CO, pure H₂, and a syngas mixture with a H₂/CO ratio of 2. When pure CO is used, a high carbon chemical potential favors the formation of both Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂, often with a bimodal particle size distribution. In contrast, pure H₂ pretreatment tends to yield ε‑Fe₂C along with χ‑Fe₅C₂, while the syngas atmosphere predominantly leads to the formation of χ‑Fe₅C₂. These variations directly influence phase composition, catalytic activity, and product selectivity during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### Activation Space-Time Velocity
During pretreatment, the reactor is operated at a gas hourly space velocity of approximately 4000 mL/(g_cat·h) (4 NL/(g_cat·h)). This parameter is essential as it ensures uniform gas flow over the catalyst particles, thereby promoting consistent gas–catalyst interactions. Such controlled flow conditions are vital for preventing over-reduction or incomplete activation, ultimately leading to stable and reproducible carbide formation.

Collectively, these carefully controlled activation parameters directly impact the catalyst’s dispersion, phase composition, and overall performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was performed under rigorously controlled conditions to enable consistent catalytic evaluation and direct reproducibility of results. The reaction environment was systematically established with three key parameters, each playing a critical role in catalyst performance and hydrocarbon product distribution.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
• **Definition:** The reaction temperature governs the kinetics of syngas conversion, the formation and stability of active iron carbide phases, and ultimately the catalyst’s intrinsic activity.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were conducted at approximately 260 °C.  
• **Observations:** At this temperature, the active phase (particularly χ‑Fe₅C₂ and Fe₇C₃) is optimally stabilized, promoting an efficient CO conversion rate and maintaining catalyst integrity throughout the evaluation period. The selected temperature also minimizes undesirable phase transformations, thus ensuring reliable performance.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
• **Definition:** Reaction pressure directly impacts the concentration of reactant gases in the fixed-bed reactor. It is a critical factor in controlling syngas reactivity and product selectivity during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was carried out at 3.0 MPa.  
• **Observations:** Operating at this elevated pressure enhances the solubility and effective collisions of syngas molecules within the catalyst bed. This condition has been observed to favor the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺) while helping to suppress excessive methane production. The pressure also contributes to maintaining stable catalyst activity under continuous operation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
• **Definition:** The ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed gas critically influences the hydrocarbon distribution and selectivity in the Fischer–Tropsch process.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** A feed H₂/CO ratio of 2 was maintained throughout the reaction.  
• **Observations:** This ratio has been demonstrated to promote the formation of desired hydrocarbon products, although minor shifts in selectivity can occur due to in situ water–gas shift effects. The balance provided by an H₂/CO ratio of 2 is essential for achieving optimal CO conversion and ensuring the catalyst delivers reproducible performance across successive runs.

Each of these parameters was precisely controlled using a fixed-bed reactor equipped with advanced temperature and pressure management systems, ensuring that the reaction conditions could be accurately replicated to study the intrinsic activity of the Fe/SiO₂ catalysts under Fischer–Tropsch synthesis conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases present in the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD), with phase assignments based on standard reference patterns. The Scherrer equation was applied to estimate crystallite sizes from peak broadening.
- **Findings:** After calcination, XRD patterns showed broad peaks corresponding to highly dispersed iron oxides (α-Fe₂O₃ or γ-Fe₂O₃). Post-pretreatment, distinct carbide phases formed depending on the gas atmosphere:
  - **CO pretreatment:** Fe₇C₃ (peaks at 40.0, 42.9, 44.9, 50.8, 53.4°) and χ‑Fe₅C₂.
  - **H₂ pretreatment:** α-Fe initially, transforming to ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂ after Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS).
  - **Syngas pretreatment:** Only χ‑Fe₅C₂ detected.
  The average crystallite size of Fe₇C₃ in the spent FeSi-CO catalyst was estimated at 28.2 nm.
- **Observations:** The pretreatment atmosphere directly determined the resulting carbide phases and their distribution, with CO and H₂ yielding bimodal distributions (large Fe₇C₃ or ε‑Fe₂C, small χ‑Fe₅C₂), while syngas led to uniform χ‑Fe₅C₂.

#### 6.1.2 Phase Quantification and Transformation (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer Spectroscopy (MES), performed at 11 K, quantified the relative amounts of iron oxide and carbide phases and tracked phase changes during reaction.
- **Findings:** MES spectra revealed that:
  - **FeSi-CO catalyst:** Comprised mainly of Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ after CO pretreatment and FTS.
  - **FeSi-H₂ catalyst:** Contained ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂ after FTS.
  - **FeSi-syn catalyst:** Showed only χ‑Fe₅C₂.
  MES provided quantitative phase compositions, confirming the coexistence and transformation of phases observed by XRD.
- **Observations:** In situ MES sometimes indicated higher carbide content due to differences in experimental conditions, but ex situ MES and XRD results were consistent for structure–performance analysis.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution (TEM/STEM)
- **Description:** Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Scanning TEM (STEM) were used to assess particle morphology, size distribution, and elemental mapping.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed:
  - **FeSi-CO catalyst:** Bimodal distribution with large Fe₇C₃ particles (~27.5 nm) and small χ‑Fe₅C₂ particles (~7.1 nm).
  - **FeSi-H₂ catalyst:** Large ε‑Fe₂C (~13.9 nm) and small χ‑Fe₅C₂ (~6.7 nm).
  - **FeSi-syn catalyst:** Uniform χ‑Fe₅C₂ particles (~7.5 nm).
  STEM mapping confirmed the presence of iron carbides and revealed amorphous shells (likely iron oxides) around some particles.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ support stabilized the nanoparticles, and the observed bimodal or uniform distributions correlated with the pretreatment atmosphere and catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Local Structure and Coordination Environment (XAFS/EXAFS)
- **Description:** X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS/EXAFS) analysis provided insights into the local coordination of Fe atoms in oxide and carbide phases.
- **Findings:** In the spent FeSi-CO catalyst, EXAFS fitting yielded Fe–C and Fe–Fe coordination numbers of 1.7 and 3.4, respectively, for the carbide phase, and Fe–O and Fe–Fe coordination numbers of 5.6 and 3.3 for the oxide phase. Characteristic Fe–Fe shell distances (2.17 Å for χ‑Fe₅C₂, 2.06 Å for Fe₇C₃) were identified.
- **Observations:** These results confirmed the coexistence of small iron oxide and iron carbide particles, supporting the phase assignments from XRD and MES.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (260 °C, 3.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2), the FeSi-CO catalyst achieves a CO conversion of 50.8%. Comparative catalysts (FeSi-H₂, FeSi-syn, and recycled FeSi-CO-H₂) exhibit lower conversion rates, correlating with their distinct iron carbide phase compositions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest CO conversion is observed for FeSi-CO, attributed to its significant Fe₇C₃ content. The pretreatment atmosphere directly influences the reduction and carburization degree, thereby tuning the active phase and conversion efficiency.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (C mol \%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The FeSi-H₂ catalyst demonstrates a CH₄ selectivity of 10.6%, which is notably low among promoter-free Fe/SiO₂ catalysts. Other catalysts show higher methane selectivity, reflecting differences in active phase composition.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower methane selectivity in FeSi-H₂ is linked to the presence of ε‑Fe₂C, which enhances C–C coupling and suppresses undesired methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms among total hydrocarbon products:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (wt \%)} = \frac{\text{mass of C}_5^+}{\text{total hydrocarbon mass}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-H₂ achieves a C₅⁺ selectivity of 74.4%, the highest among the tested catalysts. This demonstrates its effectiveness in producing longer-chain hydrocarbons suitable for liquid fuels.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high C₅⁺ selectivity of FeSi-H₂, despite a slightly lower α factor, underscores the role of ε‑Fe₂C in promoting chain growth and suppressing light hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon synthesis, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher tendency for long-chain product formation.
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values are: FeSi-CO (1.00), FeSi-H₂ (0.78), and FeSi-syn (0.91). The FeSi-CO catalyst, with the highest α, favors longer-chain hydrocarbons, while FeSi-H₂, despite a lower α, achieves superior C₅⁺ selectivity due to its unique phase composition.
- **Observations and Trends:** The data reveal that both the α factor and the nature of the iron carbide phase (notably ε‑Fe₂C and Fe₇C₃) critically determine product distribution.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring the consistency of CO conversion and phase composition during reaction. Deactivation rate is typically expressed as the decline in CO conversion per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe/SiO₂ catalysts maintain stable CO conversion and phase composition throughout the reaction, as confirmed by in situ Mössbauer spectroscopy and XRD. No significant deactivation or phase transformation is observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The structural integrity of the iron carbide phases and the absence of Fe diffusion into the SiO₂ support underpin the catalysts’ robust stability under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.
