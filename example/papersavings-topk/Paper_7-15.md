## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalyst proceeds via a continuous coprecipitation process followed by well‐controlled post‑processing steps. Below is a detailed, step‑by‑step description ensuring reproducibility:

#### Precursor Mixing / Coprecipitation
1. Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ is combined with silica sol in a controlled flow. Concurrently, a flowing ammonia solution is introduced at 80 ± 1 °C.  
   • Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol; ammonia is used to adjust the pH to 8.2–8.5.  
   • Observation: The mixture rapidly forms a distinct precipitate, indicating successful deposition of the catalyst precursor.

#### Precipitation–Deposition
2. As the iron nitrate and silica sol are mixed with ammonia, the precipitation–deposition step occurs naturally.  
   • Role: This step ensures even distribution of the active components before any further treatments.  
   • Equipment: A pH meter and continuous stirring apparatus secure constant conditions.

#### Drying and Calcination
3. The formed precipitate is then subjected to an extensive washing with distilled water, ensuring removal of residual impurities.  
4. The washed cake is dried for 12 hours at 120 °C to achieve sufficient moisture removal.  
5. Calcination follows in static air at 500 °C for 5 hours.  
   • Impact: This thermal treatment facilitates the formation of catalyst precursors that eventually yield a mixture of carbide phases.

#### Controlled Gas Treatment (Post‑treatment)
6. The calcined precursor is loaded into a fixed‑bed reactor and treated with different gas atmospheres under controlled conditions:
   • Conditions: 300 °C with a gas hourly space velocity of 4000 mL/(gcat·h) for 20 hours.
   • Atmospheres: Pure CO (for forming χ‑Fe₅C₂), pure H₂ (favoring ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂), or syngas (2H₂/CO, resulting in mixed phases).
   • Modification: Variation in reduction time (5, 10, or 20 hours) allows tuning of phase distribution and catalyst performance.

- **Reagents**: An aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ (adjusted to meet the target iron loading) is combined with 50 mL of silica sol. Ammonia solution is used to adjust the pH.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch system is performed as a pre‐reaction treatment to promote in situ carburization and the formation of active iron carbide phases.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical to initiate the reduction and carburization of the iron precursor, setting the carbon chemical potential necessary for carbide formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** For CO pretreatment, the catalyst is activated at approximately 280 °C, while treatments under pure H₂ and syngas (2H₂/CO) are conducted at 300 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating in the 250–350 °C range allows control over the phase evolution; lower temperatures favor selective carbide formation, whereas higher temperatures could lead to carbon‐deficient phases.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure governs the gas–solid interactions during the pretreatment, influencing both reduction kinetics and carburization efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is carried out at around 1 bar.
- **Observations:** This low-pressure condition ensures a controlled environment that promotes uniform exposure of the catalyst to the reactive gas atmosphere.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The nature of the activation gas directly affects the catalyst’s reduction and carburization, thus steering the formation of specific carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Three gas atmospheres are employed:
  - Pure CO: Leads to a bimodal carbide distribution containing both Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂.
  - Pure H₂: Yields predominantly χ‑Fe₅C₂ in combination with minor ε‑Fe₂C formation.
  - Syngas (2H₂/CO): Exclusively forms the χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase.
- **Observations:** The choice of gas determines not only the phase composition but also affects particle dispersion and overall catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity (STV) controls the exposure time of catalyst particles to the activation gas per unit mass, influencing the carburization extent.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An STV of 4000 mL/(g_cat·h) is maintained during the gas treatment.
- **Observations:** This parameter ensures a consistent and reproducible gas–solid contact, critical for achieving uniform carbide formation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The activation duration is essential for complete catalyst reduction and carburization without over-exposure that might cause sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The controlled gas treatment is typically carried out for 20 h.
- **Observations:** Longer activation times facilitate the development of desired carbide phases; shorter durations (e.g., 5 or 10 h) have been shown to yield incomplete conversion.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Transformations
- **Description:** The crystalline phases present in Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD), in situ Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES), and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis. These techniques elucidate the evolution of iron carbide phases under different reduction and carburization atmospheres, which is critical for understanding catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD and MES analyses reveal that the gas atmosphere during catalyst pretreatment at 300 °C determines the dominant iron carbide phase:
  - **Syngas (2H₂/CO):** Only χ‑Fe₅C₂ is formed.
  - **Pure CO:** Both Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ are present, with a bimodal particle size distribution.
  - **Pure H₂ (after FTS):** Both ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂ are observed, also with a bimodal distribution.
  XAFS confirms the presence of Fe–C coordination (N = 1.3 at R = 1.80 Å) and Fe–Fe coordination (N = 2.5 at R = 2.20–2.42 Å) in FeSi-CO, with a significant edge shift (ΔE = –9.1 eV), indicative of carbide formation.
- **Observations:** The phase composition is highly sensitive to the carbon chemical potential controlled by the gas environment. χ‑Fe₅C₂ is stabilized under syngas, while Fe₇C₃ and ε‑Fe₂C require more reducing or carburizing conditions. The formation of these phases directly correlates with catalytic activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) were employed to assess particle size, morphology, and dispersion of iron carbide nanoparticles on the silica support. Particle sizes were also estimated from XRD using the Scherrer equation.
- **Findings:** TEM images of the calcined catalyst show nanoparticles with an average size of 5.5 nm, finely dispersed on SiO₂. After reaction:
  - **χ‑Fe₅C₂:** Average particle size is about 7.5 nm (fresh) and 6.7 nm (spent FeSi-H₂ catalyst).
  - **ε‑Fe₂C:** Average particle size is 13.9 nm (spent FeSi-H₂ catalyst).
  - **Fe₇C₃:** Particles are notably larger than χ‑Fe₅C₂ in the spent FeSi-CO catalyst, contributing to a bimodal distribution.
  Lattice fringes (e.g., 2.03 Å for the (510) plane of χ‑Fe₅C₂) confirm phase identity. STEM mapping verifies the composition as iron carbides.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ support effectively stabilizes and disperses the nanoparticles, minimizing agglomeration even after prolonged reaction. The presence of amorphous shells, attributed to mild surface oxidation, is also noted. Bimodal distributions reflect the coexistence of multiple carbide phases under certain conditions.

#### 6.1.3 Phase Composition and Oxidation State Analysis
- **Description:** In situ Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES) at 11 K and XAFS were used to determine the oxidation states and phase composition of iron species after various treatments.
- **Findings:** MES spectra confirm the coexistence of χ‑Fe₅C₂, Fe₇C₃, and ε‑Fe₂C depending on the reduction/carburization atmosphere. XAFS analysis, calibrated with Fe foil, shows significant Fe–C coordination in FeSi-CO, minimal in FeSi-syn, and absent in FeSi-H₂, consistent with the degree of carburization.
- **Observations:** Careful sample handling (e.g., paraffin sealing, glovebox transfer) preserves the true phase state, ensuring accurate characterization. The degree of carburization and resulting phase composition are directly linked to the synthesis atmosphere and are critical for catalytic performance.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - FeSi‑CO: 50.8%  
  - FeSi‑H₂: 44.8%  
  - FeSi‑syn: 22.3%
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The FeSi‑CO catalyst, containing both Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases, achieves the highest CO conversion. FeSi‑H₂, with ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂, shows moderately high conversion, while FeSi‑syn, dominated by χ‑Fe₅C₂, exhibits the lowest conversion. Increased reduction time (e.g., 20 h) during CO pretreatment enhances carbide content and CO conversion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - FeSi‑CO: 9.7%  
  - FeSi‑H₂: 4.6%  
  - FeSi‑syn: 2.8%
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  FeSi‑CO produces the most CO₂, suggesting higher water–gas shift activity, while FeSi‑syn minimizes CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - FeSi‑CO: 19.2%  
  - FeSi‑H₂: 10.6%  
  - FeSi‑syn: 12.9%
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  FeSi‑CO exhibits the highest methane selectivity, correlating with the presence of Fe₇C₃. FeSi‑H₂, with ε‑Fe₂C, achieves the lowest CH₄ selectivity, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity denotes the proportion of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator for liquid fuel production:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - FeSi‑CO: 50.0%  
  - FeSi‑H₂: 74.4%  
  - FeSi‑syn: 70.0%
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  FeSi‑H₂ demonstrates the highest C₅⁺ selectivity, indicating superior chain growth capability, while FeSi‑CO is less effective for long-chain hydrocarbon synthesis.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model:
  - Higher α values (closer to 1) favor long-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - FeSi‑CO: 0.74  
  - FeSi‑H₂: 0.81  
  - FeSi‑syn: 0.78
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The FeSi‑H₂ catalyst, with the highest α value, is most selective for long-chain hydrocarbons, consistent with its low methane selectivity and high C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  r_d = -\frac{dX_{\text{CO}}/dt}{X_{\text{CO}}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - FeSi‑CO shows a higher deactivation rate compared to FeSi‑H₂ and FeSi‑syn.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The presence of Fe₇C₃ in FeSi‑CO correlates with faster deactivation, while catalysts with ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂ (FeSi‑H₂, FeSi‑syn) exhibit greater stability, attributed to high dispersion and phase stability on silica.

**Summary:**  
The catalytic performance of Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is strongly influenced by the nature and dispersion of iron carbide phases. FeSi‑H₂, rich in ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂, achieves the most desirable balance of high C₅⁺ selectivity, low methane formation, and catalyst stability, while FeSi‑CO, containing Fe₇C₃, offers higher activity but at the expense of selectivity and stability.
