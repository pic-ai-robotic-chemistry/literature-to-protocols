## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines the synthesis methods for the Fischer–Tropsch Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic) and Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic) catalysts, detailing precursor preparation, deposition, drying, calcination, and relevant post‑treatments.

#### Hydrothermal Synthesis of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Dissolve 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O in 110 mL water; sodium acetate trihydrate and hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide are added to enhance solubility and control particle morphology. Ethylenediamine is incorporated to create a basic environment, promoting uniform nucleation.  
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Not applicable in this step.  
3. • Drying  
  – The hydrothermally treated mixture is washed and dried at 100 °C for 12 h.  
4. • Calcination  
  – Calcine at 500 °C for 5 h to yield phase-pure Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.  

*Equipment:* Teflon‑lined autoclave is used for the hydrothermal reaction conducted at 200 °C for 10 h, ensuring reproducible nanoparticle formation.

#### Manganese Deposition via Deposition‑Precipitation
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Disperse 0.5 g of the as‐prepared Fe₂O₃ in ethanol.  
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Add dropwise an aqueous KMnO₄ solution prepared by dissolving 0.078 g KMnO₄ in 5 mL water while stirring at 30 °C for 2 h; this dropwise addition promotes homogeneous manganese deposition, as confirmed by observed color changes and precipitate formation.  
3. • Drying  
  – Collect the product via centrifugation, wash, and dry at 100 °C for 12 h.

#### SiO₂ Shell Formation and Surface Modification
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Disperse Fe@Mn in ethanol and add TEOS (0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL) under stirring at 30 °C.  
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Introduce ammonia solution and water to catalyze TEOS hydrolysis and condensation, forming an amorphous SiO₂ shell.  
3. • Drying  
  – Collect and dry the SiO₂-coated product under controlled conditions.  
4. • Additional Post‑treatment  
  – For hydrophobic catalysts (Fe@Mn@xSi-c), perform surface silanization by adding chlorotrimethylsilane, ensuring the shell’s hydrophobic modification while maintaining catalyst integrity.

Each step, detailed with precise temperatures, durations, reagent concentrations, and equipment settings, ensures reproducibility and clear transferability of this synthesis procedure to similar catalyst preparation endeavors.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe@Mn@xSi
- **TEOS-Treated Suspension**: Mixed by stirring at 30 °C for 3 hours.

## 4. Characterization Methods

- **Operational Description**: XRD is employed to determine the crystalline phases and monitor phase evolution of the Fe-based catalyst during reaction. In situ measurements are performed to track transitions among Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄, χ‑Fe₅C₂, and θ‑Fe₃C under reactive atmospheres.

- **Key Notes**: Accurate phase identification depends on proper sample alignment and controlling atmospheric conditions.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)  
- **Operational Description**: Analyzes the reduction behavior by monitoring H₂ consumption as the temperature ramps.  
- **Instruments and Models**: DAS‑7000 chemical adsorption instrument coupled with a quartz tube reactor.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Pre‑treatment in N₂ at 350 °C for 1 hour; cooling to 80 °C then exposure to 5% H₂/95% N₂ at 30 mL/min, with temperature ramped to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.  
- **Test Parameters**: Sample weight of 50 mg per run.  
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and precise temperature control are critical for reproducibility.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)

- **Key Notes**: Monitoring CO consumption provides insights into carburization.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

Catalyst activation is achieved through a sequential approach designed to convert the iron precursor into a stable, active carbide phase essential for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. Initially, a pure H₂ reduction is performed followed by a syngas activation step.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature determines the energy available for carburization of the reduced metallic iron into the active iron carbide phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** After reduction, the catalyst is exposed to syngas at 320 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 320 °C effectively drives the carburization process without causing excessive oxidation or sintering, thereby exposing stable active sites necessary for CO activation and C–C coupling.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Pressure control during activation is critical for ensuring steady reduction and carburization kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The syngas activation is conducted at 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** The mild pressure supports controlled gas–solid interactions, preventing aggressive phase changes and catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas environment not only provides the necessary reducing conditions but also supplies the carbon source for carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Following a H₂ pretreatment, the catalyst is activated using syngas composed of CO and H₂, with the molar ratio maintained at 1:2 (CO:H₂). CO facilitates the transformation of metallic iron into the catalytically active iron carbide phase.
- **Observations:** The introduction of CO during the syngas activation step is critical to achieve simultaneous carburization while preserving structural integrity.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation ensures complete conversion of the metallic phase into the active carbide without undesired side reactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to syngas for 5 hours at 320 °C and 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** This 5‐hour period allows sufficient time for the formation and stabilization of the active phase, as confirmed by reproducible phase evolution and catalytic performance, while minimizing the risk of over-carburization.

By rigorously controlling these parameters during the pre-reaction treatment, the catalyst is reliably transformed into its active form, ensuring optimal performance and stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction section for the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation is defined by a set of tightly controlled experimental parameters, ensuring reproducibility and reliable performance analysis.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature governs the activation of syngas and the formation of the active catalytic phase. In Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, it dictates the balance between desired hydrocarbon chain growth and undesired side reactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reactor is maintained at 320 °C. This temperature is critical for optimally reducing the iron species and promoting the formation of iron carbide, which is essential for CO hydrogenation.
- **Observations:** At 320 °C, the catalyst exhibits efficient activation, maintaining a stable environment for the Fischer–Tropsch process throughout the 24-hour test period.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure influences the concentration of syngas in the reaction medium, thus affecting product distribution and conversion rates.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is conducted at a fixed pressure of 2.0 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at 2.0 MPa aids in enhancing the solubility of reactants and helps shift the equilibrium towards higher hydrocarbon formation, favoring heavier product fractions.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is pivotal in controlling the hydrocarbon distribution, influencing both chain growth probability and selectivity toward olefins versus paraffins.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A molar ratio of H₂ to CO of 2 is used consistently in the experiments.
- **Observations:** This ratio strikes a balance, providing sufficient hydrogen for CO conversion while promoting an optimal distribution of hydrocarbon products. A higher ratio could lead to increased methane formation, whereas a lower ratio might not adequately drive C–C coupling.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity and Duration
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) reflects the reactant flow rate per unit mass of catalyst, affecting contact time and conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV is set at 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹ with a reaction duration of 24 hours.
- **Observations:** This parameter ensures a controlled exposure of the catalyst to reactants, which is reflected in varied CO conversions (e.g., 53.8% for Fe@Mn@0.05Si decreasing with increased SiO₂ shell thickness) and product selectivity trends, confirming both catalyst performance and stability under the tested conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and evolution of the catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and in situ XRD (Ultima IV-185 and Rigaku SmartLab diffractometers). These techniques enabled real-time monitoring of phase transitions under various atmospheres and temperatures, crucial for understanding catalyst behavior during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles undergo sequential transformation to Fe₃O₄, FeO, and ultimately iron carbide phases (χ-Fe₅C₂ and θ-Fe₃C) during reduction and reaction. In hydrophilic catalysts (Fe@Mn@xSi), post-reaction analysis showed a mixture of 59.7% χ-Fe₅C₂, 34.3% Fe₃O₄, and 6.0% Fe³⁺ (spm). In contrast, hydrophobic catalysts (Fe@Mn@xSi-c) predominantly contained 74.1% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ-Fe₃C, with Fe₃O₄ formation effectively suppressed.
- **Observations:** The preservation of iron carbide phases, especially χ-Fe₅C₂, is directly linked to catalytic activity. Hydrophobic surface modification inhibits the oxidation of iron carbide to Fe₃O₄, thereby stabilizing the active phase under reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Dispersion
- **Description:** Elemental composition and spatial distribution were assessed using inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping in conjunction with transmission electron microscopy (TEM).
- **Findings:** ICP confirmed the intended incorporation of Fe, Mn, and Si in the catalyst formulations. EDS mapping demonstrated homogeneous dispersion of Mn (from KMnO₄) on the Fe₂O₃ core and uniform encapsulation by the amorphous SiO₂ shell.
- **Observations:** The successful spatial separation and uniform distribution of active components are essential for controlled phase evolution and catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Composition and Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Surface chemical states were characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), while hydrophobicity was quantified via water-droplet contact angle measurements (Kruss DSA100).
- **Findings:** XPS confirmed the presence of surface elements and, in hydrophobic catalysts, trace chlorine from silanization. FTIR (including in situ DRIFTS) showed that hydrophilic catalysts exhibited strong CO adsorption and carbonate species formation, whereas hydrophobic catalysts displayed only gaseous CO bands, indicating hindered CO adsorption due to surface modification. Contact angle measurements demonstrated increased hydrophobicity after silanization, correlating with enhanced water resistance.
- **Observations:** Surface hydrophobization effectively limits water ingress, protecting the iron carbide phase and altering surface adsorption properties, which is critical for catalyst stability and selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Core–Shell Structure
- **Description:** Morphological features and core–shell architecture were visualized using TEM and EDS.
- **Findings:** TEM images confirmed the encapsulation of Fe@Mn cores within a tunable SiO₂ shell. Post-reaction images revealed changes in core size, reflecting phase evolution. The shell thickness, controlled by TEOS dosage, was shown to influence the accessibility and protection of the active core.
- **Observations:** The engineered core–shell structure enables precise control over reactant diffusion and phase stability, directly impacting catalytic behavior during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (320 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts exhibited a decrease in CO conversion with increasing SiO₂ shell thickness: 53.8% (Fe@Mn@0.05Si), 41.2% (Fe@Mn@0.2Si), 32.7% (Fe@Mn@0.5Si), 27.1% (Fe@Mn@1.0Si), and 24.3% (Fe@Mn@2.8Si). Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalysts showed even lower CO conversion, with the thinnest shell (Fe@Mn@0.05Si-c) yielding <2% and Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c achieving 30.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Thicker SiO₂ shells hinder CO diffusion and carburization, reducing the formation of active iron carbide phases and thus lowering CO conversion. Hydrophobic modification further suppresses CO conversion, especially at low shell thickness, due to trace chlorine poisoning and restricted water ingress.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, primarily via the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,out}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe@Mn@0.2Si (hydrophilic), CO₂ selectivity was 28.9%. In contrast, Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c (hydrophobic) exhibited a markedly lower CO₂ selectivity of 7.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophilic catalysts facilitate bidirectional water diffusion, promoting the WGS reaction and increasing CO₂ formation. Hydrophobic modification suppresses water ingress, thereby reducing CO₂ selectivity and limiting the WGS side reaction.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,out}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity for Fe@Mn@0.2Si was 21.5%, while Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c showed a reduced value of 14.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic catalysts suppress hydrogenation activity, resulting in lower methane formation and favoring olefin production.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{C in C}_3^+ \text{ hydrocarbons}}{\text{Total converted C}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c achieved a C₃⁺ selectivity of 78.0%, compared to 65.2% for Fe@Mn@0.2Si.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic surface engineering enhances C–C coupling, increasing the yield of long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, indicates the probability of chain growth during hydrocarbon formation:
  \[
  W_n = (1 - \alpha) \alpha^{n-1}
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the molar fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c exhibited an α value of 0.81, higher than the 0.74 observed for Fe@Mn@0.2Si.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic modification stabilizes iron carbide phases, increasing α and favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction time (h)}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c maintained stable CO conversion and selectivity over 110 h, with negligible deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic catalysts demonstrate superior long-term stability, attributed to the preservation of the active iron carbide phase and suppression of water-induced oxidation.
