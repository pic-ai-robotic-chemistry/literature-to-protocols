## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalysts (100Fe/4Cu; 100Fe/4Cu/2La; 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba; 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba) were synthesized via a water‑in‑oil microemulsion method. This section details the step‑by‑step procedural sequence to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

#### • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation
1. An aqueous solution of FeCl₃·6H₂O (Merck, > 99%) is prepared along with copper, lanthanum, and barium precursors in predetermined atomic ratios (e.g., 100Fe/4Cu, with promoter amounts adjusted as needed).  
   - Reagents: FeCl₃·6H₂O, copper nitrate, La₂O₃ (or suitable La precursor), and BaO (or appropriate Ba precursor) in defined proportion.
2. The oil phase is prepared by mixing chloroform and 1‑butanol in a 1:1 ratio with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as the surfactant to stabilize the microemulsion.  
   - Equipment: Magnetic stirrer, beakers.

#### • Precipitation–Deposition
3. The prepared aqueous solution is added dropwise into the oil phase under continuous stirring to promote homogeneous mixing.  
   - Immediately after mixing, hydrazine (25–30%) is introduced to precipitate the catalyst components.  
4. The mixture is stirred continuously for 4 hours, ensuring complete precipitation and deposition of the metal precursors.
   - Observational note: Although no explicit color change is reported, the formation of precipitates confirms reaction progress.

#### • Drying
5. Post‑stirring, the reaction mixture is allowed to decant overnight before it is centrifuged to recover the solid catalyst.  
   - The recovered solids are washed sequentially with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.
6. Drying is carried out overnight at 110 °C (approximately 15 hours) to remove moisture thoroughly.

#### • Calcination and Post‑processing
7. The dry catalyst is calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 hours.  
   - This step promotes crystallinity and ensures the formation of the desired phases.
8. Following calcination, the catalyst is pressed, crushed, and sieved to obtain particle sizes within the 30–40 mesh range, ensuring consistent sample preparation for subsequent catalytic testing.

This structured synthesis not only controls the atomic ratios and morphology via precise reagent mixing and post‑processing but also assures reproducibility—key for evaluating catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

The following protocol outlines the step‐by‐step synthesis of the nanostructured iron catalysts (100Fe/4Cu; 100Fe/4Cu/2La; 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba; and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba) using a water-in-oil microemulsion method. Each synthesis workflow follows the strict order from precursor preparation to purification, drying, calcination, and activation, ensuring reproducibility and control over particle dispersion.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe/4Cu

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • 100 parts FeCl₃·6H₂O and 4 parts copper precursor dissolved in deionized water.  
  • Oil phase composed of a 1:1 mixture of chloroform and 1-butanol with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as surfactant.
  • Hydrazine (25–30%) as the precipitating agent.
- **Conditions**:  
  Stir the aqueous solution and oil phase at room temperature for 4 hours.
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O and the copper precursor in water.  
  2. Add the aqueous solution into the oil phase containing chloroform, 1-butanol, and SDS to form a stable microemulsion.  
  3. Introduce hydrazine gradually while maintaining continuous stirring to ensure uniform precipitation.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Maintain the specified stirring duration and order of reagent addition for controlled particle formation.

##### Step 2: Purification
- **Process**:  
  Allow the reaction mixture to decant overnight. Recover the precipitate by centrifugation (standard laboratory settings) and sequentially wash with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone to remove residual surfactants and unreacted chemicals.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**:  
  Dry the washed samples overnight at 110 °C.
- **Calcination**:  
  Calcine the dried catalyst in air at a final temperature of 400 °C for 4 hours, ensuring proper oxidation and stabilization.
- **Post-Processing**:  
  Press, crush, and sieve the calcined material to generate 30–40 mesh particles.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Formulations with Promoters

For the catalysts 100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba, incorporate the promoter precursors (La precursor and/or Ba precursor) in the aqueous phase simultaneously with FeCl₃·6H₂O and the copper precursor. The promoter addition ratios are: 2 parts La for 100Fe/4Cu/2La; 1 part La and 1 part Ba for 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba; and 2 parts Ba for 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba.

All subsequent steps—microemulsion formation, stirring, decantation, centrifugation, washing, drying, and calcination—are performed identically to maintain consistent particle dispersion and catalyst integrity. Prior to catalytic testing, a reduction activation step (using a 10% H₂–90% N₂ flow for 3 hours) may be applied to ensure the catalyst is in its reduced state.

This detailed workflow, including controlled mixing, precise reagent addition, purification, and thermal treatment, guarantees reproducibility and high performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis reactions.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

Prior to the Fischer–Tropsch reaction, the catalyst undergoes a two‐step pre‐reaction activation process designed to expose the active metallic sites efficiently while preserving catalyst integrity.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for driving the reduction of surface oxides and ensuring the formation of active sites without inducing adverse sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** After an initial reduction step, the catalyst is activated at 270 °C.
- **Observations:** This moderate temperature is selected to balance effective reduction with catalyst stability.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure governs the interaction between the gas phase and the catalyst surface, influencing reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Atmospheric pressure provides a controlled environment that supports uniform activation across the catalyst bed.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
- **Definition:** The gas composition in the activation phase is responsible for maintaining a reducing environment to remove residual oxides.
- **Experimental Conditions:** During the activation phase, a synthesis gas stream with an H₂/CO ratio of 1 is used. Prior to this, an initial reduction is conducted using a 10% H₂–90% N₂ mixture for 3 hours.
- **Observations:** The synthesis gas composition ensures efficient reduction while the initial reducing gas preconditions the catalyst for subsequent activation.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity parameter regulates the exposure of the catalyst to the activation gas, impacting the uniformity of gas–solid interactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A flow rate corresponding to a space velocity of 1.5 nl/(h·g) is maintained during the activation.
- **Observations:** This rate is optimized to prevent over-reduction and ensure complete treatment of the catalyst particles.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Activation time determines the duration over which the catalyst is subjected to the pre-reaction conditions, ensuring full conversion of precursor compounds into the active phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The synthesis gas activation is carried out for 24 hours following the initial 3-hour reduction.
- **Observations:** The extended activation period assures that the catalyst surface is thoroughly conditioned for optimal Fischer–Tropsch synthesis performance.

This structured activation protocol, executed in a fixed-bed reactor equipped with precise temperature and mass-flow control, ensures reproducibility and sets the stage for enhanced CO dissociative adsorption during subsequent FTS reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is the thermal condition at which the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis occurs, critically influencing syngas activation and the subsequent hydrocarbon formation.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The fixed-bed reactor was maintained at a temperature of 290 °C throughout the catalytic evaluation.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 290 °C promotes effective activation of the iron catalyst, ensuring an adequate balance between chain growth and hydrogenation reactions. This condition has been optimized to favor longer-chain hydrocarbon production while minimizing excessive methane formation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure governs the concentration of reactants in the reactor, directly impacting the catalytic performance and product distribution by enhancing gas solubility.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were conducted at a reaction pressure of 1.8 MPa.  
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure favors improved syngas concentration and increases the rate of molecular collisions, which in turn shifts the product distribution towards heavier hydrocarbons. Such conditions are critical for efficient chain growth.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio in the feed gas is a pivotal parameter that enables a balance between hydrogenation and chain propagation reactions.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A H₂/CO ratio of 1.1 was maintained during all catalytic tests.  
- **Observations:** This slightly hydrogen-rich condition ensures sufficient hydrogen availability for the conversion of CO into hydrocarbons while suppressing the formation of undesired light products like methane, thereby improving overall selectivity.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) defines the rate at which the reactants are passed over the catalyst, dictating the contact time between reactants and catalyst surface.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reactor was operated at a GHSV of 3 nl/(g·h).  
- **Observations:** A controlled space velocity of 3 nl/(g·h) allows for a sufficient residence time for the syngas, ensuring thorough conversion and enabling detailed assessment of catalyst stability. The 72-hour duration of the tests further confirms that this parameter is key to monitoring long-term catalyst performance and deactivation trends.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases relevant to Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS) performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns for all catalyst formulations (100Fe/4Cu, 100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba) exhibited a characteristic peak at 2θ = 33.3°, corresponding to the (104) plane of cubic hematite (Fe₂O₃). No new crystalline phases were detected upon addition of La or Ba promoters.
- **Observations:** The preservation of the hematite phase across all samples indicates that promoter addition does not alter the primary crystal structure, which is critical for maintaining catalytic activity and stability in FTS.

#### 6.1.2 Particle Size and Morphology
- **Description:** Particle size and morphology were assessed using both XRD (via the Scherrer equation) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), providing complementary insights into crystallite dimensions and nanoparticle distribution.
- **Findings:** The average crystallite size, calculated from XRD peak broadening using the Scherrer equation, was consistent with TEM observations, which revealed nanoparticles in the 10–30 nm range for all catalysts. TEM images confirmed uniform morphology and the absence of significant agglomeration differences among the samples.
- **Observations:** The consistent nanoparticle size across formulations suggests that the microemulsion synthesis method effectively controls particle growth, while promoter addition does not significantly impact morphology.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were measured by N₂ physisorption using the BET method, with results reported in m²/g for surface area and cm³/g for pore volume. These parameters influence reactant accessibility and catalytic efficiency.
- **Findings:** The addition of La, Ba, or their combination led to a decrease in both BET surface area and pore volume compared to the unpromoted catalyst. This reduction is attributed to crystallite aggregation and partial pore blockage induced by the promoters.
- **Observations:** Lower surface area and pore volume in promoted catalysts may limit reactant diffusion but are offset by other beneficial effects, such as enhanced basicity and improved selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Elemental analysis via atomic absorption spectroscopy was used to verify the actual Fe, Cu, La, and Ba content in each catalyst, ensuring accurate formulation.
- **Findings:** The measured elemental ratios closely matched the intended compositions: 100Fe/4Cu, 100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba.
- **Observations:** Accurate elemental composition is crucial for correlating structural properties with catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Reducibility (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was employed to evaluate the reducibility of catalyst components, which affects the formation of active sites during FTS.
- **Findings:** Three main reduction stages were observed: (1) CuO to Cu at low temperature, (2) Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄, and (3) Fe₃O₄ to Fe at higher temperatures. Promoter addition (especially Ba and La) increased the reduction temperatures, indicating suppressed H₂ adsorption and decreased reducibility at lower temperatures.
- **Observations:** Enhanced basicity from promoters makes the catalysts less reducible, which can influence the activation and stability of the active phase.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Basicity (CO₂-TPD)
- **Description:** Surface basicity was quantified by CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD), measuring the amount and strength of basic sites, which are linked to CO adsorption and water-gas shift activity.
- **Findings:** CO₂ desorption increased markedly with promoter addition: 100Fe/4Cu (123 mmol/gcat), 100Fe/4Cu/2La (575 mmol/gcat), 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba (747 mmol/gcat), and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba (1062 mmol/gcat). Ba-promoted catalysts exhibited the highest basicity, with broader and higher-temperature desorption peaks.
- **Observations:** Increased surface basicity correlates with improved CO conversion and selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons, demonstrating a synergistic effect when both La and Ba are present.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 290 °C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.1, and a space velocity of 3 nl/(g·h) over 72 hours, the following CO conversions were observed:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 59.6%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 68.5%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 71.8%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 79.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Ba and La promoters, especially in combination, significantly enhances CO conversion. The dual-promoted catalyst (1Ba/1La) exhibits the highest activity, attributed to synergistic effects that improve CO adsorption and surface basicity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating water–gas shift (WGS) activity. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 17.29%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 20.31%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 23.43%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 25.03%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity increases with promoter addition, with the dual-promoted catalyst showing the highest value, indicating enhanced WGS activity due to increased surface basicity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.96%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 10.44%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 12.32%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 10.81%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition, particularly Ba and La, suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 Product Distribution and C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** Product distribution is assessed by the selectivity toward hydrocarbon fractions (C₂–C₄, C₅–C₁₂, C₁₃–C₁₉, C₁₉⁺). C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of heavier hydrocarbons, reflecting chain growth.
- **Experimental Data:** With promoter addition, C₅–C₁₂ selectivity increases from 15.79% (unpromoted) to 26.46% (Ba-promoted) and 21.80% (dual-promoted), while C₂–C₄ selectivity decreases.
- **Observations and Trends:** The shift toward heavier hydrocarbons with promoter addition demonstrates enhanced chain growth and improved FTS performance.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, represents the probability of chain growth:
  \[
  x_i = (1 - \alpha) \alpha^{i-1}
  \]
  where \( x_i \) is the mole fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( i \).
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 0.61
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 0.71
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 0.66
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 0.68
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition increases α, with Ba having the strongest effect. The dual-promoted catalyst maintains a high α, supporting the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is evaluated by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 72 hours, all catalysts exhibit some decline in CO conversion, but the dual-promoted catalyst shows the slowest deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced stability in promoted catalysts is attributed to increased basicity and improved retention of active sites, with the 1Ba/1La catalyst demonstrating superior long-term performance.
