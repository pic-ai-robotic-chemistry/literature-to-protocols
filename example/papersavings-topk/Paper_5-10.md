## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Na‑FeC*x* catalyst follows a strict sequence to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. The method begins with precursor mixing and co‑precipitation, followed by a defined precipitation–deposition step prior to drying, calcination, and subsequent post‑treatments.

#### • Na‑FeC*x* Catalyst Synthesis
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: 30.0 g FeCl₃⋅6H₂O, 11.9 g FeCl₂⋅4H₂O, and 5.0 mL aqueous HCl solution (12.0 mol L⁻¹) are mixed in 200 mL deionized water.  
   - pH Adjustment: NaOH aqueous solution (2.0 mol L⁻¹) is added dropwise until the pH reaches 10. A pH meter is used for precise control.  
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Procedure: Following initial mixing, the addition of NaOH is continued under constant stirring at room temperature for 1 hour to ensure complete precipitation.  
   - Equipment: Standard laboratory stirrer and pH meter ensure uniform conditions.  
3. Drying  
   - Conditions: The resultant solid is separated via centrifugation and dried at 100 °C overnight.  
4. Calcination  
   - Parameters: The dried material is calcined at 550 °C for 4 hours in a furnace to remove residual organic content.  
5. Additional Post‑treatments  
   - Carbonization: The Na‑Fe₃O₄ precursor is transformed into Na‑FeC*x* by treatment with syngas (CO/H₂ molar ratio of 1:1) at 300 °C for 24 hours in a fixed-bed reactor.
   - Pre‑reduction: A subsequent stage involves pre‑reduction at 400 °C for 6 hours under a pure H₂ flow to activate the catalyst.

#### • Zeolite Synthesis via Hydrothermal Method
- The s‑ZSM‑5 zeolite is synthesized hydrothermally using a precursor mixture of TEOS, TPAOH, and aluminum isopropoxide.
   - Conditions: The synthesis is conducted at 180 °C for 72 hours, yielding zeolites with Si/Al ratios within 26–30.5 and nanosheet morphologies.
- Comparative Analysis: This method offers well‑defined microporous architectures, though its scalability might be limited by extended crystallization times compared to more rapid impregnation or granule mixing techniques.

This structured synthesis approach, with its detailed step order and precise reaction parameters, ensures that the catalyst’s performance—evaluated by CO conversion and olefin selectivity—can be reliably reproduced by researchers.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation of Na‑Fe₃O₄  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 30.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O  
  • 11.9 g FeCl₂·4H₂O  
  • 200 mL deionized water  
  • 5.0 mL HCl (12.0 M)  
  • NaOH aqueous solution (for pH adjustment)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Room temperature stirring  
  • pH adjusted to 10  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O and FeCl₂·4H₂O in a mixture of deionized water and HCl, forming a homogeneous solution.  
  2. Add NaOH dropwise until the pH reaches 10, ensuring uniform precipitation.  
  3. Continue stirring for an additional hour to complete precursor formation.  
- **Key Steps**: Accurate pH adjustment is critical to obtain a reproducible phase.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Carbonization  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Na‑Fe₃O₄ precursor  
  • Syngas (CO/H₂ in a 1:1 molar ratio)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Pre-reduction: 400 °C for 6 hours under pure H₂  
  • Carbonization: 300 °C for 24 hours in syngas  
- **Process**:  
  1. Pre-reduce the Na‑Fe₃O₄ precursor at 400 °C in a H₂ atmosphere.  
  2. Subject the pre-reduced sample to carbonization using syngas at 300 °C for 24 hours to form Na‑FeC*x*.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain precise temperatures and gas flows to ensure optimal carbide formation.

##### Step 3: Zeolite Synthesis and Catalyst Mixing  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Zeolite precursors formulated for s‑ZSM‑5 (targeting Si/Al ratios of 26–30.5)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Hydrothermal synthesis at 180 °C for 3 days  
- **Process**:  
  1. Synthesize s‑ZSM‑5 using standard hydrothermal crystallization protocols.  
  2. Physically granule mix the resulting zeolite with Na‑FeC*x* to achieve a mass ratio of approximately 3, minimizing sodium migration.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete crystallization of the zeolite and uniform granule mixing.

##### Step 4: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  1. Following synthesis, purify the catalyst via centrifugation and wash it repeatedly with deionized water.  
  2. Dry the sample at 100 °C overnight.  
  3. Calcine the dried product at 550 °C for 4 hours to remove any residual organics and stabilize the catalyst structure.

##### Step 5: Activation (if required)  
- **Process**:  
  Optionally, reduce the calcined catalyst under controlled H₂ flow to further expose active sites for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This systematic workflow—from precursor preparation through catalyst activation—ensures reproducibility and alignment with the multistep synthesis parameters critical to achieving high CO conversion and olefin selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for driving the reduction reaction, transforming the iron oxide precursor into the active carbide form.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The pre‐reduction is carried out at 400 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature is sufficient to achieve complete transformation without inducing excessive sintering, thus preserving catalyst morphology.

- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen (H₂) is used to provide the reducing atmosphere.

- **Definition:** The duration of activation is important to allow complete reduction while preventing over-treatment that might lead to structural degradation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated for 6 hours.
- **Observations:** This time period is optimized to achieve full reduction and formation of the active phase without adverse sintering effects. The pre‐reduction conditions have been linked with stable catalytic performance and consistent CO conversion in subsequent catalytic tests.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is defined as the operational temperature at which the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic reaction is conducted, directly influencing syngas activation and the distribution of reaction products. In this study, the reactor was maintained at 260 °C throughout the catalytic tests. This temperature was carefully selected for its ability to promote CO conversion while ensuring the catalyst remained stable over extended periods. Notably, catalyst stability tests demonstrated that under these conditions, the Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5 catalyst retained steady performance after approximately 50 hours (with a CO conversion around 68.1%) and even after 600 hours, indicating minimal deactivation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure is critical as it affects both the concentration of syngas in the reaction environment and the overall selectivity of the hydrocarbon products. The experiments were conducted at a pressure of 2.0 MPa. Maintaining this pressure helps optimize the conditions required for effective CO conversion and favorable hydrocarbon distribution. The high-pressure environment ensures that the feed syngas remains in a suitable phase, facilitating consistent reactivity and catalyst performance.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio is a key parameter that influences the distribution between olefins and alkanes in the product slate. For the Na‑FeC*x*/s‑ZSM‑5 catalyst, the reaction was carried out with an H₂/CO ratio of 1, which is instrumental in favoring olefin formation. Comparative experiments using a higher H₂/CO ratio of 3 with other catalyst derivatives (e.g., Na‑FeC*x*/s‑S1‑OH) revealed that increased hydrogen content can lead to enhanced CO conversion but might also shift the balance towards different hydrocarbon distributions. This parameter is therefore carefully tuned based on the specific catalyst formulation and targeted product profile.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) represents the ratio of the syngas flow rate to the catalyst mass, dictating the exposure time of reactants to the catalyst. In the reported experiments, a GHSV of 2,400 mL·h⁻¹ per g FeC*x* was employed. This setting ensures adequate contact time to achieve high conversion while preventing excessive reactant throughput, which was observed to reduce CO conversion if increased further.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The evolution of crystalline phases during catalyst synthesis and reaction was monitored by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases and assessing structural stability under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the as-synthesized Na‑Fe₃O₄ precursor displayed reflections corresponding to Fe₂O₃ and Fe₃O₄. After carbonization, Na‑FeCₓ* exhibited residual ferric oxide signals, indicating incomplete conversion. Following the Fischer–Tropsch reaction, only Fe₅C₂ peaks were observed, with oxide phases nearly undetectable, confirming complete transformation to the active iron carbide phase. The MFI structure of s‑ZSM‑5 was retained throughout, as verified by characteristic XRD reflections.
- **Observations:** The persistence of Fe₅C₂ after long-term reaction (up to 600 h) and the stability of the zeolite framework demonstrate robust phase integrity, which is critical for sustained catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphological features and particle size distributions were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).
- **Findings:** SEM images revealed well-defined crystal shapes for s‑ZSM‑5 and related zeolites, while TEM confirmed the uniform dispersion and nanoscale size of Na‑FeCₓ* particles, with no significant aggregation. HAADF-STEM and EDS mapping demonstrated homogeneous distribution of Fe and Na within catalyst granules. Granule sizes (e.g., 20–40 mesh, ~0.6 mm diameter) were maintained during mixing and reaction.
- **Observations:** The controlled morphology and particle size distribution are crucial for minimizing sodium migration and optimizing catalyst–zeolite interactions, directly impacting catalytic selectivity.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Sodium Migration
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze surface elemental composition and track sodium migration between Na‑FeCₓ* and zeolite phases.
- **Findings:** Na1s XPS peaks at 1071.8 eV (Na on FeCₓ*) and 1073.3 eV (Na on zeolite) enabled differentiation of sodium environments. Granule-mixed catalysts with larger particles showed minimal sodium migration (peak at 1071.8 eV), while smaller granules and powder-mixed samples exhibited peak shifts up to 1073.3 eV, indicating increased migration.
- **Observations:** The extent of sodium migration is strongly influenced by granule size and mixing method, with excessive migration correlating with reduced olefin selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen sorption (BET) analysis characterized the surface area and porosity of zeolite components, which are vital for reactant diffusion and product desorption.
- **Findings:** While explicit numerical values for the composite catalyst are not provided, nitrogen sorption isotherms confirmed the microporous nature of s‑ZSM‑5 and related zeolites. The analysis was performed using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020M system.
- **Observations:** The high density of accessible micropores in nanosheet zeolites facilitates rapid olefin desorption, enhancing catalytic turnover.

#### 6.1.5 Adsorption and Desorption Properties
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) and in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) were used to probe olefin adsorption strength and desorption kinetics.
- **Findings:** TPD profiles showed that ethene and propene desorbed rapidly from s‑ZSM‑5 and s‑S1‑OH, with over 91% of ethene and 96% of propene removed within 4 minutes, whereas Na‑FeCₓ* retained olefins more strongly. DRIFTS confirmed faster desorption on nanosheet zeolites, as indicated by the rapid decrease of characteristic IR bands.
- **Observations:** The zeolite’s nanochannel architecture is pivotal in promoting swift olefin desorption, thereby suppressing secondary reactions and favoring high olefin selectivity.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Na‑FeCₓ*/s‑ZSM‑5, CO conversion varies with the zeolite/Na‑FeCₓ* mass ratio: 27.3% (0.5), 66.8% (2), 82.5% (3, optimal), and 53.2% (4). Na‑FeCₓ* alone exhibits very low conversion (1.9% at 300 °C). With s‑S1‑OH, conversion ranges from 66.5% (H₂/CO = 1) to 82.6% (H₂/CO = 3).
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing zeolite content enhances CO conversion up to a mass ratio of 3, after which further addition reduces conversion, likely due to dilution of active sites or diffusion limitations. Higher H₂/CO ratios also promote conversion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The CO₂ fraction in products typically exceeds 45% for syngas-to-olefin reactions across all zeolite-promoted catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** High CO₂ selectivity is a general feature of the process, reflecting the significant water-gas shift activity under the tested conditions.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Na‑FeCₓ*/s‑ZSM‑5 achieves CH₄ selectivity below 3.8%; Na‑FeCₓ*/s‑S1‑OH records 6.0% under H₂/CO = 3.
- **Observations and Trends:** Zeolite promoters, especially s‑ZSM‑5, markedly suppress methane formation compared to unpromoted catalysts.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–C₁₀ Olefin Selectivity and Olefin/Alkane Ratio
- **Description:** Selectivity toward C₂–C₁₀ olefins is determined by the fraction of these products among all hydrocarbons. The olefin/alkane (o/a) ratio indicates the preference for olefin formation.
- **Experimental Data:** Na‑FeCₓ*/s‑ZSM‑5 (mass ratio 3) yields 72.0% C₂–C₁₀ olefin selectivity with an o/a ratio of 3.5. At mass ratio 2, the o/a ratio increases to 4.2 (conversion 66.8%). Long-term tests (600 h) maintain 67.8–71.0% olefin selectivity and o/a ratios of 4.2–5.2.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal zeolite loading maximizes both conversion and olefin selectivity. Excessive zeolite reduces conversion but can further increase the o/a ratio.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory model, represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination, shaping the hydrocarbon distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** The LT-FTO process with zeolite promoters narrows the product range to C₁–C₁₀, indicating a lower α compared to classical FTO, which produces broader distributions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Zeolite addition reduces α, favoring light olefins and suppressing long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by the rate of decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Na‑FeCₓ*/s‑ZSM‑5 maintains CO conversion from 68.1% (post-activation) to 67.6% after 600 hours, indicating negligible deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst demonstrates robust long-term stability, with high activity and selectivity sustained over extended operation.
