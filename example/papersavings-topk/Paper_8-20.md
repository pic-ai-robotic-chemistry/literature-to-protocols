## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe@C catalysts involves a hydrothermal preparation of MIL‑101(Fe) followed by controlled pyrolysis, as well as a coprecipitation approach for the unsupported‑Fe catalyst. Both routes employ a systematic sequence of steps—precursor mixing, precipitation–deposition, drying, calcination, and, where applicable, reduction—to ensure reproducibility and precise control over the catalyst structure.

#### Hydrothermal–Pyrolysis Method for Fe@C Catalysts

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Dissolve 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 166 mg terephthalic acid, 5 ml nitric acid, and 70 ml DMF in a single solution.  
   - Stir the solution for 2 h to ensure homogeneity before transferring it into a Teflon‑lined autoclave.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - After the hydrothermal reaction at 120 °C for 48 h, a controlled deposition step is conducted. This involves the adjustment of conditions to promote uniform nucleation of MIL‑101(Fe).

3. Drying  
   - Wash the MIL‑101(Fe) product with DMF and ethanol.  
   - Dry at 80 °C overnight to remove solvent residues.

4. Calcination  
   - Pyrolyze the dried precursor in a flow of 2% C₂H₂ in helium at a heating rate of 5 °C/min for 1 h.  
   - Tailor the temperature to 500, 600, or 700 °C to obtain varying carbon shell structures: amorphous carbon at 500 °C, multilayer graphene (3–5 layers) at 600 °C, and well‑oriented graphite at 700 °C.

5. Post‑treatment  
   - For Fe@C‑600‑H₂, perform a hydrogen reduction at 50 ml/min, 400 °C, for 24 h to further activate the catalyst.

#### Coprecipitation Method for Unsupported‑Fe Catalyst

1. Precursor Mixing  
   - Mix an aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ with ammonia under stirring, maintaining critical concentrations (e.g., 1.5 mmol Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and 1 mmol terephthalic acid equivalents) and adjusting pH to 9.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Add the ammonia dropwise and maintain stirring.  
   - Age the resulting mixture for 2 h to facilitate complete precipitation.

3. Drying & Calcination  
   - Dry the precipitate at 80 °C overnight.  
   - Calcinate at 500 °C for 5 h in static air, followed by a reduction step in hydrogen (100 ml/min) at 350 °C for 24 h.

This methodical approach highlights the efficiency of the hydrothermal–pyrolysis route in generating a protective carbon shell that limits nanoparticle sintering, while the coprecipitation process, albeit more complex, offers valuable comparative insights into catalyst performance and structure. All specified numerical values, reaction times, temperatures, and reagent concentrations have been precisely reported to ensure reproducibility of the synthesis process.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@C Series (Fe@C-500, Fe@C-600, Fe@C-700)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – MIL-101(Fe)
- **Reagents**: 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 166 mg terephthalic acid, 5 ml nitric acid, and 70 ml DMF.
- **Conditions**: Hydrothermal synthesis in a Teflon-lined autoclave at 120 °C for 48 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve all reagents in DMF under stirring to form a homogeneous solution.
  - Transfer the solution to the autoclave and maintain at 120 °C for 48 hours to yield MIL-101(Fe).
- **Key Steps**: Achieve complete dissolution and controlled hydrothermal conditions to ensure a consistent precursor.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly – Controlled Pyrolysis
- **Reagents**: MIL-101(Fe) precursor.
- **Conditions**: Pyrolysis in a tube furnace under a 2% C₂H₂/He atmosphere, with a heating rate of 5 °C/min and a dwell time of 1 hour.
- **Process**:
  - Load MIL-101(Fe) into the furnace.
  - Ramp up the temperature to the target level (500 °C, 600 °C, or 700 °C) at 5 °C/min.
  - Maintain the set temperature for 1 hour to induce a transformation into an Fe@C catalyst featuring an iron phase core encased in a carbon shell.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure steady temperature increase and reliable gas flow to control carbon shell formation.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Process**:
  - Allow the pyrolyzed sample to cool.
  - Wash gently with distilled water to remove residual byproducts.
  - Dry the catalyst at 120 °C for 12 hours.
  - Optionally, perform calcination at 500 °C in static air for 5 hours to further develop the desired phase.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform drying and controlled calcination prevent unwanted phase changes.

##### Step 4: Activation – Fe@C-600-H₂ Catalyst
- **Reagents**: Fe@C-600 obtained from the above steps.
- **Conditions**: Hydrogen reduction in a tube furnace at 400 °C with a H₂ flow rate of 50 ml/min for 24 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Treat Fe@C-600 under H₂ at 400 °C for 24 hours to activate and optimize the iron phase.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain a consistent hydrogen flow and temperature to achieve effective reduction and activation.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Unsupported-Fe Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Formation via Coprecipitation
- **Reagents**: Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ (1 M) and ammonia solution (3 M).
- **Conditions**: Stir at 80 °C, maintaining a pH between 8.2 and 8.5.
- **Process**:
  - Add ammonia dropwise to the Fe(NO₃)₃ solution under continuous stirring.
  - Allow the mixture to age for 2 hours to complete precipitation.
- **Key Steps**: Careful pH regulation is essential for reproducibility.

##### Step 2: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Process**:
  - Wash the precipitate repeatedly with distilled water and filter.
  - Dry at 120 °C for 12 hours.
  - Calcine at 500 °C in static air for 5 hours, then reduce in hydrogen (100 ml/min) at 350 °C for 24 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough washing and controlled thermal treatment to prevent nanoparticle sintering and secure a reproducible catalyst structure.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis involves a dedicated hydrogen reduction treatment applied to the Fe@C-600 sample. This activation step is designed to remove residual oxidic species and promote the formation of the active iron carbide phase without interfering with the evaluation of the core–shell structure.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for ensuring complete reduction of residual iron oxides while preventing excessive sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction treatment is performed at exactly 400 °C.  
- **Observations:** At this temperature, the hydrogen atmosphere effectively converts oxidic species into the catalytically active phases without compromising the integrity of the carbon shell.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas and Flow Rate
- **Definition:** The activation gas provides a reducing environment necessary for the transformation of the precursor phase into the active catalyst form.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen is used, supplied at a precise flow rate of 50 mL/min.  
- **Observations:** This controlled hydrogen flow ensures uniform exposure and reduction across the catalyst, enhancing the exposure of active sites while minimizing risks of local over-reduction or particle sintering.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is crucial to achieve complete reduction without leading to particle agglomeration.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to the hydrogen treatment for 24 hours.  
- **Observations:** The extended activation period allows for thorough reduction and stabilization of the active phase, which is essential for maintaining high catalytic activity and ensuring reproducible performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This hydrogen reduction activation, exclusively applied to the Fe@C-600 catalyst prior to its use in Fischer–Tropsch testing, distinguishes it from the pyrolysis conditions used to form the initial Fe@C catalysts at higher temperatures. The process not only facilitates the conversion of iron species into a more active phase but also preserves the beneficial core–shell structure that is critical for long-chain hydrocarbon production.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** This parameter indicates the operating temperature at which Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is carried out. The temperature plays a crucial role in activating syngas and determining the phase composition of the Fe@C catalysts.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was performed at an exact temperature of 300 °C using a fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** Operating at 300 °C enhances both syngas conversion and the formation of long-chain hydrocarbons while maintaining catalyst stability. Iron-based catalysts under these conditions are noted to be susceptible to deactivation by phase transformation and carbon deposition, but the selected temperature optimizes active phase formation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure affects the concentration of reactants in the reactor, thereby influencing both conversion rates and product distributions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was conducted at a pressure of 2.0 MPa.
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure promotes a higher concentration of syngas, which facilitates the preferential formation of heavier hydrocarbons over methane. This pressure regime, maintained in the fixed-bed setup, is critical for balancing activity and selectivity.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The molar ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide is essential for controlling the hydrocarbon distribution during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A feed gas ratio of H₂/CO equal to 1.0 was employed throughout the catalytic tests.
- **Observations:** This ratio, representative of coal- or biomass-derived syngas, promotes the formation of long-chain (C₅⁺) hydrocarbons while suppressing excessive methane production. Such control of the H₂/CO ratio is pivotal in steering product selectivity.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space–time velocity, expressed as weight hourly space velocity (WHSV), defines the flow rate of reactants relative to the catalyst mass, impacting the contact time and overall conversion.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reported conditions include a WHSV of 8000 mL/(gcat·h) for the MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts and 3000 mL/(gcat·h) for the unsupported-Fe catalyst.
- **Observations:** The differential WHSV values reflect the tailored approach for catalyst evaluation. Higher WHSV for MOF-derived catalysts ensures efficient contact while mitigating carbon buildup, whereas the lower WHSV for unsupported-Fe enhances conversion under its specific operating environment.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and their evolution with pyrolysis temperature were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES), providing insight into the active iron species and their stability.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns reveal that Fe@C-500 contains FeO, Fe@C-600 exhibits h‑Fe₃C (cementite), graphite-carbon, and α-Fe, while Fe@C-700 shows h‑Fe₃C and graphite-carbon with increased crystallinity. Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe are dominated by α-Fe. MES quantifies the h‑Fe₃C content, which increases with pyrolysis temperature: 68.2% (Fe@C-500), 87.2% (Fe@C-600), and 95.5% (Fe@C-700). After hydrogen reduction, Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe are primarily α-Fe.
- **Observations:** Higher pyrolysis temperatures favor the formation of h‑Fe₃C, which remains stable during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). The presence of h‑Fe₃C is linked to enhanced selectivity for long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.1.2 Carbon Shell Structure and Graphitization
- **Description:** The structure and degree of graphitization of the carbon shell were characterized by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and Raman spectroscopy.
- **Findings:** HRTEM images show amorphous carbon shells at 500 °C, concentric 3–5 layer graphene shells at 600 °C (and in Fe@C-600-H₂), and well-oriented graphite shells at 700 °C. Raman spectra confirm these observations: Fe@C-500 displays a weak, single 2D peak (amorphous), Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ exhibit four peaks in the 2D band (3–5 layer graphene), and Fe@C-700 shows merged peaks (graphite). The ID/IG ratios decrease with increasing temperature: 1.10 (Fe@C-500), 0.79 (Fe@C-600), 0.78 (Fe@C-600-H₂), and 0.91 (Fe@C-700).
- **Observations:** Increased graphitization at higher temperatures enhances the stability of encapsulated nanoparticles and inhibits sintering during FTS.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to assess the core–shell morphology and particle size distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images reveal uniform dispersion of iron nanoparticles within the carbon matrix for all Fe@C-x catalysts. Fe@C-600 exhibits the smallest average particle size (5.9 nm) and the narrowest distribution. After FTS, the graphene shell in Fe@C-600 effectively prevents aggregation, maintaining particle size and uniformity.
- **Observations:** The graphene shell is critical for stabilizing active iron species and preventing nanoparticle sintering, as evidenced by minimal particle growth post-reaction.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET method) was employed to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution.
- **Findings:** Fe@C-600, the optimal catalyst, has a BET surface area of 280 m²/g, a pore volume of 0.27 cm³/g, and a mean pore size of 3.8 nm. MOF-derived Fe@C-x catalysts show higher surface areas (135–291 m²/g) compared to unsupported-Fe (15.9 m²/g).
- **Observations:** High surface area and porosity, inherited from the MOF precursor, facilitate better iron dispersion and contribute to superior catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Thermal Stability and Carbon Deposition
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to monitor weight loss during pyrolysis and ligand decomposition.
- **Findings:** MIL-101(Fe) exhibits two main weight loss stages: solvent removal below 300 °C and ligand decomposition between 300–500 °C. In C₂H₂, a plateau at 500–700 °C indicates carbon deposition, with less weight loss than in N₂. Beyond 700 °C, weight increases in C₂H₂ due to excessive carbon deposition.
- **Observations:** The TGA results, combined with Raman data, confirm that higher pyrolysis temperatures promote the formation of more ordered carbon shells, which are essential for catalyst stability and performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (300 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.0, WHSV = 8000 ml/g/h), Fe@C-600-H₂ achieved a CO conversion of 78.7%. Fe@C-600 exhibited 65.0% at 24 h and 74.5% at 184 h. Fe@C-500 and Fe@C-700 showed similar trends, while unsupported-Fe reached 75.3% at 24 h (WHSV = 3000 ml/g/h).
- **Observations and Trends:** The α-Fe dominated Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe catalysts display higher initial CO conversion than h‑Fe₃C dominated Fe@C-x catalysts. Fe@C-600 demonstrates stable or increasing conversion over extended operation, indicating robust performance.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-500, Fe@C-600, and Fe@C-700 exhibit low CH₄ selectivity (10.5–12.4% at 24 h). Fe@C-600-H₂ shows a significantly higher value (23.4%), while unsupported-Fe reaches 28.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** h‑Fe₃C dominated catalysts consistently suppress methane formation compared to α-Fe or unsupported-Fe, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, reflecting long-chain hydrocarbon production.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-500, Fe@C-600, and Fe@C-700 achieve high C₅⁺ selectivity (59.0–60.4%). In contrast, Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe yield lower C₅⁺ selectivity (values not explicitly stated for Fe@C-600-H₂; unsupported-Fe: 14.7%).
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of h‑Fe₃C and an optimized carbon shell (graphene in Fe@C-600) enhances long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity, while α-Fe phases promote shorter-chain products.

#### 6.2.4 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY measures the intrinsic activity of the catalyst, defined as the amount of CO converted per gram of iron per second:
  \[
  \text{FTY} = \frac{\text{mol CO converted}}{\text{g Fe} \cdot \text{s}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-x catalysts (500–700 °C) show FTY values of 161–176 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹. Fe@C-600-H₂ achieves 203.0 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹, while unsupported-Fe records 39.5 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts exhibit much higher FTY than unsupported-Fe, with Fe@C-600-H₂ displaying the highest activity due to its α-Fe phase.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination, influencing hydrocarbon distribution. It is derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model by plotting ln(Wₙ/n) versus carbon number (n).
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, h‑Fe₃C dominated Fe@C-x catalysts are reported to have higher α, correlating with elevated C₅⁺ selectivity and reduced CH₄ selectivity compared to α-Fe dominated Fe@C-600-H₂.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher h‑Fe₃C content enhances chain growth probability, favoring long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by the deactivation rate, calculated as the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate (h}^{-1}\text{)} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-600 shows negligible deactivation (stable CO conversion up to 196 h). Fe@C-500 and Fe@C-700 exhibit deactivation rates of 13.1% and 20.1%, respectively, while unsupported-Fe deactivates rapidly (47.9%).
- **Observations and Trends:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 effectively prevents nanoparticle aggregation, ensuring superior long-term stability compared to other shell types or unsupported catalysts.
