## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section outlines the synthesis techniques employed for preparing Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, with an emphasis on reproducibility and clarity. Two primary approaches are reported: the coprecipitation method for CoMnC synthesis and the modified Stöber method for preparing silica-supported variants.

#### Coprecipitation Method (CoMnC Catalyst)
1. **Precursor Mixing**  
   • Reagents:  Co(NO₃)₂⋅6H₂O and 50 wt% Mn(NO₃)₂ are dissolved in deionized water to form solution A.  
   • Equipment: A beaker and pH meter are used to ensure accurate reagent measurements.  
   • Conditions: pH is adjusted to ~8 using aqueous ammonia.
2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   • Procedure: Following mixing, a simultaneous dropwise addition of support precursor is performed under vigorous stirring to promote uniform nucleation.  
   • Observation: Homogeneous precipitation is indicated by the gradual formation of a solid phase.
3. **Drying**  
   • Conditions: The precipitate is dried at 100 °C for 10 hours.
4. **Calcination**  
   • Conditions: Calcination is carried out at 330 °C for 3 hours in an air atmosphere to convert the mixed oxide to the appropriate phase.
5. **Post‑treatment**  
   • Carbonization/Reduction: A pre‑reduction step is performed at 300 °C for 5 hours in pure H₂ flow (30 mL/min) to facilitate formation of the active carbide phase.  
   • Final Step: The activated catalyst is physically mixed with hydrophobic PDVB; typical weight ratios reported are 1:1, with alternatives (0.5 and 1.5) evaluated for optimizing performance.

#### Modified Stöber Method (Silica-Supported Variants)
1. **Precursor Preparation and Mixing**  
   • Reagents: Tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS, 7.49 g) and Dimethyldiethoxysilane (DEMS, 0.592 g) are dissolved in a mixed liquor of 80 mL ethanol, 100 mL water, and 6 mL aqueous ammonia.  
   • Equipment: Mixing is performed in a beaker with continuous stirring; a pH meter monitors the reaction environment.
2. **Aging and Liquor Removal**  
   • Conditions: The mixture is stirred at room temperature for 12 hours before the liquor is removed via vacuum distillation.
3. **Drying**  
   • Conditions: The resulting solid is dried at 100 °C overnight to yield amorphous silica modified with hydrophobic methyl groups.

This sequential ordering—from precursor mixing through to post‑processing—ensures experimental reproducibility and optimal dispersion of active phases within the catalyst support networks.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: CoMnC Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 29.4 g of Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and 17.9 g of 50 wt% Mn(NO₃)₂ are dissolved in deionized water to form solution A. Simultaneously, sodium carbonate solution (solution B) is prepared.
- **Conditions**: Both solutions are maintained at 30 °C under vigorous stirring. The pH is carefully controlled at approximately 8 by dropwise addition of solution B to solution A.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the metal nitrates in deionized water at 30 °C until a homogeneous solution is achieved.
  - Simultaneously add sodium carbonate dropwise while stirring vigorously to adjust and maintain pH ~8.
  - Continue stirring the resulting mixture for 1 hour and then allow it to age for 2 hours at the same temperature.
- **Key Steps**: Precise dropwise addition and constant pH monitoring ensure complete precursor formation.

##### Step 2: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Purification**:
  - Centrifuge the aged suspension to collect the precipitate.
  - Wash the precipitate with a sufficient quantity of deionized water.
- **Drying**:
  - Dry the washed precipitate at 100 °C for 10 hours, ensuring granule integrity.
- **Calcination**:
  - Calcine the dried precursor at 330 °C for 3 hours in an air atmosphere. Although the ramp rate is not specified, adherence to temperature and time is crucial for complete conversion to CoMn oxide.
- **Key Steps**: Controlled drying and calcination consolidate the formation of the oxide precursor.

##### Step 3: Activation
- **Conditions**: Reduce the calcined material under a pure H₂ flow at 300 °C for 5 hours (30 mL/min flow rate) to convert the oxide to active CoMn carbide (CoMnC).
- **Key Steps**: Strict temperature and flow control during activation ensure reproducibility.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: PDVB Physical Mixing
  
##### Step 1: PDVB Synthesis
- **Reagents**: 10 g of divinylbenzene (DVB) and 0.5 g of AIBN.
- **Conditions & Process**:
  - Stir the mixture at room temperature for 1 hour for initiation.
  - Transfer the mixture into an autoclave and treat at 100 °C for 24 hours.
  - Subsequently, wash the solid product with methanol and dry overnight at 100 °C.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Process**:
  - Physically mix the pre-reduced CoMnC with PDVB using a powder-mixing technique. Weight ratios of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 are explored, with a 1:1 ratio found optimal.
- **Key Notes**: This physical mixing preserves the active carbide phase and enhances water desorption during CO hydrogenation, a critical factor for improved catalyst performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation step for the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis was meticulously designed to convert the cobalt–manganese oxide precursor into its active carbide form, critical for efficient CO hydrogenation. This activation is carried out as a dedicated pre-reaction treatment and is distinct from the milder conditions used in characterization procedures.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is pivotal for reducing the oxide precursor and initiating the formation of the active CoMn carbide phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is pre-treated at 300 °C.
- **Observations:** Maintaining 300 °C ensures complete reduction while preserving the catalyst’s nanostructure, thereby promoting uniform metal dispersion, which is essential for catalytic durability and performance.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Activation duration is critical for ensuring that the reduction of the catalyst precursor is thorough, thereby guaranteeing the formation of the desired active phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is treated in pure hydrogen (H₂) for 5 hours.
- **Observations:** A 5-hour pre-treatment under H₂ allows for complete conversion of the precursor, leading to a stable and uniformly reduced catalyst surface that is primed for high activity during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas used during activation is responsible for reducing the catalyst precursor, transforming it into its active metallic state.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen (H₂) is used during the activation process.
- **Observations:** The exclusive use of H₂ prevents the formation of undesirable oxide or hydroxide species, ensuring that all active sites are available for subsequent CO hydrogenation. #### 5.1.4 Activation Pressure and Reactor Setup

- **Observations:** Atmospheric pressure supports the optimal reduction kinetics while the fixed-bed reactor configuration facilitates an even flow of H₂, contributing to uniform activation of the catalyst.

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction section details the precise conditions employed during the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation using the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst. The experimental setup was designed to ensure reproducibility in product formation while providing insights into catalyst behavior under controlled conditions.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The catalytic test was conducted at a reaction temperature of 250 °C. This temperature is critical as it enables the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst to achieve enhanced CO conversion (approximately 63.5%) while promoting the formation of light olefins. The operation at 250 °C was found to maintain catalyst stability over extended periods (up to 120 hours) with minimal deactivation, ensuring consistent performance.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
The reaction pressure was maintained at 0.1 MPa. This parameter is essential in controlling the syngas concentration and product selectivity during CO hydrogenation. The controlled low-pressure environment contributes to the favorable production of light olefins while limiting undesirable side reactions such as excessive methane formation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
A H₂/CO ratio of 2 was employed during the experimental tests. This ratio plays a significant role in directing the hydrocarbon distribution towards light olefins over heavier hydrocarbons or methane. By carefully balancing the syngas composition, the catalyst system harnesses optimal reactivity, which is evident in the high selectivity for olefins (around 71.4%) reported in the study.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) was set to 1800 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹. This parameter ensures an appropriate residence time for the reactants in the fixed-bed reactor, providing sufficient contact with the catalyst while avoiding excessive CO conversion diminutions observed at higher GHSV values. Notably, although higher velocities (e.g., 3600 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹) have been explored and were associated with decreased CO conversion rates, the chosen condition was optimized to balance conversion efficiency and catalyst longevity.

Together, these carefully controlled reaction conditions (temperature, pressure, H₂/CO ratio, and GHSV) ensure reproducibility, effective syngas conversion, and enhanced selectivity in the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis process with the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structural Integrity
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and structural stability of the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst were assessed using X-ray diffraction (XRD). This analysis is critical for confirming the presence and preservation of active carbide phases, which are essential for Fischer–Tropsch activity.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst revealed distinct peaks corresponding to cobalt carbide (Co₂C), as identified by standard PDF references (#65-1457 for Co₂C). The addition of PDVB did not introduce new phases or alter the diffraction pattern of CoMnC, either before or after reaction. After water treatment, the bare CoMnC catalyst exhibited diminished Co₂C peaks and the emergence of CoO (PDF #01-1227), indicating oxidation. In contrast, the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst maintained an unchanged XRD pattern, confirming the preservation of the carbide phase.
- **Observations:** The physical mixing with hydrophobic PDVB effectively protects the active carbide phase from oxidation, ensuring structural integrity and sustained catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to examine the morphology, granule integrity, and particle dispersion of the catalyst. These techniques provide insight into the physical arrangement and stability of catalyst and promoter phases.
- **Findings:** SEM images showed that CoMnC and PDVB granules were tightly packed yet disordered, with intergranular distances ranging from nanometers to micrometers. TEM analysis of CoMnC particles separated from the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst after reaction indicated that the morphology and particle size remained consistent with the as-prepared CoMnC, demonstrating that physical mixing with PDVB did not alter the catalyst’s structure. PDVB granules retained their integrity after reaction, with no evidence of melting or coating.
- **Observations:** The catalyst’s morphology and particle size distribution are preserved upon physical mixing, confirming that the enhancement in catalytic performance is not due to morphological changes.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Water-droplet contact angle measurements were conducted to evaluate the hydrophobicity of the catalyst surface, a key factor in water management during CO hydrogenation.
- **Findings:** The CoMnC/PDVB catalyst exhibited a significantly higher water-droplet contact angle compared to bare CoMnC, indicating increased surface hydrophobicity due to the presence of PDVB.
- **Observations:** Enhanced hydrophobicity facilitates rapid water desorption from the catalyst surface, reducing competitive adsorption and promoting higher CO conversion and olefin selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Thermal Stability of PDVB
- **Description:** The thermal stability of the PDVB promoter was assessed using thermogravimetric-mass spectrometry (TG-MS) and thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC), ensuring its integrity under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** TG-MS analysis showed that PDVB decomposition begins at approximately 370 °C, while TG-DSC indicated an endothermal signal starting at about 300 °C. At the reaction temperature of 250 °C, PDVB exhibited no detectable weight loss or decomposition.
- **Observations:** PDVB remains stable under Fischer–Tropsch reaction conditions, confirming its suitability as a hydrophobic promoter in the catalyst system.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and Product Formation (FTIR)
- **Description:** In situ Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to monitor CO adsorption/desorption and the formation of reaction intermediates and products on the catalyst surface.
- **Findings:** FTIR spectra of CoMnC/PDVB during CO hydrogenation displayed characteristic bands at 2717–2955 cm⁻¹, 1320–1527 cm⁻¹, and ~1039 cm⁻¹, corresponding to olefin species. Water-related signals at ~1600 cm⁻¹ and 3400–3600 cm⁻¹ were extremely weak on CoMnC/PDVB, indicating efficient water removal.
- **Observations:** The rapid desorption of water on the hydrophobic catalyst surface enables sustained CO adsorption and efficient olefin formation, directly correlating with improved catalytic performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flows of CO in the feed and product streams, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (250°C, 0.1 MPa, H₂/CO = 2), the bare CoMnC catalyst achieves a CO conversion of 32.2%. Physical mixing with PDVB (1:1 weight ratio) nearly doubles the conversion to 63.5%. Varying the PDVB/CoMnC ratio from 0.5 to 1.5 increases conversion from 56.4% to 70.5%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal PDVB/CoMnC ratio is 1.0, balancing high conversion and selectivity. The enhancement is attributed to PDVB’s hydrophobicity, which accelerates water desorption and maintains active CO adsorption sites.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, defined as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For CoMnC/PDVB at a GHSV of 3600 mL·gCoMnC⁻¹·h⁻¹, CO₂ selectivity is 46.0–48.5%. When the H₂/CO ratio is increased to 3.6 and CO₂ is co-fed, selectivity drops to 23.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of PDVB does not significantly increase CO₂ selectivity under standard conditions, indicating that the promotional effect is not due to enhanced water–gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity reflects the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity remains below 5% for CoMnC/PDVB (e.g., 4.8% at 1:1 PDVB/CoMnC).
- **Observations and Trends:** Low methane formation demonstrates effective suppression of undesired side reactions, favoring olefin production.

#### 6.2.4 Light Olefin (C₂–C₄=) Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity to light olefins (C₂–C₄=) is the percentage of converted CO forming C₂–C₄ olefins.
- **Experimental Data:** At a 1:1 PDVB/CoMnC ratio, light olefin selectivity reaches 71.4%. At ratios of 0.5 and 1.5, selectivity is 62.8% and 64.5%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest light olefin selectivity coincides with the optimal PDVB/CoMnC ratio, indicating a synergistic effect between conversion and selectivity.

#### 6.2.5 C₅⁺ Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity denotes the fraction of C₅ and higher hydrocarbons among products.
- **Experimental Data:** For CoMnC/PDVB, 92% of the C₅–C₈ fraction consists of olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst system favors longer-chain olefin formation, further supporting the chain growth mechanism.

#### 6.2.6 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation in the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, with values closer to 1 indicating longer hydrocarbon chains.
- **Experimental Data:** The α value increases upon PDVB addition, consistent with the observed shift toward higher C₃⁺ and C₅⁺ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced α values correlate with improved water removal and reduced inhibition of chain growth.

#### 6.2.7 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over time. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} \times \text{time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 120 hours of continuous operation, CoMnC/PDVB maintains an average CO conversion of 64.7%, with only a slight decrease to 62.8% and stable light olefin selectivity (~70%).
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibits minimal deactivation, confirming excellent long-term durability and operational stability.
