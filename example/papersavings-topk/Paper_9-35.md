## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article details three distinct synthesis methods for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, each incorporating precise processing steps to ensure reproducibility.

#### Fe@Si Synthesis Method
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Dissolve 7.39 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and 7.21 g PVP in 800 mL anhydrous ethanol at room temperature. Stir for 5 h until a yellow suspension indicates the formation of Fe₂O₃ precursors.  
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   • Transfer the mixture to a stainless autoclave and perform a solvothermal treatment at 180 °C for 18 h under stirring.  
3. SiO₂ Shell Formation (Stöber Process)  
   • Prepare a solution with 1656 mL deionized water and 1276 mL ethanol. Add 144 mL NH₃∙H₂O (25%) under continuous stirring, followed by 6 g CTAB.  
   • Slowly add 25 mL TEOS over 6 h, then stir the mixture for an additional 48 h to complete hydrolysis and shell formation.  
4. Drying and Calcination  
   • Collect the product by filtration, wash thoroughly, dry at 120 °C for 24 h, and finally calcine at 550 °C for 6 h.

#### Fe/Si Synthesis Method
1. Precursor Mixing  
   • Impregnate 10.0 g m‑SiO₂ with a 2 M ethanol solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ for 5 h to ensure uniform metal distribution.  
2. Autoclave Treatment  
   • Transfer the impregnated mixture to a Teflon‑lined stainless steel autoclave and heat it at 180 °C for 18 h, facilitating iron oxide formation within the support.  
3. Post‑processing  
   • Filter and wash with distilled water and anhydrous ethanol. Dry at 120 °C for 24 h followed by calcination at 550 °C for 6 h.

#### Fe‑Si Synthesis Method
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Dissolve 20.6 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and 31.7 g silica sol (30 wt.%) in distilled water.  
   • Simultaneously add 200 mL NH₃∙H₂O (25%) to maintain a constant pH of 9.0 ± 0.1 at 85 °C while stirring continuously, leading to the formation of a precipitate.  
2. Drying and Calcination  
   • Filter, wash, and dry the precipitate at 120 °C for 24 h, then calcine at 550 °C for 6 h.

Throughout, the article emphasizes exact control of temperatures, durations, pH values, and reagent concentrations. The use of PVP in Fe@Si prevents nanoparticle agglomeration, while CTAB directs pore formation. In contrast, the impregnation and co‑precipitation routes (Fe/Si and Fe‑Si, respectively) highlight variations in Fe–SiO₂ interactions, synthesis complexity, scalability, and cost, ensuring that each method can be reliably reproduced for Fischer–Tropsch catalyst development.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Si

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation for Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles  
- **Reagents**: 7.39 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O, 7.21 g PVP, 800 mL anhydrous ethanol.  
- **Conditions**: Solvothermal synthesis at 180 °C for 18 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and PVP in anhydrous ethanol under vigorous stirring to form a homogeneous solution.  
  - Transfer the solution to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heat at 180 °C for 18 hours.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and control of temperature to prevent agglomeration, with PVP acting as a stabilizer.

##### Step [2]: Formation of SiO₂ Shell via the Stöber Method  
- **Reagents**: 25 mL TEOS, 144 mL NH₃⋅H₂O, 1656 mL deionized water.  
- **Conditions**: Room temperature, continuous stirring for 48 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Add TEOS to the dispersion of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.  
  - Maintain stirring for 48 hours to enable the hydrolysis of TEOS and controlled SiO₂ shell formation.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain constant pH and stirring to achieve uniform pore structure and prevent nanoparticle sintering.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe/Si

##### Step [1]: Catalyst Assembly Using Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g m‑SiO₂, Fe(NO₃)₃ dissolved in a 2 M ethanol solution.  
- **Conditions**: Impregnation for 5 hours, followed by autoclave treatment at 180 °C for 18 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Impregnate m‑SiO₂ with the ethanol solution under controlled conditions.  
  - Subject the mixture to autoclave treatment in a Teflon-lined vessel ensuring thorough impregnation.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitoring the impregnation time and autoclave parameters is critical for reproducibility.

#### Catalyst Preparation [3]: Fe‑Si via Co‑Precipitation

- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O, silica sol, and NH₃⋅H₂O.  
- **Conditions**: Co‑precipitation at 85 °C, maintaining pH ≈9.0 ±0.1; drying at 120 °C; calcination at 550 °C for 6 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Simultaneously add Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol while introducing NH₃⋅H₂O to achieve the target pH.  
  - Filter and wash the precipitate, then dry at 120 °C before calcining.  
- **Key Steps**: Strict pH monitoring is essential to ensure the proper interaction between Fe species and SiO₂.

#### Common Steps for All Catalysts

- **Purification**: Follow with thorough washing using deionized water and anhydrous ethanol, along with filtration.  
- **Drying & Calcination**: Dry the material under controlled conditions and calcine at 550 °C for 6 hours in an air atmosphere to complete catalyst formation.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: XRD was employed to determine the crystalline phases of the catalysts, such as hematite, iron carbides, and Fe₃O₄, by comparison with standard JCPDF data.
- **Instruments and Models**: D8 ADVANCE diffractometer utilizing Co Kα radiation.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were first degassed under vacuum at 90 °C for 120 minutes and then at 350 °C for 480 minutes. Measurements were recorded using an acceleration voltage of 35 kV and a current of 40 mA, with a 2θ scan range of 30°–90° and a step size of 0.04°.
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample mounting and instrument calibration are critical to ensure accurate phase identification.

### 4.2 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Energy‑Dispersive X‑ray (EDX) Mapping
- **Operational Description**: TEM and EDX mapping were used to assess the catalyst morphology, particle dispersion, and to confirm the integrity of the SiO₂ shell.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Talos F200X operated at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Catalyst samples were dispersed in ethanol for 20 minutes and subsequently deposited onto a carbon membrane for imaging.
- **Key Notes**: Ensuring optimal electron beam alignment is essential to avoid artifacts and provide clear elemental distribution.

### 4.3 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: N₂ physisorption was utilized to determine the surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: ASAP 2420 instrument, conducted at −196 °C.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Prior to analysis, samples were degassed similarly to the XRD procedure.
- **Key Notes**: BET data, including hysteresis loop analysis, effectively differentiate pore structures among catalysts.

### 4.4 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: H₂‑TPR was performed to evaluate the reduction behavior and quantify H₂ consumption.
- **Instruments and Models**: U-type tube reactor equipped with a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Approximately 100 mg of catalyst was subjected to a 10% H₂/90% Ar gas flow at 50 mL/min, with the temperature ramped from 50 °C to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Key Notes**: The removal of water by isopropyl alcohol gel ensures clearer observation of reduction peaks.

### 4.5 Mössbauer Spectroscopy (MES)
- **Operational Description**: MES quantified the phase compositions and oxidation states (including iron carbides and Fe₃O₄) by analyzing spectral components.
- **Instruments and Models**: MR-351 constant-acceleration Mössbauer spectrometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were conducted at 50 K using a triangular reference signal.
- **Key Notes**: Calibration with an α‑Fe foil (330 kOe) is crucial for precise spectral interpretation.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments is systematically executed as a pre‐reaction treatment to transform the iron oxide precursors into an active phase. The activation conditions are strictly defined to ensure that the catalysts achieve a uniform and reproducible pre‐active state.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for partially reducing iron oxides without inducing excessive carburization, thereby preparing the catalyst for subsequent iron carbide formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are activated at 350 °C.
- **Observations:** This moderate temperature ensures a controlled reduction process that avoids over‐reduction while initiating the transformation of Fe₂O₃ into intermediate species.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The pressure during activation influences the gas–solid interactions and reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is performed at atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Using atmospheric pressure offers a gentle and uniform reduction environment, minimizing risks of rapid or uneven catalyst reduction and sintering.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition drives the reduction and pre-carburization reaction by providing a controlled reducing environment.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with an H₂/CO ratio of 2 is employed.
- **Observations:** The H₂-rich syngas facilitates the partial reduction of iron oxides, setting the stage for the formation of catalytically active iron carbide phases while maintaining catalyst stability.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The activation duration is essential for ensuring that the catalyst’s structural transformation is complete prior to the catalytic test.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation step is maintained for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** This extended exposure at 350 °C allows sufficient time for the reduction process to proceed uniformly across the catalyst, with the moderate temperature and prolonged duration designed to avoid sintering. After activation, the catalyst bed is cooled to 200 °C before the introduction of the reaction gas.

These carefully delineated activation parameters—temperature, pressure, gas composition, and time—are exclusively applied during the catalyst pre-treatment phase in the fixed-bed reactor, ensuring reproducibility and reliable performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter for syngas activation and the overall catalytic performance during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. In this study, the reactor was maintained at 280 °C. This temperature provides sufficient thermal energy to overcome activation barriers and facilitates the conversion of syngas into products. Under this condition, the catalysts—Fe@Si, Fe/Si, and Fe‑Si—exhibited distinct performance trends. While Fe/Si and Fe@Si showed high initial CO conversion that gradually declined due to catalyst deactivation (likely related to oxidation of active phases), Fe‑Si demonstrated increasing activity over time, indicating prolonged catalyst activation and better stability.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The total system pressure was set at 2.0 MPa. High pressure enhances the concentration of syngas in the reactor, favoring reaction kinetics by increasing the number of molecular collisions. This elevated pressure also promotes the formation of heavier hydrocarbons as it facilitates chain growth reactions. The selected pressure condition was observed to contribute to the superior catalytic performance of the catalysts under evaluation by providing an environment conducive to both catalyst activation and sustained conversion rates.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
A feed gas ratio of H₂ to CO of 2 was established. This ratio is instrumental in dictating the product distribution in Fischer–Tropsch reactions. A hydrogen-rich environment not only aids in catalyst reduction and activation but also influences the selectivity of the produced hydrocarbons. The condition was correlated with the promotion of lighter hydrocarbons, particularly an increase in methane formation under certain catalyst configurations, while still supporting olefin production depending on catalyst type.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) utilized in the fixed-bed reactor was 5000 mL∙h⁻¹∙g_cat⁻¹. GHSV directly determines the residence time of the reactants in contact with the catalyst. The chosen flow rate ensured a balance between adequate contact time and minimal catalyst deactivation due to prolonged exposure, allowing consistent evaluation of catalyst performance across different reaction periods.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** Crystal phases and structural evolution were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a scan range of 30°–90° (2θ) and a step size of 0.04°. This method identifies the presence and transformation of iron oxide and carbide phases, which are critical for Fischer-Tropsch catalyst performance.
- **Findings:** The as-prepared catalysts exhibited characteristic α‑Fe₂O₃ (hematite) peaks at 2θ = 38.7°, 41.7°, 63.9°, and 74.1°. After activation and Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS), XRD patterns revealed the disappearance of hematite peaks and the emergence of iron carbide and Fe₃O₄ peaks, particularly pronounced in Fe@Si and Fe/Si. In Fe‑Si, broader peaks indicated more amorphous iron oxides and smaller crystallites.
- **Observations:** The transformation from α‑Fe₂O₃ to iron carbides was more extensive in Fe@Si and Fe/Si, correlating with higher reduction and carburization degrees. The Fe‑Si catalyst’s strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction suppressed carbide formation, as evidenced by weaker carbide peaks.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping were employed to assess particle morphology, size, and elemental distribution. These techniques elucidate the effectiveness of synthesis strategies in controlling aggregation and dispersion.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed Fe@Si comprised Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles (average size 50.4 nm) embedded and isolated within a highly porous SiO₂ shell, maintaining discrete particles even after reaction. EDX mapping confirmed co-location of Fe and Si. Fe/Si exhibited significant agglomeration of Fe₂O₃ particles, while Fe‑Si displayed even distribution of iron species within the SiO₂ matrix, attributed to strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively inhibited sintering and aggregation, preserving particle size and dispersion. In contrast, Fe/Si suffered from particle growth, and Fe‑Si’s strong interaction promoted uniform dispersion but with some aggregation post-reaction.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET) measurements provided quantitative data on surface area, pore volume, and pore size, which influence reactant diffusion and catalytic activity.
- **Findings:** 
  - Fe@Si: BET surface area = 652 m²/g, pore volume = 0.64 cm³/g, pore size = 2.62 nm
  - Fe/Si: BET surface area = 642 m²/g, pore volume = 0.55 cm³/g, pore size = 2.55 nm
  - Fe‑Si: BET surface area = 295 m²/g, pore volume = 0.46 cm³/g, pore size = 6.28 nm
- **Observations:** The high surface areas and small, uniform pores in Fe@Si and Fe/Si result from CTAB templating, while Fe‑Si’s lower surface area and larger pores reflect the co-precipitation synthesis and strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂‑TPR) assessed the reducibility of iron species and the influence of Fe–SiO₂ interactions on reduction pathways.
- **Findings:** Total H₂ consumption (mol H₂/mol Fe): Fe@Si – 0.95, Fe/Si – 1.00, Fe‑Si – 0.42. Fe@Si and Fe/Si underwent more extensive reduction, while Fe‑Si’s strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction suppressed further reduction.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si hindered H₂O removal during reduction, limiting reduction degree despite weaker Fe–SiO₂ interaction. Fe‑Si’s strong interaction stabilized iron species but reduced overall reducibility.

#### 6.1.5 Phase Composition and Oxidation State
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified iron phase compositions and oxidation states, providing insight into the chemical environment of active sites.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra identified Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺ superparamagnetic ions, Fe₃O₄, and iron carbides. After activation and FTS, Fe@Si and Fe/Si showed higher iron carbide content, while Fe‑Si retained more oxidic species due to hindered reduction and carburization.
- **Observations:** The extent of iron carbide formation directly correlated with catalyst synthesis method and Fe–SiO₂ interaction, impacting catalytic performance and stability.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO entering and exiting the reactor, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standardized conditions (280 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹), the CO conversion values at 48 h on stream were:
  - Fe@Si: 47.9%
  - Fe/Si: 55.7%
  - Fe‑Si: 29.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si exhibited higher initial CO conversion but showed gradual deactivation over time. In contrast, Fe‑Si demonstrated increasing CO conversion with time on stream, attributed to continuous carburization of iron species.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicative of water–gas shift (WGS) activity. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity at 48 h on stream:
  - Fe@Si: 24.3%
  - Fe/Si: 27.1%
  - Fe‑Si: 10.5%
- **Observations and Trends:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si increases local H₂O partial pressure, enhancing the WGS reaction and resulting in higher CO₂ selectivity compared to Fe‑Si.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity at 48 h on stream:
  - Fe@Si: 24.4%
  - Fe/Si: 22.4%
  - Fe‑Si: 13.2%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@Si shows the highest CH₄ selectivity, attributed to the confinement effect of the SiO₂ shell, which promotes secondary hydrogenation and restricts heavier hydrocarbon desorption.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, reflecting chain growth. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{\text{C in C}_5^+ \text{ products}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivity at 48 h on stream:
  - Fe@Si: 27.2%
  - Fe/Si: 28.1%
  - Fe‑Si: 41.2%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe‑Si exhibits the highest C₅⁺ selectivity, indicating superior chain growth capability, likely due to better iron dispersion and less confinement.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, quantifies the probability of chain growth versus termination:
  \[
  \text{ASF:} \quad W_n = n(1-\alpha)^2 \alpha^{n-1}
  \]
  where \( \alpha \) ranges from 0 to 1.
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values:
  - Fe@Si: 1.4
  - Fe/Si: 1.5
  - Fe‑Si: 1.1
- **Observations and Trends:** Although the reported α values exceed the typical ASF range, the trend indicates that Fe/Si favors longer chain growth, while Fe‑Si, despite a lower α, achieves higher C₅⁺ selectivity due to its structural features.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si showed declining CO conversion with time, while Fe‑Si exhibited increasing activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The deactivation of Fe@Si and Fe/Si is attributed to iron carbide oxidation by H₂O, whereas Fe‑Si’s stability and activation are linked to continuous carburization and strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction.
