## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fischer–Tropsch catalyst synthesis for the Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 catalyst is based on an in situ carburization route that utilizes a Raney iron precursor and subsequent promoter addition. The method emphasizes reproducibility and precise control of reaction parameters.

#### Catalyst Synthesis via In Situ Carburization

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Iron–aluminium alloy powder (50:50 by weight) is introduced into an 8 mol⁻¹ KOH solution.  
   • The mixture is stirred and heated to 70°C, ensuring effective dissolution of aluminum.  
   • Equipment: A standard laboratory flask with a controlled heating setup.  
   • Observations: Formation of a porous Raney iron precursor, verified through extensive washing.

2. Washing and (Precipitation–Deposition)  
   • Although no distinct precipitation–deposition step is employed, the precursor is thoroughly washed with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual K⁺ and AlO₂⁻ ions.

3. Drying and Promoter Addition  
   • The unpromoted precursor is dried in an argon flow at room temperature for 6 hours.  
   • For Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2, manganese is introduced via wet impregnation using a Mn(NO₃)₂ solution immediately following aluminum removal.  
   • Post‐impregnation, drying is carried out in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 hours to ensure uniform promoter distribution.

4. Passivation and In Situ Carburization  
   • The dried precursor is passivated in a flow of 1% O₂/He at room temperature for 20 hours.  
   • Carburization is initiated by ramping the temperature at 1°C min⁻¹ up to 350°C under a syngas flow (H₂/CO/He = 100/3.2/21.8).  
   • A dwelling period of 6 hours at 350°C completes the conversion to phase‑pure χ‑Fe5C2.

### Comparative Analysis of the Synthesis Approach

• Advantages:  
  – The single-step in situ carburization simplifies synthesis, minimizing the need for extensive post‑synthesis treatments.  
  – Standard laboratory equipment suffices, improving scalability and lowering synthesis complexity.  

• Disadvantages:  
  – The procedure demands strict control of reaction conditions (temperature, gas composition, and duration) to ensure uniform phase purity.  

This structured approach—detailing each step with precise reagent concentrations, temperatures, durations, and equipment—ensures that another researcher can exactly replicate the catalyst synthesis procedure and validate the reported performance results.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Iron–aluminium alloy powder (50:50 by weight)  
  • 8 mol⁻¹ KOH solution  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Heat the KOH solution to 70°C under continuous stirring to ensure complete dissolution of aluminium.  
- **Process**:  
  • Disperse the iron–aluminium alloy powder into the heated 8 mol⁻¹ KOH solution.  
  • Maintain stirring at 70°C until the aluminium is effectively leached out.  
  • Remove residual K⁺ and AlO₂⁻ ions by washing the resultant iron-rich solid ten times with deionized water and seven times with ethanol.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Rigorous washing to ensure a clean Raney iron precursor.  

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Raney iron precursor (from Step 1)  
  • Mn(NO₃)₂ solution (for 0.5–12.5 wt% manganese addition)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Perform wet impregnation immediately post washing under controlled conditions (stirring at room temperature).  
- **Process**:  
  • Introduce the Mn(NO₃)₂ solution dropwise into the Raney iron precursor to ensure uniform dispersion of manganese.  
  • After sufficient mixing, allow the mixture to stabilize for impregnation to occur.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Immediate manganese addition to maintain precursor integrity.

##### Step 3: Purification and Drying  
- **Process**:  
  • Following impregnation, remove any excess solution via centrifugation or filtration as applicable.  
  • Dry the sample in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 hours to remove residual solvents without altering the active sites.

##### Step 4: Activation via In Situ Carburization  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Use a stainless-steel tubular fixed-bed reactor.  
  • Employ a syngas mixture of H₂/CO/He at 100/3.2/21.8 with a low CO partial pressure (~40 Pa).  
  • Temperature ramp: 0.1°C min⁻¹ up to 350°C, dwelled for 6 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  • Gradually raise the temperature under the specified syngas atmosphere to convert the precursor fully into phase-pure χ‐Fe5C2.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Careful monitoring of the temperature profile and gas composition ensures reproducibility and phase purity.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for the synthesis of phase‐pure Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 is executed through an in situ carburization procedure designed to fully convert the Raney iron precursor into the desired active phase under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Activation temperature is crucial for initiating the reduction and carburization processes. In this experiment, the catalyst is heated with a controlled ramp rate of 1°C/min, reaching a target temperature of 350 °C. This gradual increase prevents abrupt thermal shocks and guarantees a uniform transformation of the precursor. The dwell at 350 °C facilitates complete carburization, resulting in a phase‐pure χ‐Fe5C2.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
The activation is carried out at ambient pressure. Maintaining an atmospheric pressure during the carburization step supports efficient gas–catalyst interactions without the risk of excessive pressure-induced agglomeration. This mild pressure condition helps preserve catalyst stability while ensuring complete activation.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition  
A tailored syngas mixture, with a composition of H₂/CO/He in the ratios 100/3.2/21.8, is employed to create a reducing and carburizing environment. The high concentration of hydrogen drives the reduction of the iron precursor, while the presence of CO and helium ensures controlled carburization. This specific gas composition is critical to attaining the pure phase of χ‐Fe5C2, as it balances the removal of oxygen with the incorporation of carbon.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
The interaction between the catalyst and the reactive gas is finely regulated by setting a space‐time velocity of approximately 600 mL/(g_cat·h). This parameter ensures that the gas mixture uniformly contacts the catalyst surface, facilitating an even carburization process across the entire catalyst bed.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
The carburization dwell time is maintained at 6 hours. This prolonged exposure at 350 °C ensures that the conversion to phase‐pure χ‐Fe5C2 is complete while minimizing the risk of sintering or over‐reduction. Overall, the deliberate selection of activation parameters under the controlled environment of a stainless‐steel tubular fixed‐bed reactor ensures reproducible catalyst activation for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

The reaction section focuses on the parameters governing Fischer-Tropsch synthesis using the Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ catalyst in a stainless-steel tubular fixed-bed reactor. The following subsections detail the reaction conditions with experimental data directly extracted from the article.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature is critical for syngas activation and product distribution. Experiments were performed within a temperature span of 250–320 °C. In practice, a temperature of 290 °C was frequently employed, as it provided an optimal balance between CO conversion—reported to reach up to 70.7%—and low CO₂ selectivity. This temperature range was chosen to maximize the formation of linear C₂–C₁₀ α-olefins while minimizing undesired byproducts.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Reaction pressure directly affects the syngas concentration and overall kinetics. The catalytic evaluations were conducted at pressures of 2.3, 2.5, and 3.0 MPa. Notably, a pressure of 2.5 MPa was linked to more favorable selectivity towards longer-chain hydrocarbons. The high-pressure environment in the tubular fixed-bed reactor ensures intensified contact between reactants and catalyst, thus enhancing the formation of target products.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The H₂/CO ratio in the feed impacts both the hydrocarbon distribution and catalyst behavior. The article reports the use of two specific ratios, 1.5 and 2.0, during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. A lower ratio of 1.5 was observed to yield higher CO conversion with minimal CO₂ formation, whereas a ratio of 2.0 tended to increase methane formation. These findings help tailor the reaction to produce a more desirable olefin-rich product slate.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
Space-time velocity (SV) is defined as the ratio of gas flow rate to catalyst mass, influencing the residence time of syngas in the reactor. The study used SV values ranging from 5,000 mL/(g_cat·h) to 12,000 mL/(g_cat·h), and in some tests up to 100,000 mL/(g_cat·h). Observations indicate that while higher SVs improve CO conversion, they must be optimized carefully to sustain catalyst stability over extended operation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of the Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ catalyst were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Rietveld refinement. This method is essential for confirming the formation of the desired iron carbide phase and excluding the presence of other iron-containing phases that could affect catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns, refined using the Fullprof software, matched exclusively with the χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase, with no detectable contributions from other iron carbides or oxides. The Rietveld refinement yielded a goodness of fit (χ² = 7.58%, Rp = 18.7%, Rwp = 15.3%, Rexp = 5.6%), and the space group and lattice parameters were consistent with published data for χ‐Fe₅C₂. A characteristic lattice spacing of approximately 2.7 Å along the (311) direction was identified, serving as a crystallographic fingerprint for the phase.
- **Observations:** The exclusive presence of χ‐Fe₅C₂ confirms the successful synthesis of a phase-pure catalyst. The crystallographic integrity is maintained after prolonged reaction, directly correlating with the catalyst’s high activity and selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Carburization Process
- **Description:** High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM) and inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) imaging were employed to monitor morphological evolution during carburization and to visualize the spatial distribution of the χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase at the nanoscale.
- **Findings:** Initial HRTEM images showed crystallized iron particles enveloped by an amorphous oxide passivation layer. Upon exposure to syngas (H₂/CO = 30, 1,200 Pa, 350°C), the transformation to χ‐Fe₅C₂ initiated at the particle core and progressed outward, completing within 30 minutes. IFFT images confirmed the emergence of the χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase with the characteristic 2.7 Å lattice spacing. Post-reaction TEM analysis of Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ revealed well-maintained phase structure, with no evidence of carbon deposition or manganese migration.
- **Observations:** The controlled carburization process yields uniform, phase-pure χ‐Fe₅C₂ grains. The absence of carbon buildup and promoter migration after extended operation demonstrates the catalyst’s morphological stability and the effectiveness of manganese in surface cleaning.

#### 6.1.3 Phase Stability and Iron Speciation
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was used to identify iron phases and monitor their stability under reaction conditions. This technique provides detailed information on the oxidation state and local environment of iron atoms.
- **Findings:** In situ Mössbauer spectra, collected at −153°C, showed that after carburization and Fischer–Tropsch operation, only the χ‐Fe₅C₂ (Hägg carbide) phase was present in both unpromoted and Mn-promoted catalysts. No other iron phases were detected, confirming complete and stable transformation.
- **Observations:** The persistence of the χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase throughout prolonged catalytic testing underscores its robustness and suitability as the active phase for selective syngas conversion.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition and Promoter Distribution
- **Description:** Elemental analysis focused on the distribution and retention of manganese promoter within the catalyst matrix, as observed by TEM and supported by the absence of secondary phases in XRD and Mössbauer data.
- **Findings:** TEM analysis of used Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ samples showed no evidence of manganese migration or segregation, and XRD confirmed the absence of manganese-containing secondary phases.
- **Observations:** The stable incorporation of manganese contributes to the catalyst’s resistance to deactivation and supports its role in enhancing selectivity and suppressing carbon deposition.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 catalyst achieves CO conversion values ranging from 18% to 53% under fixed-bed reactor conditions (250–320°C, 2.3–3.0 MPa, SV 5,000–100,000 ml g_cat⁻¹ h⁻¹). Notably, a high conversion of 53% is maintained during long-term operation at 320–325°C and 2.3 MPa.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher Mn loading and increased reaction temperature enhance CO conversion. The catalyst demonstrates robust activity across a broad operational window, outperforming reference systems at comparable or milder conditions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of undesired oxidation:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 exhibits low CO₂ selectivity, with values as low as 9.3% at 250°C/2.3 MPa and up to 21–32% at higher temperatures (290–320°C). These are significantly lower than those of unpromoted or reference catalysts (often 37–47%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese promotion suppresses CO₂ formation by inhibiting secondary hydrogenation and favoring H₂O as the oxygen removal product, thereby increasing carbon efficiency.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2, CH₄ selectivity is low, reported as 4.3% at 240°C and 2.0% at 280°C. Other Fe-based catalysts may achieve even lower values (e.g., 0.4% for Fe-Al₂O₃ at 320°C).
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst maintains low methane formation, with Mn promotion further reducing CH₄ selectivity compared to unpromoted systems.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity denotes the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 catalyst achieves high selectivity to C₂–C₁₀ linear α-olefins, with C₂–C₁₀ fractions of 83.1% at 250°C and 81.5% at 290°C.
- **Observations and Trends:** High C₃⁺ selectivity reflects efficient chain growth and a favorable hydrocarbon distribution for olefin production.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination, influencing product distribution:
  - ASF model: α ≈ 0.63 maximizes C₂–C₁₀ fraction.
- **Experimental Data:** Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 exhibits α values of 0.61 (250°C) and 0.63 (290°C).
- **Observations and Trends:** These values are near the theoretical optimum, confirming the catalyst’s ability to favor long-chain olefin formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time; deactivation rate is:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 225 h at 320–325°C and 2.3 MPa, Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 maintains 53% CO conversion and a catalyst time yield (CTY) of 265 μmol g_cat⁻¹ s⁻¹, with negligible deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The phase-pure χ‐Fe5C2 structure remains stable, with no evidence of carbon deposition or Mn migration, ensuring sustained performance.

#### 6.2.7 Carbon Efficiency
- **Description:** Carbon efficiency is the fraction of carbon from converted CO incorporated into hydrocarbons and oxygenates.
- **Experimental Data:** Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 achieves carbon efficiencies of 90.7% at 250°C/2.3 MPa, with similar high values under other conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** High carbon efficiency is attributed to suppressed CO₂ formation and selective hydrocarbon production.

**Summary:** The Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2 catalyst demonstrates outstanding performance in syngas-to-olefin conversion, combining high CO conversion, low CO₂ and CH₄ selectivities, high C₃⁺ and olefin selectivity, optimal α factor, and exceptional long-term stability. Manganese promotion is critical for enhancing selectivity and stability, positioning this catalyst as a benchmark for efficient linear α-olefin synthesis.
