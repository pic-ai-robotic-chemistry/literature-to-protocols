## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article presents three distinct synthesis routes for Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, each employing a structured sequence of experimental steps to ensure reproducibility and optimal catalyst formation. Below are the detailed procedures as reported:

#### Fe@Si Catalyst
• 1. Precursor Mixing / Solvothermal Synthesis  
  – Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃ in an appropriate solvent with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) as the stabilizer.  
  – A yellow suspension forms, indicating the formation of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.  
• 2. Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Introduce tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) dropwise in the presence of CTAB as the structure-directing agent.  
  – Transfer the mixture into a Teflon‑lined autoclave and heat at 180 °C for 18 h to form a protective SiO₂ shell around the Fe₂O₃ cores.  
• 3. Drying  
  – Dry the composite at 120 °C for 24 h to remove residual solvents.  
• 4. Calcination  
  – Calcine at 550 °C for 6 h to remove organic residues and promote the Fe–O–Si structural interaction.

#### Fe/Si Catalyst
• 1. Precursor Mixing / Impregnation  
  – Impregnate 10.0 g of mesoporous SiO₂ (m‑SiO₂) with a 2 M ethanol solution of Fe(NO₃)₃.  
• 2. (No Precipitation–Deposition step is applied.)  
• 3. Autoclave Treatment  
  – Subject the impregnated support to autoclave heating at 180 °C for 18 h to ensure uniform distribution of the precursor.  
• 4. Drying  
  – Follow with drying at 120 °C for 24 h.  
• 5. Calcination  
  – Conclude with calcination at 550 °C for 6 h, ensuring the formation of active catalytic phases.

#### Fe‑Si Catalyst
• 1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Simultaneously dissolve 20.6 g of Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and 31.7 g of silica sol (30 wt.%) in distilled water.  
• 2. Precipitation–Deposition  
  – Add a 25 wt.% NH₃·H₂O solution dropwise to maintain the pH at 9.0 ± 0.1 while heating at 85 °C, which promotes uniform precipitation of iron and silica species.  
• 3. Drying  
  – Dry the resulting precipitate under controlled conditions (e.g., at 120 °C for 24 h) to remove excess moisture.  
• 4. Calcination  
  – Finally, calcine at 550 °C for 6 h to enhance the interaction between Fe species and the silica matrix.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural features of the Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD). This technique identifies the nature and crystallinity of iron species, which are critical for catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the calcined Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts revealed only well-crystallized hematite (α‑Fe₂O₃), with characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 38.7°, 41.7°, 63.9°, and 74.1°. In contrast, the Fe–Si catalyst exhibited broad peaks at 2θ = 42° and 75°, indicative of amorphous iron oxides with small crystallite sizes.
- **Observations:** The presence of well-defined α‑Fe₂O₃ in Fe@Si and Fe/Si suggests high crystallinity, while the amorphous nature of Fe–Si reflects strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions that hinder crystallite growth. After activation under syngas, XRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy confirm the formation of iron carbides (FexC), particularly in Fe@Si and Fe/Si.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET) was employed to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which influence reactant diffusion and accessibility of active sites.
- **Findings:** All catalysts displayed type IV isotherms, characteristic of mesoporous materials. Fe@Si and Fe/Si exhibited H1-type hysteresis loops (P/P₀ = 0.4–0.9), indicating uniform cylindrical mesopores and higher BET surface areas. The Fe–Si catalyst showed an H3-type hysteresis loop (P/P₀ = 0.6–0.9), corresponding to larger, less uniform mesopores and a lower surface area.
- **Observations:** The use of CTAB in Fe@Si and Fe/Si synthesis led to small, uniform pores, while random dehydration during Fe–Si co-precipitation resulted in larger mesopores. These textural differences are linked to improved diffusion in Fe–Si and enhanced active site accessibility in Fe@Si and Fe/Si.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Metal–Support Interaction
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂‑TPR) assessed the reducibility of iron oxides and the strength of Fe–SiO₂ interactions, which affect the ease of iron activation and subsequent carburization.
- **Findings:** Fe–Si exhibited the lowest H₂ consumption, reflecting the strongest Fe–SiO₂ interaction and most difficult reduction. Fe@Si showed lower H₂ consumption than Fe/Si, attributed to the SiO₂ shell hindering H₂ access and H₂O removal.
- **Observations:** Strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions in Fe–Si stabilize iron species, favoring continuous iron carbide formation during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis and contributing to catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping were used to examine particle size, morphology, and elemental dispersion.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed that Fe@Si consists of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles (average size ~45–50 nm) embedded in a SiO₂ shell, effectively preventing sintering. Fe/Si showed significant agglomeration of iron particles, while Fe–Si displayed even iron dispersion within the SiO₂ matrix, as confirmed by EDX mapping.
- **Observations:** The embedded structure in Fe@Si and strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction in Fe–Si both promote superior iron dispersion and inhibit sintering, which are beneficial for catalytic stability.

#### 6.1.5 Chemical Bonding and Phase Composition
- **Description:** Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT‑IR) and Mössbauer spectroscopy provided insights into Fe–SiO₂ bonding and iron phase composition.
- **Findings:** FT‑IR spectra showed Fe–O–Si bond absorption bands (957–977 cm⁻¹), with intensity correlating to Fe–SiO₂ interaction strength. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified iron carbide (FexC) content after activation: 52.01% (Fe@Si), 55.75% (Fe/Si), and 29.67% (Fe–Si).
- **Observations:** Strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions in Fe–Si favor sustained iron carbide formation during reaction, supporting enhanced catalyst stability and performance.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (280 °C, 2.0 MPa, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹, H₂/CO = 2), CO conversion after 64 hours on stream was:
  - Fe@Si: 47.9%
  - Fe/Si: 55.7%
  - Fe–Si: 29.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe/Si exhibited the highest CO conversion, attributed to its higher initial iron dispersion. Fe@Si also showed substantial activity, while Fe–Si had lower initial conversion due to stronger Fe–SiO₂ interaction, which limited iron oxide reduction. Notably, Fe–Si’s CO conversion increased over time, indicating enhanced stability.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is typically defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity at 64 hours:
  - Fe@Si: 24.4%
  - Fe/Si: 19.5%
  - Fe–Si: 17.9%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@Si showed the highest CO₂ selectivity, likely due to the SiO₂ shell’s confinement effect, which promoted secondary reactions and hindered product desorption. Fe–Si’s lower CO₂ selectivity is consistent with its stronger Fe–SiO₂ interaction, suppressing the water–gas shift reaction.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, representing heavy hydrocarbon production.
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivity at 64 hours:
  - Fe@Si: 27.1%
  - Fe/Si: 31.0%
  - Fe–Si: 25.1%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe/Si achieved the highest C₅⁺ selectivity, correlating with its high initial activity and iron dispersion. Fe–Si’s lower C₅⁺ selectivity reflects its initial structural constraints, though selectivity improved as the reaction progressed.

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination in FTS, influencing hydrocarbon chain length distribution. Values range from 0 (short chains) to 1 (long chains).
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si exhibited higher initial α values, which decreased over time, while Fe–Si started with a lower α that increased during the reaction.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values corresponded to greater C₅⁺ selectivity. The increasing α for Fe–Si over time paralleled its rising heavy hydrocarbon selectivity, attributed to ongoing iron carbide formation.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time, reflecting catalyst stability:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \ (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si showed a gradual decrease in CO conversion, indicating higher deactivation rates, while Fe–Si demonstrated increasing activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Deactivation in Fe@Si and Fe/Si was linked to iron carbide oxidation, exacerbated by water confinement (Fe@Si) and sintering (Fe/Si). In contrast, Fe–Si’s strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction promoted sustained carbonization, enhancing long-term stability.
