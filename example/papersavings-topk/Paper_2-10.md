## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of Phase‑pure χ‑Fe5C2 / Mn‑χ‑Fe5C2 is achieved through a systematic process starting with a Raney iron precursor derived from an iron–aluminium alloy. The method is designed for reproducibility and scalability, with each step carefully controlled.

#### Precursor Preparation and Aluminium Removal
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • Reagents: Iron–aluminium alloy powder is added to an 8 mol·L⁻¹ KOH solution for selective aluminium removal.  
   • Reaction Conditions: The process is conducted under controlled stirring while monitoring pH to ensure adequate interaction.  
   • Observations: The successful removal of aluminium is inferred from established process markers.

2. Washing  
   • Procedure: Post aluminium removal, the sample is washed with deionized water (10 cycles) followed by ethanol (7 cycles).  
   • Equipment: Standard washing apparatus with a pH meter ensures consistency in the removal of residual ions.

#### Promoter Incorporation via Wet Impregnation (Precipitation–Deposition)
3. Promoter Addition  
   • For promoted catalysts, manganese is introduced by wet impregnation using a Mn(NO₃)₂ solution (target loading: 0.5–12.5 wt%), while potassium is added using a KNO₃ solution (1 wt%).  
   • Process: The promoter solution is added dropwise under continuous stirring, ensuring uniform deposition prior to subsequent drying.

#### Drying, Passivation, and Carburization
4. Drying  
   • Conditions: The impregnated precursor is dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 hours, ensuring complete solvent removal.

5. Passivation  
   • Conditions: The dried sample is passivated at room temperature for 20 hours in a flow of 1% O₂ in He.  
   • Purpose: This step stabilizes the precursor surface before high-temperature processing.

6. Carburization  
   • Reactor and Conditions: Carburization is performed in a stainless-steel reactor using a syngas mixture (H₂/CO/He = 100/3.2/21.8) at 350°C for 6 hours.  
   • Outcome: This final step converts the precursor into phase‑pure χ‑Fe5C2, ensuring the desired catalyst phase.

• Comparative Analysis: This synthesis method is advantageous for its simplicity and cost efficiency. The integration of wet impregnation enables direct promoter deposition, while the sequential drying, passivation, and carburization steps ensure phase purity and enhanced catalytic performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Phase‐pure χ‐Fe5C2  
##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Iron–aluminium alloy powder (50:50 weight ratio)  
  • 8 M KOH solution (sufficient volume to immerse the alloy)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Temperature: 70°C  
  • Stirring: Continuous at moderate speed using a standard laboratory agitator  
- **Process**:  
  1. Add the iron–aluminium alloy powder to an 8 M KOH solution under stirring.  
  2. Heat the mixture to 70°C to dissolve the aluminium component effectively.  
  3. Monitor the reaction until the alloy is completely treated.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete aluminium dissolution to avoid competing phases in later steps.

##### Step [2]: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Resulting Raney iron precursor  
  • For promoted catalysts: Mn(NO₃)₂ solution (targeting 0.5–12.5 wt% Mn) or KNO₃ for potassium incorporation  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Employ wet impregnation at room temperature with moderate stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  1. For Mn‐χ‐Fe5C2, add the appropriate Mn(NO₃)₂ amount dropwise to the precursor suspension.  
  2. Continue stirring to ensure homogeneous distribution of the promoter.  
- **Key Steps**: Sequential addition and thorough mixing ensure a uniform catalyst assembly.

##### Step [3]: Purification, Drying & Passivation  
- **Process**:  
  1. Wash the treated precursor ten times with deionized water and seven times with ethanol to remove residual ions.  
  2. Dry the washed sample in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 hours.  
  3. Passivate the dry precursor by exposing it to a flow of 1% O₂ in He at room temperature for 20 hours to stabilize the surface.  
- **Key Steps**: Rigorous washing and controlled drying are crucial for preparing a clean, reactive surface.

##### Step [4]: Carburization (Activation)  
- **Reagents & Conditions**:  
  • Syngas mixture formulated as H₂:CO:He in a 100:3.2:21.8 ratio  
  • Temperature: 350°C with a ramp rate of 0.5°C/min  
  • Duration: 6 hours  
- **Process**:  
  1. Introduce the passivated precursor into a fixed-bed reactor.  
  2. Carburize under the specified syngas conditions to convert into phase‐pure χ‐Fe5C2.  
- **Key Steps**: The controlled environment and heating program are essential to achieve the desired catalyst structure without impurity formation.  

This detailed workflow—from raw precursor preparation through purification, drying, passivation, and final carburization—ensures high reproducibility and phase purity for both the unpromoted and Mn-promoted catalysts.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was carried out as a pre-reaction treatment focused on converting the passivated Raney iron precursor into phase‐pure χ‐Fe₅C₂. Activation proceeds via controlled carburization using a defined syngas mixture, specific temperature, duration, and pressure conditions.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical to drive the carburization process while preventing excessive sintering or undesirable oxide formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 350 °C. This temperature is chosen because it is high enough to facilitate carbon incorporation from CO and ensure the formation of a pure χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase, yet remains low enough to maintain excellent metal dispersion.
- **Observations:** Operating at 350 °C yields phase‐pure iron carbide with enhanced long-term catalyst stability.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The pressure during activation influences the kinetics of reduction and carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed under an explicit pressure of 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** Controlled low pressure aids in precise gas interaction with the catalyst surface, ensuring uniform carburization without promoting agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition dictates the balance between reduction and carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation syngas mixture comprises hydrogen, carbon monoxide, and helium in a ratio of 100:3.2:21.8. Here, H₂ serves as the reducing agent, CO supplies the necessary carbon, and He acts as an inert diluent to stabilize the gas flow.
- **Observations:** This specific composition ensures complete transformation of the precursor into phase‐pure χ‐Fe₅C₂ while avoiding unwanted secondary phases.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation is essential to allow for complete carburization without overexposure that might lead to sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is subjected to the carburization process for 6 hours.
- **Observations:** The 6‑hour treatment ensures full carbon incorporation and structural transformation, leading to a catalyst with superior performance characteristics for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and structural integrity of the catalysts were determined using in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Rietveld refinement. This approach is essential for confirming the formation and phase purity of the active χ‐Fe₅C₂ phase, which is directly linked to catalytic performance in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** In situ XRD patterns, recorded during carburization under a syngas mixture (H₂/CO/He = 100/3.2/21.8) at 350°C, revealed the exclusive formation of phase-pure χ‐Fe₅C₂. Rietveld refinement yielded a χ² value of 7.58% (Rp = 18.7%, Rwp = 15.3%, Rexp = 5.6%), confirming a good fit and the absence of other crystalline phases. The refined lattice parameters and space group matched published data for χ‐Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The formation of χ‐Fe₅C₂ proceeded identically in both unpromoted and manganese-promoted catalysts, indicating that promoter addition does not disrupt phase development. The phase remained stable after extended Fischer-Tropsch operation, demonstrating structural robustness under reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Phase Evolution
- **Description:** Environmental transmission electron microscopy (ETEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were employed to visualize morphological evolution and phase transformation from Raney iron to χ‐Fe₅C₂. Fast Fourier transform (FFT) and inverse FFT (IFFT) analyses were used to confirm lattice spacings characteristic of the carbide phase.
- **Findings:** ETEM and HRTEM images, obtained under syngas at 350°C, showed rapid transformation of the porous Raney iron precursor to χ‐Fe₅C₂, with the process initiating at the inner layers and completing within 30 minutes. IFFT images highlighted lattice spacings of approximately 2.7 Å, consistent with χ‐Fe₅C₂. The porous morphology, originating from aluminum leaching, was preserved, and no carbon deposition or manganese migration was observed.
- **Observations:** The uniform and stable morphology, along with the absence of agglomeration or phase segregation, supports the high activity and durability of the catalysts. The synthesis method ensures reproducible control over particle structure.

#### 6.1.3 Iron Phase Identification by Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Description:** ^57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy at −153°C was used to identify and quantify iron-containing phases, providing insight into the electronic and magnetic environment of iron atoms.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra confirmed the exclusive presence of χ‐Fe₅C₂ in both unpromoted and Mn-promoted catalysts after carburization and Fischer-Tropsch reaction. No signals corresponding to iron oxides or other carbide phases were detected.
- **Observations:** The high phase purity of χ‐Fe₅C₂, as validated by Mössbauer spectroscopy, is critical for achieving the observed high CO conversion and low CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution are typically assessed by BET analysis to evaluate their influence on reactant diffusion and catalyst accessibility.
- **Findings:** Specific BET surface area, pore volume, and pore size values were not reported in the article.
- **Observations:** The preserved porous structure, as visualized by TEM, suggests favorable mass transport properties, which are consistent with the high catalytic activity and stability observed.

#### 6.1.5 Computational Characterization and Mechanistic Insights
- **Description:** Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and microkinetic simulations were integrated with experimental data to elucidate reaction mechanisms and correlate structural features with catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** DFT-derived energetics and activation barriers for elementary steps on χ‐Fe₅C₂ surfaces were used in microkinetic models, which predicted dominant H₂O formation for oxygen removal (99.4% at 525 K) and minimal CO₂ production (0.6%). These predictions align with experimental observations of low CO₂ selectivity and high carbon efficiency.
- **Observations:** The computational results reinforce the experimental findings, providing a molecular-level rationale for the superior performance of phase-pure χ‐Fe₅C₂ catalysts in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** For phase-pure χ‐Fe₅C₂ at 250°C and 2.3 MPa, CO conversion reached 27.8%. Manganese promotion (Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂) resulted in a conversion range of 16.0–46.1%, depending on pressure (2.3–3.0 MPa) and space velocity. The highest conversion (46.1%) was observed at 250°C, 3.0 MPa, and low space velocity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher pressure and lower space velocity favor increased CO conversion. Manganese promotion can reduce conversion under identical conditions but enhances selectivity for target products.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, defined as:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For χ‐Fe₅C₂ at 250°C and 2.3 MPa, CO₂ selectivity was 11.2%. Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ exhibited lower CO₂ selectivity, consistently around 9.3–9.4% under similar conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese promotion effectively suppresses CO₂ formation, favoring oxygen removal as H₂O and improving carbon efficiency.

#### 6.2.3 Target Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Target olefin selectivity refers to the fraction of converted CO yielding linear α-olefins (C₂–C₁₀), a key performance metric for Fischer–Tropsch to olefins (FTLAO) processes.
- **Experimental Data:** At 250°C and 2.3 MPa, χ‐Fe₅C₂ achieved a target olefin selectivity of 24.3%, while Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ reached 50.7% under identical conditions. Selectivity remained high (48–51%) for Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ across tested pressures.
- **Observations and Trends:** Manganese promotion significantly enhances olefin selectivity, more than doubling the yield of valuable α-olefins compared to the unpromoted catalyst.

#### 6.2.4 Carbon Efficiency
- **Description:** Carbon efficiency represents the percentage of carbon from CO incorporated into hydrocarbons and oxygenates, rather than lost as CO₂.
- **Experimental Data:** χ‐Fe₅C₂ exhibited a carbon efficiency of 88.8%, while Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ achieved 90.7% at 250°C and 2.3 MPa.
- **Observations and Trends:** Both catalysts demonstrate high carbon utilization, with manganese promotion providing a modest improvement.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended operation. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Both χ‐Fe₅C₂ and Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ maintained stable CO conversion and selectivity over 200–225 h, with negligible deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalysts exhibit excellent long-term stability, with minimal loss in activity, supporting their industrial applicability.

#### 6.2.6 Additional Performance Indicators
- **O/P Ratio:** The olefin/paraffin ratio for Mn‐χ‐Fe₅C₂ was approximately 3.6, compared to 1.5 for χ‐Fe₅C₂, indicating a strong preference for olefin production.
- **Catalyst Time Yield (CTY):** The CTY for χ‐Fe₅C₂ at 250°C was 1–2 orders of magnitude higher than state-of-the-art FT-to-olefin catalysts operated at higher temperatures, reflecting superior intrinsic activity.

**Summary:**  
Phase-pure χ‐Fe₅C₂ catalysts, especially when promoted with manganese, deliver a unique combination of high CO conversion, low CO₂ selectivity, and exceptional selectivity for linear α-olefins under industrially relevant conditions. The catalysts maintain high carbon efficiency and stability over extended operation, with performance metrics that surpass conventional FT catalysts, highlighting their potential for efficient syngas-to-olefin conversion.
