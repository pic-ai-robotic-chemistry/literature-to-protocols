## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis of Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocrystals using a controlled reduction and carburization approach. The method capitalizes on pre‐formed Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals (nanocubes or octahedra) as templates to achieve a well‐defined core–shell structure, with the χ-Fe₅C₂ shell exposing either the {202} facet (nanocubes) or the {112} facet (octahedra).

#### Catalyst Precursor Loading and Reduction
1. • Catalyst precursor: 50 mg of as‐prepared Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals.  
   • Equipment: Fixed-bed reactor (inner diameter: 9 mm).  
2. • Process: The nanocrystals are uniformly loaded into the reactor.  
3. • Reduction Step:  
   - Atmosphere: H₂ gas (flow rate: 100 mL/min at 1 bar).  
   - Conditions: 270 °C for 10 hours.  
   - Observation: Removal of impurities and partial transformation of the oxide phase, ensuring an active metallic surface for subsequent carburization.

#### Surface Reconstruction (Carburization)
4. • Following reduction, the catalyst undergoes surface reconstruction:  
   - Atmosphere: Syngas composed of 32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar.  
   - Conditions: 20 bar pressure at 270 °C for 20 hours.  
   - Outcome: Carburization of the surface leads to the formation of a χ-Fe₅C₂ shell, observed via distinct morphological changes and the preservation of initial nanocrystal shape.

#### Post‑processing and Preservation
5. • Immediately after reaction, the gas is switched to an inert N₂ atmosphere.  
   • Cooling and transfer: The catalyst is cooled to room temperature and transferred into a glove box under N₂ to prevent oxidation and preserve the sensitive χ-Fe₅C₂ phase.

#### Comparative Analysis
• Advantages:  
   - Consistent reaction conditions in the fixed-bed reactor ensure homogeneous exposure to reactive gases, leading to reproducible catalyst characteristics.  
   - The sequential reduction and carburization steps allow precise control over the core–shell structure and facet exposure.
• Disadvantages:  
   - Scalability may be challenging due to mass transfer and heat distribution limitations inherent to fixed-bed reactor designs.

• **Activation Temperature:**  
Activation at 270 °C is critical for reducing the Fe₃O₄ phase into a metallic Fe state. This temperature is sufficiently high to remove surface impurities without inducing sintering, as confirmed by preserved particle morphology.  
• **Activation Pressure:**

• **Activation Time:**  
A duration of 10 hours is employed, sufficient to thoroughly convert Fe₃O₄ while maintaining high metal dispersion and minimizing sintering effects.

The subsequent syngas treatment is also performed at 270 °C, ensuring that the thermal conditions remain optimal for carburization while avoiding phase instability.  
• **Activation Pressure:**

• **Activation Gas:**  
A carefully formulated syngas mixture—comprising 32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar—is employed. CO serves as the carbon source for building the χ-Fe₅C₂ shell, while hydrogen maintains a reducing environment and argon acts as an inert diluent to stabilize the system.  
• **Activation Time:**  
A 20-hour exposure under syngas conditions ensures complete surface reconstruction through controlled carburization, resulting in the formation of a stable, carbon-rich χ-Fe₅C₂ shell.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD), high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These techniques are essential for identifying the presence and distribution of active phases, which directly influence Fischer–Tropsch (FT) catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirmed the coexistence of Fe₃O₄ (core) and χ-Fe₅C₂ (shell) phases in both nanocube and octahedral catalysts. Simulated and experimental fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns from HAADF-STEM images assigned the (400) facet to Fe₃O₄ and the (202) facet to χ-Fe₅C₂ in nanocubes, while the octahedra predominantly exposed the {112} facet of χ-Fe₅C₂. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the phase composition: nanocubes contained 63.4 wt% Fe₃O₄ and 33.2 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂; octahedra contained 65.1 wt% Fe₃O₄ and 29.5 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The epitaxial relationship between core and shell is confirmed, and the facet exposure is directly linked to catalytic activity and stability.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Distribution and Core–Shell Architecture
- **Description:** HAADF-STEM, electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS), and scanning transmission electron microscopy–energy dispersive X-ray (STEM-EDX) mapping were employed to resolve the spatial distribution of elements within the nanocrystals, crucial for verifying the intended core–shell structure.
- **Findings:** HAADF-STEM images revealed distinct core–shell morphologies. EELS and STEM-EDX mapping showed Fe and O concentrated in the core (Fe₃O₄), while Fe and C were localized in the shell (χ-Fe₅C₂). Line-scanning profiles further confirmed this spatial separation, with Fe distributed throughout and O and C segregated to core and shell, respectively.
- **Observations:** The clear elemental separation validates the successful synthesis of Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocrystals with well-defined interfaces.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to assess particle morphology and size distribution, parameters that affect catalyst surface area and active site accessibility.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed that Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes have an average size of 40.1 ± 3.8 nm, exhibiting uniform cubic morphology. The shell thickness and structural uniformity were confirmed by HAADF-STEM.
- **Observations:** The controlled morphology and size distribution indicate reproducible synthesis and facilitate facet-specific catalytic studies.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area
- **Description:** Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was conducted to determine the specific surface area, which influences catalytic activity by affecting reactant accessibility.
- **Findings:** BET measurements yielded surface areas of 20.4 m²/g for nanocubes and 22.0 m²/g for octahedra. All measurements were performed under inert conditions to preserve the sensitive carbide phase.
- **Observations:** The comparable surface areas suggest that differences in catalytic performance arise primarily from facet exposure rather than surface area effects.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and CO Dissociation Pathways
- **Description:** In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was used to probe surface intermediates and elucidate CO dissociation mechanisms, which are central to FT catalysis.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS spectra indicated that nanocubes ({202} facet) facilitate both direct and hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation, evidenced by the formation of CHO* species under H₂. In contrast, octahedra ({112} facet) only exhibited direct CO dissociation, with no CHO* or COH* detected.
- **Observations:** The facet-dependent dissociation pathways correlate with the observed differences in catalytic activity and stability, with the {202} facet favoring more efficient, hydrogen-assisted CO activation.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO molecules transformed during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FT conditions (270 °C, 20 bar, syngas: 64% H₂, 32% CO, 4% Ar, space velocity 2400 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹), Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a CO conversion of 45.4%. The octahedral counterpart exhibited a lower initial CO conversion of 21.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes consistently outperformed the octahedra, maintaining conversion within 1% fluctuation over 100 hours, while the octahedra showed a decline to 11.7%, indicating superior activity and stability for the nanocubes.

#### 6.2.2 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF measures the number of CO molecules converted per surface Fe atom per hour, reflecting intrinsic catalytic activity.
- **Experimental Data:** The nanocubes/SiC catalyst exhibited a TOF of 645.9 h⁻¹, while the octahedra/SiC catalyst showed a TOF of 372.7 h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher TOF for nanocubes is attributed to the exposure of the {202} facet, which facilitates a hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway with lower activation energy.

#### 6.2.3 Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity is reported as the carbon percentage (C%) of each hydrocarbon fraction among total hydrocarbons. For methane (CH₄), C₂–C₄, and C₅⁺ fractions, selectivity is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{C}_n\text{H}_m \text{ Selectivity (C\%)} = \frac{n \times F_{\text{C}_n\text{H}_m,\text{out}}}{\sum_i i \times F_{\text{C}_i\text{H}_m,\text{out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For χ-Fe₅C₂ nanoparticles/SiC, CH₄ selectivity is 15.5 C%, C₂–C₄ is 48.2 C%, and C₅–C₁₂ is 34.9 C%. For Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC, C₅⁺ selectivity is 44.8 C% and C₂–C₄ is 41.0 C%. Octahedra/SiC show C₅⁺ selectivity of 27.6 C% and C₂–C₄ of 53.1 C%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes favor longer-chain hydrocarbons (higher C₅⁺ selectivity), while octahedra and nanoparticles yield more light hydrocarbons (C₂–C₄, CH₄).

#### 6.2.4 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) statistics, represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon formation. It is determined from the product distribution, with values closer to 1 indicating preference for longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values are 0.66 for nanocubes/SiC, 0.62 for octahedra/SiC, and 0.63 for nanoparticles/SiC.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher α value for nanocubes correlates with their enhanced selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended operation. Deactivation rate is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time (h)}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 100 hours, nanocubes/SiC maintained stable CO conversion (fluctuation within 1%), while octahedra/SiC declined from 20.1% to 11.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The superior stability of nanocubes is attributed to reduced carbon deposition and structural integrity, whereas octahedra suffer from void formation and carbon buildup, accelerating deactivation.

---
**Summary:** The Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC catalyst, characterized by the exposure of the {202} facet, demonstrates higher CO conversion, TOF, α factor, and long-term stability compared to octahedral and nanoparticle analogues. These results underscore the critical influence of facet engineering on Fischer–Tropsch catalyst performance.
