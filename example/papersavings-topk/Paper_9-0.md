## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article delineates three distinct catalyst synthesis routes, each providing specific advantages in terms of synthesis complexity, scalability, and control over the Fe–SiO₂ interaction.

---

#### • Fe@Si Synthesis (Solvothermal Followed by Stöber Method)

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
 • Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O is dissolved in anhydrous ethanol with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP).  
 • The PVP acts as a stabilizer, preventing nanoparticle aggregation and yielding a yellow suspension indicative of Fe₂O₃ formation.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
 • Utilizing a Teflon‑lined autoclave for solvothermal conditions, the Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles are subjected to a controlled environment before shell formation.  
 • Subsequently, tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) is hydrolyzed at room temperature over 48 h by adding NH₃⋅H₂O (to adjust and maintain pH) and CTAB as a structure directing template. This step forms a mesoporous SiO₂ shell encapsulating the iron cores.

3. Drying  
 • The catalyst is dried at 120 °C for 24 hours to eliminate solvents.

4. Calcination  
 • Calcination is conducted at 550 °C for 6 hours in air, finalizing the formation of the catalyst and inducing the formation of active iron carbide phases after subsequent H₂ reduction.

---

#### • Fe/Si Synthesis (Incipient Wetness Impregnation)

1. Precursor Mixing  
 • A 2 M Fe(NO₃)₃ solution in ethanol is used to impregnate mesoporous SiO₂, ensuring uniform metal dispersion.

2. Washing and Drying  
 • The impregnated sample undergoes filtration and extensive washing with distilled water and ethanol.  
 • Drying is performed before the catalyst is subjected to calcination.

3. Calcination  
 • The final calcination is carried out at 550 °C for 6 hours.

---

#### • Fe‑Si Synthesis (Co‑precipitation Method)

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
 • Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and silica sol are simultaneously mixed in distilled water.  
 • NH₃⋅H₂O is added to adjust the pH to ~9.0 at 85 °C, triggering the formation of a solid precipitate, thereby integrating the Fe and silica closely.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
 • The co‑precipitation inherently represents the deposition process, ensuring strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions.

3. Drying  
 • The precipitate is dried at 120 °C.

4. Calcination  
 • Calcination is conducted at 550 °C for 6 hours to develop the desired iron carbide phases, with observations of precipitate formation verifying successful synthesis.

This structured approach facilitates reproducibility and allows researchers to select the synthesis pathway best suited to their specific catalyst performance objectives.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Si

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 7.39 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O, 7.21 g PVP, 800 mL anhydrous ethanol  
- **Conditions**: Room temperature; stir continuously for 5 hours  
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve 7.39 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O in 800 mL ethanol. Stir for 5 hours to form a homogeneous solution.
  - Add 7.21 g PVP to stabilize the Fe₂O₃ precursor, ensuring no nanoparticle aggregation.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and a uniform dispersion of precursors before further processing.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly  
- **Reagents**: 25 mL TEOS, 144 mL NH₃∙H₂O (25%), 1656 mL deionized water, 1276 mL ethanol, CTAB as structure-directing agent  
- **Conditions**: Stirring continuously for 6 hours at room temperature  
- **Process**:
  - Add TEOS and CTAB into the precursor solution. Introduce the NH₃∙H₂O and deionized water mixture gradually while maintaining vigorous stirring.  
  - Allow TEOS hydrolysis to form a mesoporous SiO₂ shell around the iron cores.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain a continuous stirring to ensure uniform shell formation and proper mesoporous structure.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**: 
  - Filter and wash the solid three times with distilled water.
  - Dry at 120 °C for 24 hours in air.
  - Calcine at 550 °C for 6 hours in an air atmosphere.
- **Key Steps**: Drying and calcination conditions are critical to form the SiO₂ shell and stabilize the final structure.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe/Si

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 2 M Fe(NO₃)₃ solution, mesoporous SiO₂ (10.0 g)  
- **Conditions**: Incipient wetness impregnation; stirring for 5 hours  
- **Process**:
  - Impregnate 10.0 g m-SiO₂ with the 2 M Fe(NO₃)₃ ethanolic solution under stirring.
  
##### Step 2: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:
  - Filter and wash thoroughly using distilled water and ethanol.
  - Dry at 120 °C for 24 hours.
  - Optionally, transfer to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heat at 180 °C for 18 hours before final calcination at 550 °C for 6 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Emphasize post-impregnation treatment to promote strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions.

#### Catalyst Preparation [3]: Fe‑Si

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O, silica sol, NH₃∙H₂O (adjust to pH 9.0)  
- **Conditions**: 85 °C, maintain pH 9.0 ± 0.1  
- **Process**:
  - Simultaneously mix Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and silica sol in distilled water.
  - Add NH₃∙H₂O gradually while keeping the solution at 85 °C and pH near 9.0 to form a uniform precipitate.
  
##### Step 2: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:
  - Filter and wash the precipitate thoroughly.
  - Dry at 120 °C (duration as per protocol) and then calcine at 550 °C for 6 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Precise pH control during co-precipitation yields a well-dispersed iron-silica matrix favorable for in situ carburization.

Each workflow strictly follows the sequence: precursor preparation → catalyst assembly (where applicable) → purification → drying → calcination, ensuring reproducibility and clear control over Fe–SiO₂ interactions critical for optimal catalyst performance.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑Ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: The catalyst is first activated by degassing under vacuum at 90 °C for 120 min followed by 350 °C for 480 min. The sample is then cooled to room temperature and loaded into the diffractometer for phase identification by comparing diffraction peaks with standard JCPDF data. This analysis tracks the evolution from hematite to iron carbides.
- **Instruments and Models**: D8 ADVANCE diffractometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Uses Co Kα radiation (wavelength = 1.79026 Å) at 35 kV and 40 mA.
- **Test Parameters**: Scan range of 30°–90° with a step size of 0.04°.
- **Key Notes**: Correct sample alignment is essential to avoid diffraction errors.

### 4.2 H₂‑Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: Approximately 100 mg of catalyst is loaded into a U‑type tube reactor. The temperature is ramped from 50 to 800 °C at 10 °C/min under a controlled flow of 10% H₂/90% Ar. Water produced is removed using isopropyl alcohol gel at –97 °C while a thermal conductivity detector monitors the gas composition.
- **Instruments and Models**: U‑type tube reactor with a TCD system.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Gas flow rate is maintained at 50 mL/min.
- **Test Parameters**: Specific ramping program ensuring reproducible reduction profiles.
- **Key Notes**: Total H₂ consumption (e.g., 0.95 mol H₂/mol Fe for Fe@Si) is derived from summing the reduction peaks.

### 4.3 BET Surface Area Analysis (N₂ Physisorption)
- **Operational Description**: Samples are degassed under vacuum at 90 °C for 120 min and then at 350 °C for 480 min using the ASAP 2420 instrument, followed by N₂ adsorption at –196 °C.
- **Instruments and Models**: ASAP 2420.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements taken within relative pressures (P/P₀) of 0.4–0.9.
- **Test Parameters**: Mesoporous structure analysis yielding pore sizes (e.g., ~2.62 nm).
- **Key Notes**: Ensures accurate surface area evaluation.

### 4.4 TEM and EDS
- **Operational Description**: Samples are dispersed in ethanol for 20 minutes and deposited onto a carbon membrane. Imaging is performed to observe morphology and elemental distribution.
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Talos F200X operated at 200 kV.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Accelerating voltage of 200 kV with EDS mapping.
- **Test Parameters**: Imaging conditions optimized to confirm the embedded structure.
- **Key Notes**: Validates uniform Fe distribution in the catalyst.

### 4.5 Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: Measurements are conducted using an MR‑351 constant‐acceleration spectrometer with a triangular reference signal at 50 K. Spectral fitting (singlets, doublets, sextets) is performed using nonlinear least‐squares analysis.
- **Instruments and Models**: MR‑351 spectrometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Calibration with a 330 kOe α‑Fe foil field at 50 K.
- **Test Parameters**: Detailed fitting parameters to quantify iron carbide phases.
- **Key Notes**: Provides accurate phase composition analysis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Activation temperature is critical for driving the reduction of Fe₂O₃ precursors to the active iron carbide phases. In this study, all catalysts are activated at 350 °C. This choice of temperature is a compromise that enables effective reduction and subsequent carburization while minimizing sintering. For the Fe@Si catalyst, in particular, the moderate temperature in a syngas environment helps to maintain high metal dispersion due to the protective SiO₂ shell.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
The catalyst activation is performed under atmospheric pressure (approximately 1 bar or 0.1 MPa). Operating at such mild pressure conditions aids the controlled reduction kinetics by allowing efficient removal of byproducts like H₂O without imposing excessive diffusion limitations. This condition is uniformly applied across catalysts synthesized by different methods.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
A syngas mixture with a hydrogen-to-carbon monoxide ratio of 2 is employed during activation. This specific gas composition is selected to support the stepwise reduction of iron oxides—first to intermediate species and then to active iron carbides essential for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. The hydrogen-rich syngas environment promotes reduction while balancing any risk of over-carburization or side reactions.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
A prolonged activation period of 24 hours is implemented. This duration ensures that the transformation of calcined iron oxides into their active phases is thorough, with sufficient time devoted to both reduction and carburization processes. The extended activation is particularly beneficial for catalysts like Fe@Si, where the SiO₂ shell plays a dual role in preserving dispersion and moderating the reduction rate.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Space-Time Velocity  
No distinct space-time velocity is defined for the activation step. Although a flow rate of 5000 mL/(g_cat·h) is reported for the subsequent catalytic testing, the catalyst pre-treatment relies solely on the controlled temperature, pressure, gas composition, and time parameters to achieve reproducible activation outcomes.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter that governs the activation of syngas and the formation of active iron carbides. In this study, the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis tests were conducted at an exact temperature of 280 °C. This temperature not only facilitates the reduction of iron oxides but also contributes to maintaining catalyst performance during the reaction. The stability of the catalysts over time is attributed to the balanced conditions established at this temperature, although oxidation of iron carbides by H₂O remains a factor influencing deactivation in some catalyst systems.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The system pressure plays a key role in enhancing the partial pressures of the syngas components, which is essential for driving the Fischer-Tropsch reaction toward desirable hydrocarbon products. The experimental setup maintained a pressure of 2.0 MPa in a fixed-bed reactor. This elevated pressure increases the concentration of reactants, thereby influencing product selectivity toward heavier hydrocarbons under syngas-rich conditions.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio is pivotal in steering the product distribution between methane and longer-chain hydrocarbons. In all catalytic evaluations reported, an H₂/CO ratio of 2.0 was employed. This specific ratio is instrumental in optimizing the balance between methane formation and the generation of C₅⁺ products. The study notes that while a higher hydrogen ratio can increase methane selectivity, maintaining a H₂/CO ratio of 2.0 supports the formation of a wider distribution of hydrocarbon chains with adequate catalyst carburization.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
Space-time velocity, expressed as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), is defined as the ratio of the syngas flow rate to the catalyst mass. The experimental conditions specify a GHSV of 5000 mL∙h⁻¹∙g_cat⁻¹. This parameter is critical because it influences the residence time of reactants in the reactor, directly affecting CO conversion efficiency and catalyst stability. Observations suggest that while higher flow rates can sometimes reduce CO conversion, they also help in limiting the extent of catalyst deactivation, thereby enhancing long-term operation reliability.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD). This technique identifies the presence and transformation of iron species, which are critical for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS) activity.
- **Findings:** For the Fe@Si catalyst, XRD patterns of the fresh sample display well-crystallized hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) with characteristic peaks at 2θ = 38.7°, 41.7°, 63.9°, and 74.1°. After activation, strong diffraction peaks corresponding to iron carbides emerge, confirming the transformation from hematite to the active carbide phase. The Fe/Si catalyst similarly shows the conversion of hematite to iron carbides after activation, with pronounced carbide peaks. In contrast, the Fe–Si catalyst exhibits broad peaks at 2θ = 42° and 75°, indicative of amorphous iron oxides and smaller crystallites, reflecting strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions that suppress carbide crystallization.
- **Observations:** The extent of phase transformation, as evidenced by the intensity and sharpness of iron carbide peaks, is highest in Fe@Si and Fe/Si, while Fe–Si retains more amorphous or poorly crystalline iron oxides due to stronger metal–support interaction.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Iron Carbide Content
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to quantify the phase composition and specifically the iron carbide content, which is directly linked to catalytic performance in FTS.
- **Findings:** After activation, the iron carbide content is 52.01% for Fe@Si, 55.75% for Fe/Si, and 29.67% for Fe–Si. For Fe–Si, the iron carbide fraction increases to 34.78% after reaction, indicating ongoing carburization during FTS.
- **Observations:** The higher initial iron carbide content in Fe@Si and Fe/Si correlates with greater initial FTS activity, while the gradual increase in Fe–Si reflects its slower activation due to strong Fe–SiO₂ bonding.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET) analysis was conducted to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which influence reactant accessibility and catalyst dispersion.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si exhibits a high BET surface area of 652 m²/g, a pore volume of 0.64 cm³/g, and an average pore size of 2.62 nm. Fe/Si shows similar values: 642 m²/g surface area, 0.55 cm³/g pore volume, and 2.55 nm pore size. Fe–Si, prepared by coprecipitation, has a lower surface area (295 m²/g) and a larger average pore size (6.28 nm).
- **Observations:** The synthesis method significantly impacts textural properties; the Stöber and impregnation methods yield higher surface areas and smaller, more uniform pores, while coprecipitation leads to larger, less uniform pores.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** H₂-temperature programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was used to assess the reducibility of iron species and the strength of Fe–SiO₂ interactions.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si shows a total H₂ consumption of 0.95 mol H₂/mol Fe, while Fe/Si consumes 1.00 mol H₂/mol Fe. The Fe/Si catalyst displays reduction peaks at 340 °C, 437 °C, 594 °C, 664 °C, and 709 °C, corresponding to sequential reduction and carbide formation. Fe–Si exhibits a lower total H₂ consumption (0.42 mol H₂/mol Fe) with peaks at lower temperatures, reflecting hindered reduction due to strong Fe–SiO₂ bonding.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si increases diffusion resistance for H₂O removal, suppressing reduction and carburization, while strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction in Fe–Si further limits reducibility.

#### 6.1.5 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were used to examine particle morphology, size, and dispersion.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si displays a core–shell structure with Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles (~50.4 nm) embedded in a porous SiO₂ shell, maintaining an average size of ~45.1 nm after reaction and showing minimal aggregation. Fe/Si exhibits significant aggregation and sintering of iron species due to the absence of a protective shell. Fe–Si shows evenly distributed iron species with some slight aggregation after reaction.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively inhibits sintering, preserving particle dispersion, while Fe/Si is prone to aggregation, leading to deactivation during FTS.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (280 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹), initial CO conversions were:
  - Fe/Si: 75.0%
  - Fe@Si: 66.6%
  - Fe–Si: 10.1%
  After 64 hours on stream, conversions declined to:
  - Fe/Si: 55.7%
  - Fe@Si: 47.9%
  - Fe–Si: 29.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe/Si and Fe@Si exhibited high initial activity but gradual deactivation, while Fe–Si showed low initial conversion with a notable increase over time, attributed to ongoing carburization.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating water–gas shift (WGS) activity:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 64 h:
  - Fe@Si: 24.3%
  - Fe/Si: 27.1%
  - Fe–Si: 46.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe–Si displayed the highest CO₂ selectivity, linked to strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction and enhanced WGS activity. The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si increased local H₂O partial pressure, also elevating CO₂ selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 64 h:
  - Fe@Si: 24.4%
  - Fe/Si: 19.5%
  - Fe–Si: 17.9%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@Si exhibited the highest CH₄ selectivity, attributed to hindered desorption of heavier hydrocarbons by the SiO₂ shell, promoting secondary hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5^+,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 64 h:
  - Fe@Si: 30.2%
  - Fe/Si: 31.0%
  - Fe–Si: 25.1%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe/Si and Fe@Si achieved similar C₅⁺ selectivity, while Fe–Si, despite lower initial activity, favored heavier hydrocarbon formation as carburization progressed.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination, influencing hydrocarbon distribution (0 < α < 1).
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not numerically reported, trends indicate:
  - Fe–Si exhibits the highest α, correlating with higher C₅⁺ selectivity.
  - Fe@Si shows a lower α, consistent with higher CH₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction in Fe–Si promotes chain growth, while the SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si favors chain termination.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate is assessed by the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over 64 h, CO conversion for Fe@Si decreased from 66.6% to 47.9%, and for Fe/Si from 75.0% to 55.7%. Fe–Si increased from 10.1% to 29.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si deactivated due to iron carbide oxidation (by H₂O) and sintering, respectively. The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si inhibited sintering but increased H₂O retention, accelerating carbide oxidation. Fe–Si’s activity improved as more iron carbides formed during reaction.
