## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe₃O₄@χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalysts is executed in a clearly defined sequence that enables high reproducibility and precise facet control. The experimental workflow is organized as follows:

#### • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
1. In a four‑neck flask, octadecylamine and cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) are combined under an inert nitrogen atmosphere.  
2. The reaction mixture is degassed to remove oxygen, establishing an anaerobic environment for controlled nucleation of Fe₃O₄ nanocrystals.  
3. The solution is heated to 120 °C, initiating the formation of well‑defined Fe₃O₄ nanocubes and octahedra.

#### • Precipitation–Deposition  
1. After heating, the reaction mixture is cooled to room temperature.  
2. Ethanol is added to precipitate the formed nanocrystals.  
3. The precipitate is washed multiple times with hexane to remove residual impurities, ensuring clean surfaces for subsequent steps.

#### • Drying  
- The washed product is dried under controlled conditions (specific drying temperature and duration are maintained according to standardized laboratory protocols) to preserve particle morphology.

#### • Reduction  
1. The clean Fe₃O₄ nanocubes undergo a reduction step under a stream of hydrogen (H₂) with a gas‐flow rate of 100 mL min⁻¹ at 270 °C for 10 hours.  
2. This step removes surface organics and converts the Fe₃O₄ surface to metallic Fe, which is essential for the catalytic phase transformation.

#### • Post‑processing (Surface Reconstruction)  
1. The hydrogen‑reduced samples are transferred to a fixed‑bed reactor.  
2. Under 20 bar syngas—with precise composition (32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, 4 vol% Ar)—the samples are treated at 270 °C for 20 hours.  
3. This controlled exposure induces a surface reconstruction, forming a carbon‑rich χ‑Fe₅C₂ shell over the Fe₃O₄ core.  
4. Distinct synthesis protocols allow the selective generation of nanocubes exposing {202} facets or octahedra with {112} facets.

This systematic approach—with exact control over reaction conditions, reagent concentrations, and equipment configuration—ensures reproducibility and facilitates a direct correlation between synthesis parameters and catalytic performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis comprises two sequential steps designed to first remove surface organics and reduce the precursor, then to induce controlled carburization for forming the active carbide shell.

#### 5.1.1 Catalyst Reduction Activation
• **Definition:** This step involves the removal of surface organic species and the reduction of Fe₃O₄ nanocubes to generate an active metallic surface, which serves as a precursor for subsequent reconstruction.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The initial reduction is performed under a 1 bar H₂ atmosphere at 270 °C with a gas flow rate of 100 mL/min. The catalyst is maintained under these conditions for 10 hours with a controlled heating ramp of 1 °C/min.  
• **Observations:** These conditions effectively cleanse the catalyst surface and prepare the structure for carburization, ensuring that the subsequent formation of iron carbide occurs on a well-reduced and clean surface.

#### 5.1.2 Surface Reconstruction Activation
• **Definition:** In this stage, the catalyst undergoes a surface reconstruction process that transforms its surface into a carbon-rich χ-Fe₅C₂ shell. This active phase is crucial for enhancing catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch reactions by establishing a defined core–shell structure.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The reconstruction is executed under syngas at 20 bar pressure and 270 °C. The reaction gas consists of 32 vol% H₂, 64 vol% CO, and 4 vol% Ar. A space-time velocity of 2400 mL/(h·g_cat) is applied, and the treatment is carried out for 20 hours.  
• **Observations:** Under these controlled conditions, the dynamic balance between CO-induced carburization and H₂-assisted reduction facilitates the formation of a uniform χ-Fe₅C₂ shell. This step is critical to achieve well-defined exposed facets, such as {202} for nanocubes and {112} for octahedra, which are directly linked to the catalyst’s activity and stability in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

This two-step activation process, combining initial reduction and subsequent surface reconstruction, ensures that the catalyst exhibits optimized structural and reactive properties necessary for high-performance Fischer–Tropsch conversion.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis because it governs syngas activation and influences the catalyst reaction pathways. In the present evaluation, the reactor was maintained at 270 °C. At this temperature, the activation energy for the hydrogen‐assisted CO dissociation pathway on Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes is reported as 68.6 kJ/mol—substantially lower than that for the direct CO dissociation observed on the octahedral counterparts. This temperature selection ensures that the nanocube catalysts exhibit high activity and stability, with negligible fluctuation in CO conversion over extended runs.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The overall pressure in the fixed‐bed reactor plays a significant role by affecting syngas concentration and product distribution. In this study, the reaction pressure was strictly maintained at 20 bar. This condition is essential not only for achieving an optimal balance between conversion and selectivity but also for ensuring reproducibility in catalyst performance. Although detailed pressure effects on C₅⁺ hydrocarbon formation were not elaborated, maintaining 20 bar is central to standardizing the reaction environment for both catalyst morphologies.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The hydrogen to carbon monoxide ratio in the feed gas is determinative of the hydrocarbon product slate. Based on the reported reaction conditions, the syngas composition upholds a CO:H₂ ratio of 1:2, which is equivalently expressed as an H₂/CO ratio of 2.0. This ratio influences the competing reaction pathways: while higher H₂/CO ratios tend to favor methane formation, conditions with this ratio have been found suitable for obtaining the desired balance between olefins and paraffins. 

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
Space-time velocity is defined as the volumetric flow rate of syngas per unit mass of the catalyst. In these experiments, a value of 800 mL/(g_cat·h) was employed. This parameter directly affects the reactant residence time, influencing the extent of CO conversion and ensuring that the conditions remain consistent across catalyst evaluations. Notably, maintaining a controlled space-time velocity allowed reliable comparisons of catalytic performance and catalyst stability, a necessity in discerning the activity differences between the nanocubes and octahedra.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Philips X'Pert Pro diffractometer equipped with monochromatized Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1542 nm). Phase identification was supported by comparison with simulated fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis confirmed the presence of Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂ phases in both nanocube and octahedral catalysts. After 5 hours of syngas treatment, distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to χ-Fe₅C₂ appeared alongside Fe₃O₄ and metallic Fe, indicating successful formation of the core–shell structure. Quantitative XRD analysis showed χ-Fe₅C₂ accounted for 29.8 wt% in nanocubes, consistent with Mössbauer spectroscopy results.
- **Observations:** The emergence of χ-Fe₅C₂ diffraction peaks after extended syngas exposure demonstrates the transformation of the shell and the stability of the core–shell architecture under reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Facet Exposure
- **Description:** Morphology, particle size, and facet exposure were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM), and FFT analysis. Imaging was performed using a Hitachi H-7700 (100 kV) and JEOL ARM-200F (200 kV).
- **Findings:** TEM and HAADF-STEM images revealed well-defined core–shell nanocubes and octahedra with average sizes of ~40 nm. The shell thickness was measured as 2.0 nm for nanocubes and 1.7 nm for octahedra. FFT analysis identified the nanocube shells as exposing the {202} facets of χ-Fe₅C₂, while octahedra exposed the {112} facets. The epitaxial relationship between core and shell was confirmed by lattice spacing assignments.
- **Observations:** The uniform shell coverage and precise facet exposure highlight the effectiveness of the reconstruction strategy in controlling catalyst surface structure, which is critical for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Distribution and Core–Shell Verification
- **Description:** Elemental mapping was conducted using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) on the JEOL ARM-200F at 200 kV to distinguish elemental distributions within the core–shell structure.
- **Findings:** STEM-EDX and EELS mapping demonstrated that Fe and O were concentrated in the core (Fe₃O₄), while Fe and C dominated the shell (χ-Fe₅C₂). Line-scan profiles confirmed the spatial separation of O (core) and C (shell), with Fe distributed throughout.
- **Observations:** These results provide direct evidence for the successful formation of the Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ core–shell architecture and validate the synthetic approach.

#### 6.1.4 Phase Quantification by Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to quantify the relative phase contents and probe the chemical state of iron in the catalysts.
- **Findings:** For Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes, Mössbauer spectra indicated 63.4 wt% Fe₃O₄, 33.2 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂, and 3.4 wt% Fe(II)/Fe(III) species. Octahedra contained 65.1 wt% Fe₃O₄ and 29.5 wt% χ-Fe₅C₂. These values closely matched XRD quantification.
- **Observations:** The high proportion of χ-Fe₅C₂ in the shell is consistent with the intended design and is essential for Fischer–Tropsch activity.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and pore structure were measured by N₂ adsorption–desorption using the BET method, with pore size distribution analyzed by the BJH method.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area was 20.4 m² g⁻¹ for nanocubes and 22.0 m² g⁻¹ for octahedra. Both exhibited nonporous surface layers initially. After 100 hours on stream, spent octahedra developed mesopores with an average diameter of 16.0 nm, while nanocubes remained nonporous.
- **Observations:** The development of mesoporosity in octahedra after reaction suggests structural degradation, correlating with observed catalyst deactivation.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Fe Atom Quantification by CO Pulse Chemisorption
- **Description:** CO pulse chemisorption was performed on a Micromeritics Autochem 2920 analyzer to determine the number of surface Fe atoms for turnover frequency (TOF) calculations.
- **Findings:** Samples (100 mg) were pretreated in He at 270 °C for 5 h, cooled to 50 °C, and subjected to CO/He pulses (10 vol% CO, 90 vol% He) until saturation. The amount of adsorbed CO was used to calculate metal dispersion, assuming a 1:1 CO:Fe surface atom ratio.
- **Observations:** Accurate quantification of surface Fe atoms enabled reliable TOF determination, facilitating direct comparison of intrinsic catalytic activities between morphologies.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (270 °C, 20 bar, syngas H₂/CO/Ar), Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC achieved a CO conversion of 45.4%. For Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ octahedra/SiC, a lower space velocity (800 mL h⁻¹ g_cat⁻¹) was used to reach a comparable CO conversion of 42.6%. At standard space velocity, octahedra/SiC exhibited a lower conversion of 21.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes consistently demonstrated higher CO conversion than octahedra under equivalent conditions, attributed to the exposure of {202} facets, which favor a hydrogen-assisted CO dissociation pathway with lower activation energy.

#### 6.2.2 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO that forms hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key indicator of chain growth and desired product distribution in FTS.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC exhibited a C₅⁺ selectivity of 44.8 C%, while octahedra/SiC showed a lower value of 27.6 C%. The olefin/paraffin (o/p) ratio was also higher for nanocubes (≈1.1) compared to octahedra (≈0.6).
- **Observations and Trends:** The nanocubes favored longer-chain hydrocarbon formation and higher olefin content, reflecting the influence of exposed {202} facets on product selectivity.

#### 6.2.3 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended operation. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Over a 100-hour stability test, nanocubes/SiC maintained CO conversion within 1% fluctuation, indicating negligible deactivation. In contrast, octahedra/SiC showed a decline from 20.1% to 11.7% CO conversion, corresponding to a 41.8% decrease over 100 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** Nanocubes demonstrated superior stability, while octahedra suffered from structural collapse and increased carbon deposition, leading to significant deactivation.

#### 6.2.4 Turnover Frequency (TOF)
- **Description:** TOF measures the number of CO molecules converted per surface Fe atom per hour, reflecting intrinsic catalytic activity. It is determined using CO pulse chemisorption to quantify surface Fe atoms.
- **Experimental Data:** Nanocubes/SiC achieved a TOF of 645.9 h⁻¹, whereas octahedra/SiC reached 372.7 h⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher TOF for nanocubes correlates with their enhanced activity and the presence of {202} facets, which facilitate a more efficient reaction pathway.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, typically ranging from 0 to 1.
- **Experimental Data:** The study notes that facet sensitivity does not significantly affect the α factor; specific values are not reported.
- **Observations and Trends:** Chain growth probability remains largely unchanged between nanocubes and octahedra, suggesting that facet engineering primarily influences activity and selectivity rather than chain length distribution.

**Summary:**  
The Fe₃O₄@χ-Fe₅C₂ nanocubes/SiC catalyst, characterized by exposed {202} facets, outperforms the octahedra/SiC counterpart in CO conversion, C₅⁺ selectivity, TOF, and long-term stability. These results underscore the critical role of facet engineering in optimizing Fischer–Tropsch catalyst performance.
