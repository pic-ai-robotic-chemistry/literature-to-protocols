## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article describes three synthesis approaches for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts that emphasize reproducibility, precise reaction control, and post‑processing for catalyst stabilization. Each method follows a defined sequence:

#### • Fe@Si Catalyst – Two‑Step Strategy  
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O, PVP, and anhydrous ethanol.  
   - Equipment: Teflon‑lined autoclave.  
   - Conditions: React at 180 °C for 18 h to form Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles with PVP acting as an inhibitor to aggregation.  
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - In this step, TEOS is added in a mixture comprising 1276 mL ethanol, 1656 mL deionized water, and 144 mL of 25% NH₃⋅H₂O, with CTAB serving as the templating agent for mesoporous SiO₂ shell growth.  
3. Drying and Calcination  
   - Process: Washing followed by drying and calcination at 550 °C for 6 h to remove templates and stabilize the structure.

#### • Fe/Si Catalyst – Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
1. Precursor Mixing  
   - Reagents: Impregnate m‑SiO₂ with a 2 M ethanol solution of Fe(NO₃)₃.  
   - Process: The incipient wetness impregnation is carried out for 5 h.  
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Integrated as a controlled hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 18 h to enhance the dispersion of iron species within the support.  
3. Drying and Calcination  
   - Post‑synthesis steps include filtration, washing, drying at 120 °C for 24 h, and calcination at 550 °C for 6 h.

#### • Fe‑Si Catalyst – Co‑precipitation Technique  
1. Precursor Mixing  
   - Reagents: Simultaneous addition of Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and silica sol (30 wt.% SiO₂) in distilled water.  
   - Conditions: Reaction maintained at pH ~9 and 85 °C to drive uniform precipitation and deposition.  
2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - The process is monitored by observable phenomena (color change and precipitate formation) ensuring proper incorporation of iron with silica.  
3. Drying and Calcination  
   - The precipitate is recovered, washed, dried at 120 °C, and finally calcined at 550 °C for 6 h.

A comparative analysis shows that although the Fe@Si method requires specialized equipment and two discrete synthesis steps, it delivers a stable, embedded structure. In contrast, the Fe/Si method is more scalable and simpler, while the co‑precipitation approach for the Fe‑Si catalyst offers a cost-effective route with controlled morphology through precise pH and temperature conditions.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe@Si Catalyst  
- **Anhydrous Ethanol**: Poured 800 mL into the vessel containing the weighed Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and PVP to form the initial precursor solution.  
- **Deionized Water**: In a subsequent step, 1656 mL of deionized water is added along with other components (e.g., NH₃∙H₂O) to prepare the hydrolysis environment before transferring the yellow suspension into a stainless steel autoclave.

##### 3.1.2.2 Fe/Si Catalyst  
- **Ethanol Solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ (2 M)**: Transferred using a syringe into the pre-weighed m-SiO₂ under continuous stirring over a 5‑hour impregnation period.

##### 3.1.2.3 Fe‐Si Catalyst  
- **Distilled Water with Silica Sol**: Poured concurrently with Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O into the reaction vessel to ensure simultaneous mixing while maintaining the target pH.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe@Si Catalyst  
- **Precursor Mixture**: Stirred continuously for 5 hours at room temperature to ensure complete dissolution before further addition of TEOS. Afterwards, the mixture is strongly stirred for 48 hours to promote uniform shell growth.

##### 3.1.3.2 Fe/Si Catalyst  
- **Impregnated Suspension**: Mixed under continuous stirring for 5 hours, allowing the ethanol solution and m-SiO₂ to interact thoroughly for effective impregnation.

##### 3.1.3.3 Fe‐Si Catalyst  
- **Co‑precipitated System**: The simultaneous addition of Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and silica sol is mixed while carefully maintaining a pH of 9.0 ± 0.1 at 85 °C, ensuring homogeneous co-precipitation of iron species and silica.

These grouped procedures—detailed by catalyst and by the nature of the operation—provide a reproducible methodology suited for advanced Fischer–Tropsch catalyst fabrication.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Si Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Synthesis of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles  
- **Reagents**: 7.39 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O, 7.21 g PVP, 800 mL anhydrous ethanol  
- **Conditions**: Dissolution at room temperature with stirring until a homogeneous solution is achieved.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve the precursors in ethanol to ensure intimate mixing.  
  - Transfer the solution into a Teflon-lined autoclave and maintain at 180 °C for 18 hours. This temperature and duration ensure complete nucleation and crystallization of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain constant stirring and verify solution homogeneity before sealing the autoclave.

##### Step 2: SiO₂ Shell Formation  
- **Reagents**: TEOS, EtOH, deionized water, NH₃∙H₂O, CTAB  
- **Conditions**: Controlled hydrolysis with initial addition of TEOS over 6 hours; pH maintained by NH₃∙H₂O; room temperature stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  - Disperse the Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles in an EtOH-water mixture with CTAB as template.  
  - Slowly add TEOS while adjusting pH to promote hydrolysis.  
  - Continue stirring to allow uniform deposition of the SiO₂ shell on the nanoparticles.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure even addition of TEOS to direct mesoporous shell formation.

##### Step 3: Drying, Calcination, and Activation  
- **Drying**: Dry the catalyst at 120 °C for 24 hours.  
- **Calcination**: Ramp to 550 °C with a controlled rate, calcine for 6 hours in air to remove CTAB and stabilize the structure.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor temperature to prevent nanoparticle sintering while ensuring complete template removal.

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe/Si Catalyst

##### Step 1: Incipient Wetness Impregnation  
- **Reagents**: m-SiO₂ and 2 M ethanol solution of Fe(NO₃)₃  
- **Process**:  
  - Impregnate m-SiO₂ evenly using the Fe(NO₃)₃ solution.  
  - Allow for gradual solvent evaporation, followed by hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 18 hours to achieve uniform dispersion.  

##### Step 2: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- Follow similar washing, drying at 120 °C, and calcination at 550 °C for 6 hours.

#### Catalyst Preparation [3]: Fe‐Si Catalyst

- **Conditions**: Reaction maintained at 85 °C with continuous stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  - Simultaneously mix the solutions to ensure intimate interaction between iron species and silica.  
  - Maintain pH and temperature to promote controlled precipitation.  

##### Step 2: Recovery, Drying & Calcination  
- Filter, wash the precipitate; dry at 120 °C and calcine at 550 °C for 6 hours.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑Ray Diffraction (XRD)  
- **Operational Description**: X‑RD patterns were collected to determine the crystalline structure and phase composition of the catalysts.  
- **Instruments and Models**: D8 ADVANCE diffractometer by Bruker.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Utilized Co Kα radiation (λ = 1.79026 Å) at 35 kV and 40 mA; measurements were performed in a 2θ range of 30°–90° with a step size of 0.04°.  
- **Test Parameters**: Samples were degassed under vacuum at 90 °C for 120 minutes and then at 350 °C for 480 minutes before analysis.  
- **Key Notes**: Phase identification was verified by comparison with standard JCPDF references; proper sample alignment minimizes systematic errors.

### 4.2 Hydrogen Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)  
- **Operational Description**: This method evaluates the reduction behavior and Fe–SiO₂ interaction by monitoring H₂ consumption during a controlled temperature ramp.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Performed in a U‑type tube reactor using an Autochem II 2920 setup.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Approximately 100 mg of catalyst is loaded and the temperature is ramped from 50 °C to 800 °C at 10 °C/min under a 10% H₂/90% Ar atmosphere delivered at 50 mL/min.  
- **Test Parameters**: Catalyst pretreatment involves the same degassing schedule as for XRD; a water removal step using isopropyl alcohol gel at −97 °C is applied.  
- **Key Notes**: Uniform loading is crucial for reproducibility; reduction profiles help quantify H₂ consumption, which is linked to catalyst performance.

### 4.3 BET Surface Area Analysis (N₂ Physisorption)  
- **Operational Description**: Textural properties such as surface area, pore volume, and pore size are determined by N₂ physisorption at −196 °C.  
- **Instruments and Models**: ASAP 2420 analyzer.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples undergo the standard degassing: vacuum treatment at 90 °C for 120 minutes followed by 350 °C for 480 minutes.  
- **Test Parameters**: The BET method calculates surface area from the adsorption data.  
- **Key Notes**: This analysis directly compares porosity among catalyst types.

### 4.4 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) & Energy‑Dispersive X‑ray Spectroscopy (EDS)  
- **Operational Description**: TEM and EDS mapping visualize catalyst morphology and elemental distribution to assess particle dispersion and sintering.  
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Talos F200X operating at 200 kV.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples are dispersed in ethanol for 20 minutes and deposited on a carbon membrane.  
- **Test Parameters**: Preparation ensures minimal aggregation for reproducible imaging.  
- **Key Notes**: Critical for verifying morphological integrity following synthesis.

### 4.5 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)  
- **Operational Description**: FTIR identifies functional groups and bonding environments in the catalysts.  
- **Instruments and Models**: VERTEX 70 FT‑IR spectrophotometer.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Spectra are recorded over 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹.  
- **Test Parameters**: Catalysts are mixed with KBr at a 1:50 ratio and pressed into translucent disks.  
- **Key Notes**: High resolution is essential for accurate peak assignment.

### 4.6 Mössbauer Spectroscopy  
- **Operational Description**: This technique monitors phase evolution, particularly the formation of iron carbides pre‑ and post‑Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Specific instrument details are not provided.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements are conducted at 50 K.  
- **Test Parameters**: Data analysis involves deconvolution of spectral features into singlets, doublets, and sextets.  
- **Key Notes**: Essential for understanding catalyst phase transformation and reactivity evolution.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation procedure is performed as a dedicated pre-reaction treatment step aimed at converting the precursor into an active phase suitable for Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. All activation parameters are strictly extracted from the article and are specific to the Fischer-Tropsch catalyst pre-treatment process.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for initiating reduction and carburization of the catalyst precursor, thereby exposing active sites.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 350 °C in a fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** This temperature facilitates the controlled reduction of iron oxides and initiates the transformation towards active iron carbide phases, although it results in partial reduction which is later complemented during the reaction.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The activation pressure sets the environment in which gas–catalyst interactions occur, influencing reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is carried out at atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Operating at atmospheric pressure ensures a gentle treatment, minimizing overly aggressive conditions that could lead to catalyst agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition is pivotal for controlling the reduction process by providing the necessary reductants.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to a syngas atmosphere consisting of hydrogen and carbon monoxide in a 2:1 ratio (H₂/CO = 2).
- **Observations:** This balanced syngas mixture ensures effective reduction while also stabilizing the developing catalyst structure.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity typically regulates the gas flow relative to the catalyst mass; however, its role here is limited as it is not specified for the activation process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** No explicit gas hourly space velocity is reported for the activation step.
- **Observations:** The design focuses on fixed operational conditions in the reactor rather than modifying flow rates during activation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation determines the extent of the catalyst transformation, balancing complete reduction against potential sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** This extended activation time allows thorough pre-reaction treatment, ensuring that active phases are uniformly developed while maintaining catalyst stability.

This activation protocol, implemented uniformly for all catalyst types (Fe@Si, Fe/Si, and Fe‐Si), prepares the catalysts for subsequent Fischer-Tropsch synthesis by achieving a consistent pre-reaction state conducive to high catalytic performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was conducted in a fixed-bed reactor under rigorously controlled conditions to ensure reproducibility and facilitate direct comparison between catalyst formulations. The following subsections detail the key experimental parameters extracted directly from the article.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is critical for syngas activation and directly influences the kinetics of carbon chain growth as well as the carburization of the iron phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reactor was maintained at 280 °C during all catalytic evaluations.
- **Observations:** At 280 °C, catalysts such as Fe@Si exhibited high initial activity, though deactivation occurred over time due to oxidation of iron carbides. In contrast, the Fe‐Si catalyst displayed a gradual increase in activity attributed to continuous carburization.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure affects the concentration of syngas in the reactor, thereby impacting hydrogenation reactions and subsequent hydrocarbon selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was performed at a pressure of 2.0 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at 2.0 MPa enhanced syngas concentration, favoring the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons. This pressure also played a role in balancing the trade-off between initial activity and long-term stability across different catalyst structures.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is essential for controlling hydrocarbon distribution by influencing the water-gas shift reaction and the formation of reactive intermediates.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A ratio of H₂ to CO equal to 2 was employed.
- **Observations:** This ratio promoted the production of heavier hydrocarbons in some catalysts while simultaneously leading to higher CH₄ selectivity in others, such as the Fe@Si catalyst, due to differences in catalyst confinement and water removal efficiency.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) dictates the residence time of the reactants over the catalyst, affecting conversion levels and product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was set to 5000 mL∙h⁻¹∙g_cat⁻¹.
- **Observations:** The controlled GHSV ensured that variations in conversion and selectivity could be directly correlated to catalyst performance. The fixed-bed reactor design maintained consistent conditions, thereby underpinning the reliability of CO conversion measurements and hydrocarbon distribution throughout the experiments.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural evolution of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy. These analyses are critical for identifying active phases and understanding phase transitions relevant to Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the fresh Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts display characteristic peaks at 2θ = 38.7°, 41.7°, 63.9°, and 74.1°, corresponding to well-crystallized hematite (α-Fe₂O₃). The Fe-Si catalyst exhibits broader peaks, indicating smaller or more amorphous iron oxide crystallites. After activation and FTS, XRD and Mössbauer spectroscopy reveal the formation and evolution of iron carbides and Fe₃O₄. Mössbauer data show that after activation, iron carbide contents are Fe/Si (55.75%), Fe@Si (52.01%), and Fe-Si (29.67%), shifting after 64 h FTS to Fe/Si (49.20%), Fe@Si (41.57%), and Fe-Si (34.78%).
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si preserves the hematite phase and inhibits sintering, while strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction in Fe-Si hinders reduction and carburization, resulting in gradual carbide formation during FTS.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties, including BET surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, were measured by N₂ physisorption at −196 °C. These parameters influence reactant accessibility and product diffusion during FTS.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts exhibit high BET surface areas (652 and 642 m²/g, respectively) and small, uniform pore sizes (2.62 and 2.55 nm), with pore volumes of 0.64 and 0.55 cm³/g. The Fe-Si catalyst shows a lower surface area (295 m²/g), larger average pore size (6.28 nm), and a pore volume of 0.46 cm³/g. All samples display type IV isotherms, with Fe@Si and Fe/Si showing H1-type hysteresis (uniform cylindrical pores) and Fe-Si showing H3-type hysteresis (non-uniform mesopores).
- **Observations:** The use of CTAB as a structure-directing agent in Fe@Si and Fe/Si results in high surface area and uniform mesoporosity, enhancing active site accessibility. The larger pores in Fe-Si may facilitate diffusion but at the expense of surface area.

#### 6.1.3 Reducibility and Fe–SiO₂ Interaction
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) profiles assess the reducibility of iron species and the influence of Fe–SiO₂ interactions, which are pivotal for catalyst activation and phase transformation.
- **Findings:** Total H₂ consumption values (mol H₂/mol Fe) are Fe@Si: 0.95, Fe/Si: 1.00, and Fe-Si: 0.42. The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si hinders H₂O removal and limits accessible reduction sites, resulting in slightly lower H₂ consumption than Fe/Si. The strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction in Fe-Si significantly restricts reduction, as reflected by its low H₂ uptake.
- **Observations:** The degree of reduction is highest in Fe/Si, followed by Fe@Si, and lowest in Fe-Si, directly correlating with the strength of Fe–SiO₂ interaction and physical confinement.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping were used to visualize particle morphology, size distribution, and dispersion, which are crucial for catalyst stability and activity.
- **Findings:** Fe@Si features Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles (average size 50.4 nm) embedded within a porous SiO₂ shell, maintaining their size (45.1 nm) after FTS, indicating minimal sintering. Fe/Si shows significant aggregation and sintering of iron particles, while Fe-Si displays evenly dispersed iron species with only slight aggregation post-reaction.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively prevents sintering and maintains particle isolation, whereas Fe/Si and Fe-Si are more prone to aggregation, impacting long-term stability.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition and Phase Evolution
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) and Mössbauer spectroscopy were employed to determine Fe content and monitor phase evolution during activation and FTS.
- **Findings:** All catalysts have similar Fe loadings (~30–31 wt%). Mössbauer spectroscopy quantifies the evolution of iron carbides and oxides, with Fe@Si and Fe/Si showing a decrease in carbide content after FTS (due to oxidation by H₂O), while Fe-Si exhibits an increase, reflecting ongoing carburization.
- **Observations:** The balance between iron carbide formation and oxidation is closely linked to catalyst structure and Fe–SiO₂ interaction, influencing both activity and deactivation trends during FTS.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 280 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, and GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹, the CO conversion values after 64 h on stream are:
  - Fe@Si: 47.9%
  - Fe/Si: 55.7%
  - Fe–Si: 29.7%
- **Observations and Trends:** Both Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts exhibit high initial CO conversion, but their activity declines over time due to deactivation. In contrast, Fe–Si shows lower initial conversion but a gradual increase in activity, attributed to ongoing carburization of iron species.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After 64 h on stream:
  - Fe@Si: 24.3%
  - Fe/Si: 27.1%
  - Fe–Si: 46.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe–Si exhibits significantly higher CO₂ selectivity, indicating a greater extent of WGS activity. The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si increases local H₂O partial pressure, enhancing CO₂ formation relative to Fe/Si.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, a key indicator of hydrocarbon distribution:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit numerical values are not provided, the study notes that Fe@Si, due to the SiO₂ shell, tends to favor higher CH₄ selectivity compared to Fe–Si, which promotes heavier hydrocarbon formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced confinement in Fe@Si increases CH₄ selectivity, whereas higher dispersion in Fe–Si suppresses methane formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms, reflecting the catalyst’s ability to produce longer-chain products.
- **Experimental Data:** Specific C₃⁺ selectivity values are not detailed, but the Fe–Si catalyst demonstrates the highest selectivity toward heavier hydrocarbons (C₅⁺), consistent with its higher dispersion and smaller iron particle size.
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe–Si catalyst structure favors chain growth, while Fe@Si’s shell confinement limits heavier hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, indicates the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
  - α values closer to 1 favor longer-chain hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not reported, Fe–Si is associated with a higher α, inferred from its greater C₅⁺ selectivity, whereas Fe@Si exhibits a lower α due to increased CH₄ formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction and high dispersion in Fe–Si enhance chain growth probability.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate is assessed by monitoring the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time on Stream}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si show rapid deactivation, with CO conversion decreasing significantly over 64 h, while Fe–Si displays increasing activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Deactivation in Fe@Si and Fe/Si is attributed to oxidation of iron carbides by H₂O, exacerbated by the SiO₂ shell’s hindrance of H₂O removal. Fe–Si’s gradual carburization leads to improved stability and activity over time.
