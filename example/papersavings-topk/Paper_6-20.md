## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

#### CoMnC Synthesis via Co‑precipitation Method

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
   - Dissolve 29.4 g of Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and 17.9 g of 50 wt% Mn(NO₃)₂ in 75 mL deionized water to maintain a Co/Mn mole ratio of 2:1.  
   - Use a beaker for uniform mixing and employ a pH meter to monitor conditions.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
   - Simultaneously add a Na₂CO₃ solution dropwise under vigorous stirring while maintaining the pH at approximately 8.  
   - Observe the formation of a suspension and precipitate, a key indicator of successful deposition.

3. **Aging**  
   - Stir the reaction mixture for 1 hour, then age the suspension for 2 hours at 30°C to ensure complete precipitation.

4. **Drying**  
   - Centrifuge and wash the precipitate with deionized water.  
   - Dry the material at 100°C for 10 hours to remove residual moisture.

5. **Calcination and Post‑treatment**  
   - Calcine the dried precursor at 330°C for 3 hours to form the oxide phase.  
   - Conduct a subsequent pre‑reduction in a pure H₂ atmosphere at 300°C for 5 hours, converting the oxide to the active CoMn carbide (CoMnC).

#### PDVB Synthesis via Thermal Polymerization

1. **Precursor Mixing**  
   - For nonporous PDVB, mix 10 g of divinylbenzene with 0.5 g AIBN (the radical initiator) in the absence of additional solvent.  
   - For nanoporous variants, combine the same amounts of divinylbenzene and AIBN with 50 g of a solvent (DMF or EA) to tailor porosity.

2. **Reactor Setup and Thermal Treatment**  
   - Transfer the mixture to a Teflon‑lined autoclave.  
   - Heat the nonporous system at 100°C for 24 hours, while nanoporous PDVB is treated at 100°C for 12 hours.

3. **Recovery and Final Catalyst Preparation**  
   - After thermal polymerization, recover the solid PDVB.  
   - Physically mix the synthesized CoMnC and PDVB in various weight ratios (e.g., 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5) to optimize catalytic performance without altering the inherent structures.

This detailed synthesis strategy emphasizes precise control of reaction conditions—including reagent concentrations, temperature, pH, and timing—to ensure reproducibility and accurate catalyst preparation. The integrated approach, as confirmed by accompanying QA details, supports the effective conversion of CoMn oxides to CoMnC and the formation of well‐defined PDVB, underpinning the enhanced performance observed during Fischer–Tropsch CO hydrogenation.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: CoMnC

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 29.4 g Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O, 17.9 g of 50 wt% Mn(NO₃)₂, deionized water, and an aqueous solution of Na₂CO₃.
- **Conditions**: Reaction at 30°C with vigorous stirring; pH maintained at approximately 8.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve 29.4 g of cobalt nitrate and 17.9 g of manganese nitrate in deionized water under vigorous stirring at 30°C.
  - Add the Na₂CO₃ solution dropwise while continuously monitoring the pH until it reaches about 8.
  - Stir for 1 hour after complete addition to ensure thorough co-precipitation.
  - Age the resulting suspension at 30°C for an additional 2 hours to complete the precipitation process.
- **Key Steps**: Accurate pH monitoring during Na₂CO₃ addition is critical to maintain the Co/Mn mole ratio of 2:1.

##### Step [2]: Purification and Drying
- **Process**:
  - Centrifuge the suspension and perform multiple wash cycles using ample deionized water to remove residual Na₂CO₃.
  - Dry the washed precipitate in an oven at 100°C for 10 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete removal of impurities through adequate washing to avoid influencing later calcination.

##### Step [3]: Calcination and Activation
- **Conditions**: Calcination at 330°C for 3 hours in air; subsequent H₂ reduction at 300°C for 5 hours with a flow rate of 30 mL/min.
- **Process**:
  - Calcine the dried precursor to obtain the oxide phase.
  - Activate the catalyst by reducing the oxide to cobalt–manganese carbide under controlled H₂ flow.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pre‐reaction treatment that transforms the precursor CoMn oxide into the active CoMn carbide phase required for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This activation is achieved through a controlled pre‐reduction step under specific experimental conditions.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
Activation temperature is paramount for achieving effective reduction. In this procedure, the catalyst is pre‐reduced at 300 °C in pure hydrogen. This temperature is carefully selected to ensure complete oxygen removal from the oxide precursor while maintaining catalyst integrity, thereby promoting the formation of the active carbide phase without inducing sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
The pre‐reduction treatment is performed at atmospheric pressure (approximately 1 bar). Operating at this pressure facilitates uniform gas interaction with the catalyst particles, ensuring an adequate reduction environment that supports the catalyst’s structural stability and efficient conversion during the reaction.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
Pure hydrogen is employed as the activation gas. Hydrogen plays a dual role by both reducing the CoMn oxide to its carbide form and preparing the active surface for syngas conversion. The exclusive use of H₂ ensures that the catalyst undergoes complete reduction without interference from other reactive gases.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time and Flow Rate
The activation is continued for 5 hours, allowing sufficient time for the reduction process to reach completion. A hydrogen flow rate of 20 mL/min is maintained during this period, ensuring a consistent and uniform gas atmosphere throughout the catalyst bed. This controlled flow supports the efficient removal of oxygen species and the formation of active sites critical for high CO conversion and light olefin selectivity during the subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

Overall, these explicitly defined activation conditions—notably the 300 °C temperature, 5-hour duration, atmospheric pressure, and pure H₂ atmosphere—are essential to reliably produce the CoMn carbide active phase. The reproducibility and efficiency of this pre‐reduction treatment significantly contribute to the observed catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature of 250 °C is critical for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis as it drives syngas activation while minimizing unwanted by-product formation. Under this condition, the CoMnC and CoMnC/PDVB catalysts demonstrate effective CO hydrogenation. The temperature was maintained precisely through in-situ reduction (performed at 300 °C prior to cooling) and continuous monitoring within a fixed-bed flow reactor, ensuring reproducibility over extended reaction periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Conducted at a pressure of 0.1 MPa, the reaction conditions favor optimal syngas concentration and permit effective interaction between the gaseous reactants and the catalyst. Operating under this low pressure aids in attaining balanced product selectivity, particularly contributing to enhanced CO conversion rates and the formation of light olefins while reducing by-products.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
An H₂/CO molar ratio of 2 is used consistently during the catalytic evaluation. This ratio is fundamental in directing the hydrocarbon distribution, promoting the formation of lighter olefins and curbing excessive methane production. Maintaining this specific ratio helps in minimizing competitive adsorption, especially of water, thereby sustaining catalyst activity throughout the process.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is set at 1800 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹. This parameter governs the reactant contact time with the catalyst and is crucial for achieving a balance between CO conversion and catalyst stability. A higher GHSV facilitates rapid water desorption from the catalyst surface—a benefit of the hydrophobic PDVB promoter—ensuring a continuous supply of active sites for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. As a result, the CoMnC/PDVB catalyst attains notable CO conversion (63.5%) with robust performance over prolonged operation (up to 120 hours).

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structural Stability
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and structural stability of the catalysts were assessed using X-ray diffraction (XRD), with phase identification referenced to standard PDF files for Co₂C (#65-1457) and CoO (#01-1227).
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirmed the formation of the active cobalt–manganese carbide (CoMnC) phase, with characteristic Co₂C peaks present in both fresh and spent catalysts. After water treatment, bare CoMnC exhibited a reduction in Co₂C peaks and the emergence of CoO peaks, indicating oxidation. In contrast, CoMnC physically mixed with PDVB retained its carbide phase, demonstrating enhanced resistance to water-induced oxidation. No significant changes in phase composition were observed after pre-reduction at 300 °C or after prolonged reaction, indicating excellent structural stability.
- **Observations:** The presence of PDVB preserves the active carbide phase under syngas and water-rich conditions, directly supporting catalyst durability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Dispersion
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to examine catalyst morphology, particle size, and dispersion before and after reaction.
- **Findings:** SEM images revealed that both CoMnC and PDVB were tightly packed and randomly distributed within the catalyst granules, with intergranular distances ranging from nanometers to micrometers. TEM analysis showed negligible differences in particle size and morphology between pure CoMnC and CoMnC separated from the hybrid catalyst after reaction, confirming that physical mixing with PDVB did not alter the nanostructure or cause aggregation.
- **Observations:** The intimate mixing of CoMnC and PDVB ensures efficient contact without compromising catalyst morphology, supporting stable performance.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen sorption measurements were conducted using the BET method to determine surface area and pore volume, providing insight into textural properties relevant to reactant diffusion.
- **Findings:** PDVB-DMF and PDVB-EA exhibited high surface areas (623.3 m²/g and 488.2 m²/g, respectively) and large pore volumes (1.98 cm³/g and 0.74 cm³/g). SiO₂-Me also showed a substantial surface area (278.2 m²/g). Nonporous PDVB had a surface area below 5 m²/g. These values indicate that the nanoporous PDVB variants offer enhanced pathways for mass transport.
- **Observations:** High surface area and porosity facilitate improved diffusion of reactants and products, contributing to the observed catalytic enhancements.

#### 6.1.4 Hydrophobicity and Water Desorption
- **Description:** Hydrophobicity was evaluated via water-droplet contact angle measurements, and in situ FTIR was used to monitor water adsorption and desorption behavior.
- **Findings:** Hydrophobic samples, including CoMnC/PDVB-DMF and CoMnC/PDVB-EA, exhibited high contact angles (>90°), indicating strong hydrophobicity. In situ FTIR spectra showed extremely weak water signals for CoMnC/PDVB, while bare CoMnC displayed increasing water adsorption bands at ~1600 cm⁻¹ and 3400–3600 cm⁻¹. FTIR also demonstrated that, in the presence of water, CO adsorption was suppressed on bare CoMnC but remained strong on CoMnC/PDVB.
- **Observations:** The hydrophobic PDVB promoter accelerates water desorption, minimizes competitive adsorption with CO, and maintains active sites for hydrogenation, directly correlating with improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Thermal Stability of PDVB
- **Description:** Thermal stability of PDVB was assessed using thermogravimetric-mass spectrometry (TG-MS) and thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC).
- **Findings:** PDVB showed no detectable weight loss or decomposition up to 250 °C, the reaction temperature. Decomposition and weight loss began only at ~370 °C. TG-DSC indicated an endothermal event starting at about 300 °C, suggesting possible melting, but no decomposition at reaction conditions. ¹³C NMR spectra and physical appearance of PDVB remained unchanged after prolonged heating.
- **Observations:** PDVB is thermally stable under Fischer–Tropsch conditions, ensuring its effectiveness as a hydrophobic promoter throughout the reaction.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under Fischer–Tropsch conditions (250°C, 0.1 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 1800 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹), the bare CoMnC catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 32.2%. Physical mixing with PDVB significantly enhanced performance: CoMnC/PDVB (1:1 weight ratio) reached 63.5% conversion. Varying the PDVB/CoMnC ratio from 0.5 to 1.5 increased conversion from 56.4% to 70.5%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of hydrophobic PDVB nearly doubled CO conversion, with higher PDVB content further increasing conversion. The optimal balance between conversion and selectivity was observed at a 1:1 ratio.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity for CoMnC/PDVB under standard conditions was 46.0–48.5%. Adjusting the H₂/CO ratio to 3.6 and introducing CO₂ in the feed reduced CO₂ selectivity to 23.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of PDVB did not significantly increase CO₂ selectivity under standard conditions, but process adjustments allowed for its reduction.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For CoMnC/PDVB (1:1), CH₄ selectivity was 4.8%. At PDVB/CoMnC ratios of 0.5 and 1.5, CH₄ selectivity was 9.8% and 8.9%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** Optimal PDVB content (ratio 1) minimized methane formation, while both lower and higher PDVB ratios increased CH₄ selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 Light Olefin Selectivity (C₂–C₄=)
- **Description:** Light olefin selectivity is the percentage of converted CO forming C₂–C₄ olefins, a key target in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Data:** Bare CoMnC yielded 60.8% light olefin selectivity (CO₂-free basis), while CoMnC/PDVB (1:1) achieved 71.4%. Selectivity was 62.8% (0.5 ratio) and 64.5% (1.5 ratio).
- **Observations and Trends:** PDVB addition significantly increased light olefin selectivity, with the highest value at a 1:1 ratio.

#### 6.2.5 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms.
- **Experimental Data:** For CoMnC/PDVB, C₅⁺ selectivity was ~18%, with 92% of these being C₅–C₈ olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** Increasing PDVB content decreased C₅⁺ selectivity, favoring light olefin production.

#### 6.2.6 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor indicates the probability of chain growth during hydrocarbon formation, influencing product distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not provided, the high light olefin and low C₅⁺ selectivities for CoMnC/PDVB suggest a relatively low α, favoring short-chain olefins.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst system is optimized for light olefin production, as reflected in the product distribution.

#### 6.2.7 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended operation. The deactivation rate is:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:** After activation (~15 h), CoMnC/PDVB maintained an average CO conversion of ~64.7%, with only a slight decrease to 62.8% after 120 h. Light olefin selectivity remained stable (~70%).
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibited excellent durability, with negligible deactivation over 120 hours.

#### 6.2.8 Space-Time Productivity of Light Olefins
- **Description:** Space-time productivity quantifies the amount of light olefins produced per gram of catalyst per hour.
- **Experimental Data:** At GHSV = 7200 mL·gCoMnC⁻¹·h⁻¹, CoMnC/PDVB achieved 7.1 mmol·gCoMnC⁻¹·h⁻¹, compared to 2.1 mmol·gCoMnC⁻¹·h⁻¹ for bare CoMnC.
- **Observations and Trends:** The physical mixture with PDVB increased productivity by a factor of 3.4, underscoring the effectiveness of the hydrophobic promoter.
