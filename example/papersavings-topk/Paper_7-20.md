## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is conducted via a continuous coprecipitation process using Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol. An ammonia solution is introduced to adjust the pH to a narrow range of 8.2–8.5 at a controlled temperature of 80 ± 1 °C. This precise pH control and temperature regulation are critical for ensuring homogeneous metal dispersion and reproducibility.

A key part of the synthesis involves systematic cleaning through washing and filtering of the resulting precipitate with distilled water to remove impurities. Although a “Precipitation–Deposition” step is suggested for proper sequencing, in this reported method the procedural focus is on thorough washing immediately after coprecipitation.

### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol in an aqueous medium  
   - Conditions: Mixing at 80 ± 1 °C with simultaneous pH adjustment to 8.2–8.5 using ammonia solution  
   - Equipment: Standard laboratory mixing assembly with real-time pH monitoring

2. • Washing and Filtering  
   - Process: The precipitate is washed repeatedly with distilled water  
   - Purpose: To remove residual ions and ensure purity of the precursor

3. • Drying  
   - Conditions: 120 °C for 12 h  
   - Outcome: Complete removal of moisture to prepare for calcination

4. • Calcination  
   - Conditions: 500 °C for 5 h in static air  
   - Purpose: To induce phase transformation and precursor stabilization

5. • Post‑calcination Pretreatment  
   - Process: Catalyst activation in a fixed-bed reactor  
   - Conditions: Treatment at 300 °C with a gas space velocity of 4000 mL/(g·h)  
   - Atmospheres: Pure CO (FeSi-CO), H₂ (FeSi-H₂), or syngas (2H₂/CO, FeSi-syn) for controlled durations (e.g., 20 h)  
   - Outcome: Formation of distinct iron carbide phases (ε‑Fe₂C, Fe₇C₃, or χ‑Fe₅C₂) that directly influence catalytic performance

This structured approach, with precise temperature, duration, and reagent concentration details, ensures that the synthesis can be accurately replicated while highlighting essential processing steps critical for optimal catalyst performance.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

The following sections detail the reproducible steps for the preparation of the Fe/SiO₂ Fischer–Tropsch catalyst precursors, which are synthesized via a continuous coprecipitation process. The catalysts are categorized by their pretreatment atmospheres: FeSi-CO, FeSi-H₂, and FeSi-syn. Each preparation step is organized into weighing of solid reagents, liquid transfer, and mixing, ensuring that the procedural details for reagent handling are clearly documented.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 FeSi-CO
- **Fe(NO₃)₃**: Weighed 0.15g using an analytical balance, providing the iron source with a target contribution corresponding to approximately 72.6% of the active component.
- **SiO₂ (from silica sol)**: Weighed 0.10g to supply the silica matrix at 27.3% of the total composition.

##### 3.1.1.2 FeSi-H₂
- **Fe(NO₃)₃**: Weighed 0.15g using an analytical balance to maintain consistent iron loading.
- **SiO₂ (from silica sol)**: Weighed 0.10g to align with the precursor composition requirements.

##### 3.1.1.3 FeSi-syn
- **Fe(NO₃)₃**: Weighed 0.15g using an analytical balance as the iron precursor.
- **SiO₂ (from silica sol)**: Weighed 0.10g to achieve the desired binary balance in the catalyst.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 FeSi-CO
- **Deionized water**: Poured 50mL into the reaction vessel using a graduated cylinder to dissolve the weighed reagents.

##### 3.1.2.2 FeSi-H₂
- **Deionized water**: Poured 50mL into the mixing vessel using a graduated cylinder to ensure complete dissolution of the precursors.

##### 3.1.2.3 FeSi-syn
- **Deionized water**: Poured 50mL into the reactor via a graduated cylinder to homogenize the suspension.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 FeSi-CO
- **Catalyst precursor solution**: Mixed by stirring at 800 rpm for 30 minutes at 80 ± 1 °C to enable uniform coprecipitation under controlled pH conditions.

##### 3.1.3.2 FeSi-H₂
- **Catalyst precursor solution**: Mixed by stirring at 800 rpm for 30 minutes at 80 ± 1 °C, ensuring a homogeneous suspension prior to downstream processing.

##### 3.1.3.3 FeSi-syn
- **Catalyst precursor solution**: Mixed by stirring at 800 rpm for 30 minutes at 80 ± 1 °C, establishing consistency in the precursor formation process.

This detailed organization—with catalyst‑specific grouping—ensures clarity in weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing steps, thereby promoting high reproducibility for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: FeSi-CO

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  Appropriate amounts of Fe(NO₃)₃ to achieve 50.9 wt % iron, silica sol, and ammonia solution.
- **Conditions**: Continuous coprecipitation is conducted at 80 ± 1 °C with the pH maintained at 8.2–8.5 by controlled, dropwise addition of ammonia.
- **Process**:  
  - Mix a flowing aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ and silica sol ensuring homogeneity.
  - Gradually introduce ammonia solution while monitoring and maintaining the target pH.
  - Allow the precipitate to form uniformly before proceeding.
- **Key Steps**: Continuous mixing, precise pH adjustment, and stable temperature control are critical for reproducibility.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Process**:  
  - Immediately filter the formed precipitate and wash several times with distilled water to remove residual ions.
  - Employ centrifugation (if applicable) to ensure purity.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the filtered product at 120 °C for 12 hours in a static oven.
- **Calcination**: Calcine at 500 °C for 5 hours in air at a controlled ramp rate to form the oxide precursor.

- **Key Steps**: Ensure stable reactor conditions and monitor reaction time closely.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is designed to transform the calcined Fe/SiO₂ precursor into an active Fischer–Tropsch catalyst through controlled gas-phase treatments. This pretreatment ensures the formation and stabilization of specific iron carbide phases that directly influence CO conversion and hydrocarbon selectivity.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is crucial for initiating the reduction of iron oxides and promoting carburization to form active carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is pretreated at 300 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature is optimal for achieving a balanced reduction in hydrogen (H₂) or carbon monoxide (CO) atmospheres, favoring the formation of χ‑Fe₅C₂ with syngas or mixed carbide phases (Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂) with pure CO. In H₂ pretreatment, ε‑Fe₂C also appears alongside χ‑Fe₅C₂.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of activation determines the extent of reduction and carburization and influences the resulting carbide phase distribution.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A standard pretreatment time of 20 hours is applied at 300 °C. Additionally, shorter activation durations (5 h and 10 h) have been tested in the CO atmosphere to study their impact on phase formation.
- **Observations:** Extended activation times promote a higher degree of carburization, while shorter periods lead to less complete phase transformation and lower active carbide content.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas and Pressure
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation steers the catalyst’s phase evolution by controlling the chemical potential for reduction and carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Three different atmospheres are employed: pure CO (conducted at approximately 1 bar), pure H₂, and a syngas mixture (2H₂/CO). 
- **Observations:** CO pretreatment yields a mixed phase with high reduction, H₂ promotes partial carburization with lower methane selectivity, and syngas treatment selectively forms χ‑Fe₅C₂.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity ensures uniform exposure of the catalyst to the activation gas, critical for consistent phase development.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The gas flow is maintained at 4000 mL/(g_cat·h).
- **Observations:** This high flow rate minimizes concentration gradients, ensuring reproducible and complete activation across the catalyst bed.

Together, these activation parameters are finely controlled within a stainless steel fixed-bed reactor, ensuring a consistent and reproducible catalyst structure tailored for optimal Fischer–Tropsch performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:**  
  The reaction temperature is the critical parameter at which the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis occurs. It directly influences syngas activation, the stability of iron carbide active phases (such as χ‑Fe₅C₂ and Fe₇C₃), and ultimately, the catalyst’s performance.
- **Experimental Conditions:**  
  The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was conducted at an exact temperature of 260 °C.
- **Observations:**  
  At 260 °C, the stability of active iron carbide phases is maintained, thereby ensuring efficient CO conversion without significant catalyst deactivation over prolonged testing.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:**  
  Reaction pressure is defined as the total system pressure applied during the catalysis process. It affects the concentration of syngas in the reactor and the distribution of hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Conditions:**  
  The tests employed a reaction pressure of 3.0 MPa.
- **Observations:**  
  Operating at 3.0 MPa has been observed to enhance product selectivity by favoring the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺) while moderating methane production.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:**  
  This ratio determines the relative amounts of hydrogen and carbon monoxide in the feed gas. It plays a crucial role in adjusting the selectivity towards methane or longer-chain olefins/hydrocarbons during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:**  
  An H₂/CO ratio of 2 was maintained during all experimental runs.
- **Observations:**  
  A ratio of 2 enables a balance between methane formation and olefin production. Increasing the ratio could lead to higher methane selectivity, while lower ratios might promote olefin formation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:**  
  Space-time velocity, represented by the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), is defined as the ratio of the gas flow rate to the catalyst mass. This parameter determines the reactant exposure time and overall turnover frequency.
- **Experimental Conditions:**  
  The gas hourly space velocity was reported as 4 NL/(gcat·h) (equivalent to 4000 mL/(gcat·h)).
- **Observations:**  
  This controlled space-time velocity allows for efficient utilization of the catalyst while balancing reactant conversion rates and limiting potential catalyst deactivation, ensuring reproducibility of the experimental conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases and their evolution were determined using X-ray Diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer Effect Spectroscopy (MES). XRD identifies phase-specific diffraction peaks, while MES quantifies phase composition and provides hyperfine parameters, enabling distinction between closely related iron carbides.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that pretreatment under different atmospheres led to the selective formation of distinct iron carbide phases: χ‑Fe₅C₂ was predominant after CO or syngas treatment at 300 °C, Fe₇C₃ was identified by characteristic peaks at 40.0, 42.9, 44.9, 50.8, and 53.4°, and ε‑Fe₂C was formed under H₂ prereduction followed by syngas carburization. MES further confirmed these assignments, quantifying the relative proportions of each phase and providing hyperfine parameters such as isomer shift (IS) and hyperfine magnetic field (Hhf). For example, in the spent FeSi-CO-H₂ catalyst, MES distinguished Fe₃O₄ (IS 0.45–0.56 mm/s, Hhf 440–490 kOe), χ‑Fe₅C₂ (IS 0.40 mm/s, Hhf 120–232 kOe), and ε‑Fe₂C (IS 0.27–0.39 mm/s, Hhf 131–243 kOe).
- **Observations:** The phase composition was highly dependent on pretreatment conditions. χ‑Fe₅C₂ was stabilized under CO or syngas, Fe₇C₃ was less stable and prone to oxidation, and ε‑Fe₂C was favored by H₂ prereduction. The stability sequence with increasing temperature was ε‑Fe₂C < χ‑Fe₅C₂ < θ‑Fe₃C.

#### 6.1.2 Local Structure and Coordination Environment
- **Description:** X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (XAFS/EXAFS) analysis was employed to probe the local atomic structure and coordination environment of iron sites, distinguishing between Fe–O, Fe–Fe, and Fe–C shells.
- **Findings:** Fe K-edge XANES and EXAFS spectra indicated the presence and proportions of ε‑Fe₂C, χ‑Fe₅C₂, and Fe₇C₃, with fitting results confirming the local coordination environments characteristic of each phase. EXAFS analysis provided coordination numbers and bond distances, supporting the phase assignments from XRD and MES.
- **Observations:** The local structure analysis corroborated the formation of targeted carbide phases and revealed that the SiO₂ support effectively stabilized small iron carbide nanoparticles.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) were used to assess particle morphology, size distribution, and elemental mapping.
- **Findings:** TEM images of the fresh catalyst showed finely dispersed nanoparticles with an average size of 5.5 nm. After reaction, the spent FeSi-CO catalyst exhibited a bimodal distribution: large Fe₇C₃ particles (~27.5 nm) and smaller χ‑Fe₅C₂ particles (~7.1 nm). The spent FeSi-H₂ catalyst displayed larger ε‑Fe₂C particles (13.9–15.4 nm) and smaller χ‑Fe₅C₂ particles (6.7–7.8 nm). The FeSi-syn catalyst maintained well-dispersed particles around 7–7.5 nm. STEM-EDX mapping confirmed the spatial distribution of iron carbides and revealed an amorphous shell, likely iron oxide, around some particles.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ support promoted uniform dispersion and stabilization of small carbide particles. χ‑Fe₅C₂ consistently formed smaller, more uniform particles, while Fe₇C₃ and ε‑Fe₂C appeared as larger particles, resulting in bimodal size distributions in certain catalysts.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) and Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES) were used to quantify elemental composition.
- **Findings:** The catalysts contained iron and silicon as the main elements, with iron content closely matching the designed value of approximately 50.9 wt%.
- **Observations:** Accurate elemental composition confirmed the reliability of the synthesis protocol and provided a basis for correlating structural features with catalytic performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet CO molar flow rates, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (260 °C, 3.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 4000 h⁻¹), the FeSi-CO catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 50.8%. FeSi-H₂ and FeSi-syn catalysts exhibited lower conversions of 33.1% and 22.3%, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO pretreatment leads to the highest CO conversion, correlating with the formation of Fe₇C₃ and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases. H₂ and syngas pretreatments result in lower conversions, reflecting differences in active phase composition.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water–gas shift reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (C mol \%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For FeSi-CO, CO₂ selectivity is 9.7% (C mol %). FeSi-H₂ and FeSi-syn show similar or slightly lower values, consistent with their lower activity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher CO conversion is accompanied by increased CO₂ selectivity, particularly for FeSi-CO, reflecting more extensive secondary reactions.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (C mol \%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-H₂ exhibits the lowest CH₄ selectivity at 10.6%, while FeSi-CO shows the highest among the tested catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of ε‑Fe₂C in FeSi-H₂ is associated with suppressed methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the proportion of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key metric for liquid fuel production. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity (C mol \%)} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-H₂ achieves the highest C₅⁺ selectivity at 74.4%, while FeSi-CO shows the lowest.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower CH₄ selectivity in FeSi-H₂ directly correlates with higher C₅⁺ selectivity, highlighting the impact of iron carbide phase composition on product distribution.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution:
  \[
  W_n = (1-\alpha)\alpha^{n-1}
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the molar fraction of hydrocarbons with n carbon atoms.
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-H₂, with dominant ε‑Fe₂C, exhibits the highest α value, consistent with its high C₅⁺ selectivity and low CH₄ selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalysts favoring ε‑Fe₂C formation promote higher α values, enhancing long-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** FeSi-H₂ maintains stable CO conversion and selectivity during prolonged FTS runs, with negligible deactivation observed.
- **Observations and Trends:** The stability of FeSi-H₂ is attributed to the persistence of ε‑Fe₂C and χ‑Fe₅C₂ phases, underscoring the importance of phase control for long-term catalyst performance.
