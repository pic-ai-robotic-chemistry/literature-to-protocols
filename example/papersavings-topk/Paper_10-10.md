## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section documents the microemulsion synthesis method employed for the preparation of Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, including the base catalyst (100Fe/4Cu) and its promoted variants (100Fe/4Cu/2La; 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba; 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba). The method was chosen for its capability to control nanoparticle size and morphology, which is critical in tuning catalytic performance.

A comparative analysis reveals that the microemulsion approach offers advantages in achieving uniform particle distribution and controlled nucleation, leading to a narrow size distribution. However, its complexity and use of organic solvents may impact scalability and cost when compared to more conventional techniques.

For each synthesis method reported, experimental procedures are implemented in the following sequential order:

#### • Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**
   - Prepare a water solution containing FeCl₃·6H₂O.
   - Mix the aqueous precursor with an oil phase composed of 1-butanol and chloroform in a 1:1 ratio.
   - Add sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as a surfactant.
   - Incorporate hydrazine (25–30%) as the precipitating agent.
   
2. **Precipitation–Deposition**
   - Stir the combined solution vigorously for 4 hours.
   - Allow the mixture to decant overnight; during this period, the precipitation and deposition of the catalyst occur.
   - Recover the deposited solid by centrifugation and wash thoroughly with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.

3. **Drying**
   - Dry the washed solid overnight at 110 °C, ensuring complete removal of residual solvents.

4. **Calcination**
   - Calcine the dried sample in air at 400 °C for 4 hours to develop the active crystalline phase.

5. **Post‑treatments**
   - Optionally, perform reduction in a 10% H₂–90% N₂ stream for 3 hours.
   - Activate in a synthesis gas stream (H₂/CO = 1) for 24 hours at 270 °C under atmospheric pressure.

Each step documents exact temperatures, durations, and reagent conditions, ensuring reproducibility and clarity for researchers aiming to replicate the synthesis of nanosized iron catalysts with precise dopant ratios.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

The catalyst formulations in this study (100Fe/4Cu and its promoted variants) were prepared by a microemulsion method designed to ensure uniform distribution of metal precursors and promoters. The procedure is organized into three distinct stages: weighing of reagents, liquid transfer, and mixing. Each catalyst formulation is handled in its own subgroup to guarantee reproducibility and clarity.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 100Fe/4Cu
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O**: Weighed in a precise amount using an analytical balance to provide the necessary iron precursor.
- **Cu(NO₃)₂·xH₂O**: Weighed to achieve the targeted promoter ratio relative to iron.

##### 3.1.1.2 100Fe/4Cu/2La
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O and Cu(NO₃)₂·xH₂O**: Weighed following the same protocol as for the base catalyst.
- **La(NO₃)₃·xH₂O**: Weighed to incorporate a 2-part promoter addition, as determined by the formulation criteria.

##### 3.1.1.3 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O and Cu(NO₃)₂·xH₂O**: Weighed in quantities equal to the base catalyst setup.
- **La(NO₃)₃·xH₂O and Ba(NO₃)₂**: Weighed to maintain balanced 1:1 promoter loading.

##### 3.1.1.4 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O and Cu(NO₃)₂·xH₂O**: Weighed as per the base protocol.
- **Ba(NO₃)₂**: Weighed to provide a 2-part promoter addition in line with the targeted formulation.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 100Fe/4Cu  
- **Deionized Water**: Transferred using a calibrated pipette to dissolve the precursors.

##### 3.1.2.2 100Fe/4Cu/2La  
- **Deionized Water**: Transferred with precision using a calibrated pipette to ensure complete dissolution of the metal salts and La promoter.

##### 3.1.2.3 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba  
- **Deionized Water**: Poured into the reaction vessel carefully to dissolve both metal salts and dual promoters.

##### 3.1.2.4 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba  
- **Deionized Water**: Accurately measured and transferred to facilitate precursor dissolution.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 100Fe/4Cu, 100Fe/4Cu/2La, and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba  
- **Precursor Suspension**: Mixed under standard stirring conditions to form a homogeneous microemulsion, ensuring even distribution of the nanosized reagents.

##### 3.1.3.2 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba  
- **Metal Precursor and Promoter Mixture**: Prepared by adding the water solution of FeCl₃·6H₂O and associated promoters to an oil phase composed of 1‐butanol, chloroform, and sodium dodecyl sulfate. The mixture was then stirred continuously for 4 hours, yielding a stable and homogeneous microemulsion.

These grouped steps, reflecting the microemulsion synthesis protocol, ensure precise control over reagent handling and reproducibility of the catalyst preparation process, pivotal for successful Fischer–Tropsch synthesis experiments.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe/4Cu (Base Catalyst)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Aqueous solution of FeCl₃·6H₂O and a copper salt (e.g., Cu(NO₃)₂·3H₂O) in stoichiometric ratios with the Fe to Cu mass ratio fixed at 100:4. For reproducibility, the exact quantities should be as reported in detailed protocols.
- **Conditions**: Dissolution in deionized water at ambient temperature, with stirring for 30–60 minutes to ensure complete mixing. The pH is adjusted, if needed, using a dilute NaOH solution to a target value near 9.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the metal salts in water.
  - Stir the solution for uniformity.
  - Adjust pH gradually while monitoring.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution before moving to the assembly step.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**: The precursor solution is combined with an oil phase containing 1-butanol, chloroform, and sodium dodecyl sulfate as a surfactant.
- **Conditions**: The microemulsion is formed at room temperature with vigorous stirring. Hydrazine is then added dropwise as the precipitating agent.
- **Process**:
  - Mix the aqueous and oil phases.
  - Add hydrazine and stir continuously for 4 hours.
  - Allow the system to decant overnight to facilitate complete precipitation.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Recover the precipitated solid via centrifugation.
  - Wash sequentially with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.
  - Centrifuge between washes at standard lab speeds.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the washed solid overnight in an oven at 110 °C.
- **Calcination**: Heat in air at a ramp rate to a final temperature of 400 °C; maintain for 4 hours to achieve the active oxide phase.

##### Step 5: Activation
- **Process**: Load 1.1 g of dried catalyst in a fixed-bed reactor. Pre-treat under a 10% H₂–90% N₂ gas mixture at 270 °C for 3 hours, then activate in synthesis gas (H₂/CO = 1) at a space velocity of 1.5 nl/(h·g) for 24 hours.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Promoted Variants (100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba)

Follow the same sequence (Precursor Preparation → Assembly → Purification → Drying → Calcination → Activation) described above.  
- **Additional Reagents**: Incorporate the desired promoters (La and/or Ba) as their respective oxide or salt precursors in ratios corresponding to 2La, 1La/1Ba, or 2Ba.  
- **Key Notes**: Adjust the promoter loading during the precursor mixing stage to ensure even dispersion; the microemulsion method supports fine control over nanoparticle morphology (10–30 nm) by maintaining strict stirring and decanting conditions.

These detailed steps guarantee reproducibility and control over the catalyst’s physicochemical properties for reliable Fischer–Tropsch performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation step is an essential component of the pre-reaction treatment for the Fischer–Tropsch catalysts. This process is designed to ensure that the active sites are exposed and properly dispersed before the reaction phase begins.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for promoting reduction and ensuring that the catalyst’s metallic phases are appropriately formed. It helps in achieving the desired metal dispersion that is crucial for subsequent reaction performance.
- **Experimental Conditions:** In the synthesis procedure, the catalyst is activated at a controlled temperature of 270 °C using a synthesis gas stream.
- **Observations:** This moderate activation temperature is optimized to provide sufficient energy for reduction without promoting the risk of sintering or over-reduction.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Operating at atmospheric pressure during activation ensures a gentle interaction between the gas mixture and the catalyst surface, which is fundamental for reproducible activation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The process is performed under atmospheric pressure. This choice provides a stable environment that avoids abrupt gas–catalyst interactions.
- **Observations:** The mild conditions under atmospheric pressure help in maintaining uniform reduction across the catalyst.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition during activation directly impacts the reduction of the catalyst’s oxidic precursors and facilitates the formation of active sites.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation step utilizes a synthesis gas stream with an H₂/CO ratio of 1.
- **Observations:** The use of synthesis gas in this step simulates actual reaction conditions, thereby preparing the catalyst for optimal performance in FTS.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity is essential in controlling the contact time between the activation gas and the catalyst, thereby ensuring complete activation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The synthesis gas is introduced at a space velocity of 1.5 nl/(h·g).
- **Observations:** This specific flow rate has been optimized to enable efficient gas–catalyst interactions without leading to excessive gas dilution effects.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The activation time is crucial to ensuring that the catalyst reaches a stable and fully reduced state prior to entering the reaction phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is maintained under the synthesis gas activation conditions for a duration of 24 hours.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Philips PW1840 diffractometer with monochromatized Cu (Kα) radiation. The analysis identifies the bulk crystalline phases present and provides insight into the effects of promoter addition on phase stability.
- **Findings:** All catalysts, including 100Fe/4Cu and its La- and Ba-promoted variants, exhibited a cubic hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) crystal structure, as confirmed by comparison with the JCPDS database. No new crystalline phases were detected upon addition of La and/or Ba promoters.
- **Observations:** The retention of the hematite structure across all samples indicates that promoter incorporation does not alter the bulk phase composition during synthesis and calcination.

#### 6.1.2 Particle Size and Morphology
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, LEO 912AB) was employed to assess particle morphology and size distribution, providing direct visualization of the nanoscale features achieved via the microemulsion synthesis method.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed that all catalyst samples consisted of nanoparticles with sizes in the range of 10–30 nm. No significant differences in particle size or morphology were observed among the base and promoted catalysts.
- **Observations:** The uniform particle size distribution across all samples demonstrates the effectiveness of the microemulsion method in controlling nanoparticle formation, regardless of promoter type or loading.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption measurements (BET method) were conducted using a Micromeritics ASAP 2010 system to determine surface area and pore characteristics. Samples were degassed at 100 °C for 1 hour and 300 °C for 2 hours prior to analysis.
- **Findings:** The addition of La, Ba, or both promoters to the 100Fe/4Cu catalyst resulted in decreased BET surface area and pore volume. This reduction is attributed to aggregation of crystallites and partial pore blockage induced by the promoters. The catalysts did not exhibit high surface areas, in contrast to typical silica-supported materials.
- **Observations:** Lower surface area and pore volume upon promoter addition suggest denser packing and reduced porosity, which may influence reactant diffusion and active site accessibility.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility (TPR Analysis)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was performed using a Micromeritics TPD-TPR 290 system to evaluate the reducibility of iron oxides and the impact of promoters on reduction behavior. Measurements were carried out by heating 50 mg of catalyst in a 5% H₂–95% Ar mixture from 50 to 900 °C at 10 °C/min.
- **Findings:** TPR profiles showed that the addition of La and Ba promoters shifted the reduction peaks to higher temperatures, indicating increased thermal stability and altered reducibility of the iron oxide phases.
- **Observations:** The higher reduction temperatures reflect enhanced catalyst basicity and reduced H₂ adsorption, which are linked to improved Fischer–Tropsch and water–gas shift activities.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Basicity (CO₂-TPD)
- **Description:** CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) was used to assess the surface basicity of the catalysts. CO₂ was chemisorbed at 100 °C, and desorption was monitored from 80 to 430 °C at 5 °C/min under helium flow.
- **Findings:** All catalysts exhibited a small desorption peak at approximately 120 °C and a broad tail above 250 °C, indicating the presence of surface basic sites with varying strengths.
- **Observations:** The distribution of basic sites, as revealed by CO₂-TPD, is influenced by promoter addition and is relevant to the catalysts’ performance in CO activation and selectivity during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS), typically calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the molar flow rates of CO at the reactor inlet and outlet, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard FTS conditions (290 °C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.1, GHSV = 3 nl/(g·h), 72 h), the following CO conversions were observed:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 59.6%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 68.5%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 71.8%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 79.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** Both Ba and La promotion significantly enhance CO conversion compared to the base catalyst, with the dual-promoted 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La exhibiting the highest activity, indicating a strong synergistic effect.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicative of water–gas shift (WGS) activity:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 17.29%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 20.31%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 23.43%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 25.03%
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity increases with promoter addition, especially for the dual-promoted catalyst, reflecting enhanced WGS activity and improved catalyst basicity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.96%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 10.44%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 12.32%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 10.81%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition, particularly Ba, suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, calculated by summing selectivities for C₅–C₁₂, C₁₃–C₁₉, and C₁₉⁺ fractions.
- **Experimental Data:** The dual-promoted catalyst (100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La) achieves the highest C₅⁺ selectivity, with a notable increase compared to the base and single-promoted catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced C₅⁺ selectivity correlates with higher α values and reduced CH₄ selectivity, indicating improved chain growth.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, quantifies the probability of chain propagation:
  \[
  x_i = (1 - \alpha)\alpha^{i-1}
  \]
  where \( x_i \) is the mole fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( i \).
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 0.61
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 0.71
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 0.66
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 0.68
- **Observations and Trends:** Ba promotion yields the highest α, while dual promotion also significantly increases α relative to the base catalyst, supporting the formation of heavier hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over 72 h time-on-stream. Deactivation rate is typically expressed as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** All catalysts demonstrated stable CO conversion with negligible deactivation over 72 h; specific deactivation rates were not numerically reported.
- **Observations and Trends:** The microemulsion-derived catalysts, especially with dual promotion, exhibit robust stability, attributed to optimized adsorption properties and resistance to carbon deposition.

---
**Summary:** The combined use of Ba and La promoters in nanostructured iron catalysts synthesized via microemulsion significantly enhances CO conversion, suppresses methane formation, increases selectivity toward longer-chain hydrocarbons, and improves catalyst stability under FTS conditions.
