## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis of the Fe@C core–shell catalysts involves two primary routes: a pyrolysis method using a MIL‑101(Fe) precursor and a co‑precipitation method for preparing an unsupported‑Fe catalyst. Both pathways adhere to a strict sequence of steps to ensure reproducibility and accurate phase formation.

#### Pyrolysis Synthesis (Fe@C Catalysts)
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation: MIL‑101(Fe) is first synthesized via a hydrothermal method. In this procedure, 166 mg terephthalic acid (1 mmol), 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O (1.5 mmol), 5 ml nitric acid (0.074 mmol), and 70 ml DMF are stirred for 2 h. The homogeneous mixture is then transferred into a Teflon‑lined autoclave and heated at 120 °C for 48 h.
2. • Pyrolysis Process: The obtained MIL‑101(Fe) is pyrolyzed under a controlled flow of 2% C₂H₂/He. Operating at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, the precursor is subjected to target temperatures as follows:
  – Fe@C‑500: 500 °C for 1 h  
  – Fe@C‑600: 600 °C for 1 h  
  – Fe@C‑700: 700 °C for 1 h  
 Observations include continuous weight loss and the evolution of a carbon matrix. The nature of the carbon shell transforms from amorphous (Fe@C‑500) to a concentric graphene structure (Fe@C‑600) and finally to a well‑oriented graphite shell (Fe@C‑700).
3. • Post‑treatment: A “Precipitation–Deposition” step is applied where necessary before the drying and calcination stages. For further improvement, Fe@C‑600 undergoes reduction in H₂ at 400 °C for 24 h (hydrogen flow: 50 ml/min), producing Fe@C‑600‑H₂.

#### Co‑precipitation Synthesis (Unsupported‑Fe Catalyst)
1. • Precursor Mixing: An aqueous solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ is combined with ammonia to adjust the pH to 8.2–8.5 while maintaining a temperature of 80 ± 1 °C.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition: The metal precursor is added dropwise under stirring, followed by an aging period of 2 h.
3. • Drying and Calcination: The precipitate is washed, dried at 100 °C for 6 h, and then calcined at 450 °C for 5 h.

### Comparative Analysis
• The pyrolysis method, though equipment‑intensive (requiring a Teflon‑lined autoclave and tube furnace), offers precise control over carbon shell formation and nanoparticle size.  
• In contrast, the co‑precipitation method is simpler and more scalable, albeit with less control over structural details.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: MIL-101(Fe) Precursor Synthesis  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 mL nitric acid, and 70 mL DMF.  
- **Conditions**: Stir the mixture at room temperature for 2 hours to achieve complete dissolution.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve all reagents in DMF until a homogeneous solution is achieved.  
  - Transfer the solution into a Teflon-lined steel autoclave and heat at 120 °C for 48 hours.  
  - After reaction, wash the precipitate with DMF and ethanol (three cycles), then dry at 80 °C overnight.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and maintain precise reagent ratios for reproducibility.

#### Catalyst Assembly [2]: Pyrolysis of MIL-101(Fe) for Fe@C Variants  
For each Fe@C catalyst, the MIL-101(Fe) precursor is subjected to controlled pyrolysis in a 2% C₂H₂/He atmosphere with a heating ramp of 5 °C/min for 1 hour.

##### Step 1: Pyrolysis  
- **Fe@C-500**  
  - **Conditions**: Heat to 500 °C; amorphous carbon shell is obtained.
- **Fe@C-600**  
  - **Conditions**: Heat to 600 °C; formation of a concentric graphene shell (3–5 layers) is observed.
- **Fe@C-700**  
  - **Conditions**: Heat to 700 °C; results in a well-organized graphite shell indicating enhanced graphitization.
- **Process**:  
  - Load the dried MIL-101(Fe) precursor into the tube furnace.  
  - Introduce the 2% C₂H₂/He gas mixture and implement the respective heating programs for each temperature.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor temperature ramp and gas flow to control carburization and carbon shell structure.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Activation for Fe@C-600-H₂  
- **Reagents**: Fe@C-600 catalyst.  
- **Conditions**: Reduction in an H₂ atmosphere at 400 °C for 24 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - Place Fe@C-600 in a controlled furnace.  
  - Expose to H₂ (with a controlled flow) for reduction, modifying the iron carbide phase while preserving shell integrity.

#### Unsupported-Fe Catalyst (Comparison Catalyst)  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation and Co-precipitation  
- **Reagents**: Fe(NO₃)₃ solution and ammonia solution (3 mol/L).  
- **Conditions**: Stir at 80 ± 1 °C maintaining pH at 8.2–8.5.  
- **Process**:  
  - Add ammonia dropwise to the Fe(NO₃)₃ solution under vigorous stirring.  
  - After complete precipitation, wash thoroughly before drying at 80 °C overnight.  
##### Step 2: Calcination  
- **Conditions**: Heat for 5 hours at 500 °C in static air, followed by 24 hours at 350 °C under H₂.  
- **Key Steps**: Strict control of pH, temperature, and gas atmosphere is essential for reproducibility.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst pre-reaction treatment for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis involves a hydrogen reduction step that transforms the Fe@C-600 precursor into the Fe@C-600-H₂ variant. This activation process is designed to expose active metallic sites by selectively controlling the reaction conditions.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical in driving the reduction of iron species while controlling the potential formation of carbide phases. A carefully chosen temperature favors the retention of the active α‑Fe phase over h‑Fe₃C.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction is performed at 400 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 400 °C minimizes the formation of h‑Fe₃C and facilitates the maintenance of an active metallic state, thus lowering the barrier for CO dissociation in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The choice of activating gas determines the chemical environment and the nature of the reduction process. A H₂-rich environment ensures adequate reduction of iron precursors.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Pure hydrogen is used as the activating gas.
- **Observations:** The H₂ atmosphere effectively converts the oxidized iron species into the desired metallic phase while preserving the integrity of the carbon shell, which serves as a barrier against nanoparticle aggregation.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step plays a crucial role in ensuring complete reduction of any oxidized species and achieving proper phase stabilization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to the hydrogen reduction for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** The extended 24-hour reduction period guarantees full conversion of iron species, which results in well-dispersed active sites and improved catalytic performance, evidenced by enhanced CO conversion rates during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Flow Rate (Space-Time Velocity)
- **Definition:** The flow rate of the activation gas per catalyst unit mass controls the contact between the gas and catalyst particles, affecting the efficiency of the reduction process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A hydrogen flow of 50 ml/min is applied throughout the 24-hour activation.
- **Observations:** This controlled flow rate prevents over-reduction and limits sintering, thereby ensuring a stable catalyst structure with a high number of accessible active sites.

Together, these carefully controlled activation parameters ensure that the Fe@C-600-H₂ catalyst exhibits excellent performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, with improved CO conversion and stability resulting from the optimized pre-reaction treatment.

### 5.2 Reaction

This section summarizes the experimental conditions employed for the Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluation, ensuring reproducibility and clarity for future replication.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is crucial for activating syngas, affecting both the kinetics and phase behavior of the catalyst during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were conducted at a controlled temperature of 300 °C in a fixed-bed reactor.  
- **Observations:** This temperature is within the optimal operating range, ensuring sufficient activation of CO and H₂ while supporting the formation of desired hydrocarbon products. Catalyst activity, particularly in Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂, remained stable, highlighting the resilience of the prepared catalysts.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** The reaction pressure impacts the concentration of reactant gases and influences the selectivity towards different hydrocarbon chains.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Experiments were performed at a maintained pressure of 2.0 MPa.  
- **Observations:** Operating at high pressure aids in the enhanced solubility and conversion of syngas, which is particularly significant for the improved performance observed with Fe@C catalysts compared to unsupported-Fe, where deactivation issues were more pronounced.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio determines the balance between hydrogenation and chain growth reactions, influencing product distribution.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A ratio of 1.0 was utilized, simulating conditions typically obtained from coal- or biomass-derived syngas.  
- **Observations:** This balanced ratio optimizes the production of long-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺) while minimizing excessive methane formation, thereby contributing to the high iron time yields (FTY) reported for the Fe@C variants.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity, expressed as the weight hourly space velocity (WHSV), denotes the volumetric flow rate of the syngas per unit mass of catalyst, influencing contact time and conversion efficiency.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts were evaluated at 8000 mL/(gcat·h), whereas the unsupported-Fe catalyst was tested at 3000 mL/(gcat·h).  
- **Observations:** Differences in WHSV conditions highlight the trade-off between catalyst contact time and reaction throughput; a higher WHSV tends to lower CO conversion slightly but supports improved stability under prolonged reaction conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Composition and Structural Evolution
- **Description:** The crystal phases and structural evolution of the Fe@C catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES), which are critical for identifying active phases and understanding their stability under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that the dominant iron phase in all Fe@C catalysts is cementite (h‑Fe₃C), with characteristic diffraction lines matching JCPDS 35‑0772. The degree of carburization increases with pyrolysis temperature: h‑Fe₃C content rises from 68.2% in Fe@C-500 to 87.2% in Fe@C-600 and 95.5% in Fe@C-700, as quantified by MES. After Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS), h‑Fe₃C remains the predominant phase in Fe@C-600, with minor α‑Fe detected. In Fe@C-600-H₂, hydrogen reduction partially converts h‑Fe₃C to α‑Fe.
- **Observations:** The high stability of h‑Fe₃C under FTS conditions is confirmed, and the phase composition is directly linked to catalytic performance, with higher h‑Fe₃C content favoring long-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.1.2 Carbon Shell Structure and Graphitization Degree
- **Description:** The nature and graphitization degree of the carbon shell were assessed by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and Raman spectroscopy, which influence nanoparticle stability and catalytic durability.
- **Findings:** HRTEM images show that Fe@C-500 possesses an amorphous carbon shell, Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ feature concentric graphene shells (3–5 layers), and Fe@C-700 exhibits a thick, well-oriented graphite shell. Raman spectra confirm these assignments: Fe@C-500 displays a high ID/IG ratio (1.10), indicating low graphitization, while Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ have lower ratios (0.79 and 0.78), characteristic of few-layer graphene. Fe@C-700 shows merged 2D peaks, typical of bulk graphite.
- **Observations:** Increasing pyrolysis temperature enhances graphitization, with the graphene shell in Fe@C-600 providing optimal protection against nanoparticle aggregation during FTS.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to evaluate particle morphology and size distribution, which are crucial for catalyst dispersion and activity.
- **Findings:** TEM analysis reveals uniform core–shell structures across all Fe@C catalysts. Fe@C-600 exhibits the smallest average particle size (5.9 nm), compared to 8.7 nm for Fe@C-500 and 40 nm for Fe@C-700. The unsupported-Fe catalyst shows much larger particles (63 nm). After FTS, Fe@C-600 maintains its particle size and dispersion, while Fe@C-500 and Fe@C-700 show significant aggregation.
- **Observations:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 effectively inhibits nanoparticle growth and aggregation, ensuring high catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET) measurements provided surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which affect reactant accessibility and metal dispersion.
- **Findings:** Fe@C-600 exhibits a high specific surface area (280 m²/g), a pore volume of 0.27 cm³/g, and an average pore size of 3.8 nm. These values are higher than those of Fe@C-500 and Fe@C-700, reflecting the preservation of MOF-derived porosity.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and mesoporosity of Fe@C-600 facilitate reactant diffusion and contribute to its superior catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Distribution and Encapsulation
- **Description:** Elemental mapping via TEM/EDS confirmed the spatial distribution of iron and carbon, supporting the core–shell architecture.
- **Findings:** Iron nanoparticles are uniformly encapsulated within the carbon matrix, as evidenced by overlapping Fe and C signals. Acid leaching experiments further reveal cavities corresponding to the original particle sizes, confirming complete encapsulation.
- **Observations:** The robust encapsulation of h‑Fe₃C within the carbon shell is essential for maintaining phase integrity and preventing sintering during reaction.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS), calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 hours on stream, CO conversion values were: Fe@C-500: 64.1%, Fe@C-600: 65.0%, Fe@C-700: 73.2%, Fe@C-600-H₂: 78.7%, and unsupported-Fe: 75.3%. At 184 hours, Fe@C-600 maintained 74.5%, while Fe@C-500 and Fe@C-700 dropped to 55.7% and 58.5%, respectively; Fe@C-600-H₂ increased to 81.5%, and unsupported-Fe declined to 39.2%.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO conversion generally increased with pyrolysis temperature, peaking for Fe@C-700 at 24 h, but Fe@C-600 demonstrated superior long-term stability. Fe@C-600-H₂ exhibited the highest conversion, while unsupported-Fe deactivated rapidly.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h, CO₂ selectivity was: Fe@C-500: 35.2%, Fe@C-600: 33.3%, Fe@C-700: 33.9%, Fe@C-600-H₂: 37.3%, unsupported-Fe: 38.5%. At 184 h, unsupported-Fe reached 43.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity correlated with CO conversion and was slightly higher for Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe, reflecting increased water-gas shift activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h: Fe@C-500: 11.5%, Fe@C-600: 10.5%, Fe@C-700: 12.4%, Fe@C-600-H₂: 23.4%, unsupported-Fe: 28.7%. At 184 h, Fe@C-600 remained low (11.0%), while unsupported-Fe increased to 40.0%.
- **Observations and Trends:** MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts consistently suppressed methane formation compared to Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity represents the proportion of long-chain hydrocarbons (C₅ and above) among total hydrocarbon products:
  \[
  \text{C}_5^+ \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{\text{mass of C}_5^+}{\text{total hydrocarbon mass}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h: Fe@C-500: 59.0%, Fe@C-600: 60.4%, Fe@C-700: 57.0%, Fe@C-600-H₂: 38.6%, unsupported-Fe: 14.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** High C₅⁺ selectivity was characteristic of h-Fe₃C–dominated Fe@C catalysts, while Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe favored shorter-chain products.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain growth versus termination, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution:
  - Calculated from the slope of ln(Wₙ/n) vs. n, where Wₙ is the mass fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number n.
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h: Fe@C-500: 0.68, Fe@C-600: 0.69, Fe@C-700: 0.67, Fe@C-600-H₂: 0.49, unsupported-Fe: 0.30.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values for Fe@C-x catalysts corresponded to greater long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity and lower methane formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate is the decline in CO conversion over time, indicating catalyst stability:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Deactivation rates: Fe@C-500: 13.1%, Fe@C-700: 20.1%, unsupported-Fe: 47.9%. Fe@C-600 and Fe@C-600-H₂ showed minimal deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 effectively inhibited nanoparticle aggregation, resulting in exceptional long-term stability.

#### 6.2.7 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY measures catalytic activity as μmol CO converted per gram Fe per second.
- **Experimental Data:** At 24 h: Fe@C-500: 161.1, Fe@C-600: 176.5, Fe@C-700: 163.6, Fe@C-600-H₂: 203.0, unsupported-Fe: 39.5 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** MOF-derived Fe@C catalysts exhibited significantly higher FTY than unsupported-Fe, with Fe@C-600-H₂ achieving the highest activity.

---
**Summary:**  
The Fe@C core–shell catalysts, especially Fe@C-600, demonstrated a unique combination of high CO conversion, superior long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity, low methane formation, and outstanding stability under FTS conditions. The presence of h-Fe₃C and a graphene shell were critical for optimal performance, distinguishing these catalysts from conventional unsupported iron systems.
