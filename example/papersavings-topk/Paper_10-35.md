## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were synthesized using a microemulsion approach that ensures uniform dispersion of metal precursors and promoters. This method involves the following structured steps:

#### 1. Precursor Mixing
• A water solution of FeCl₃·6H₂O is prepared and added to an oil phase containing 1‐butanol and chloroform in a 1:1 ratio.  
• Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) is incorporated as a surfactant to stabilize the microemulsion, ensuring even distribution of the metal precursors and promoters (e.g., Cu, La, Ba).  
• The mixture is vigorously stirred using a standard stirring apparatus to achieve homogeneity.

#### 2. Precipitation–Deposition
• Hydrazine (25%–30%) is added as a precipitating agent shortly after mixing, triggering the formation of a solid precipitate, observable by a distinct color change and particle aggregation.  
• The suspension is stirred for 4 hours to allow complete precipitation–deposition before being left to decant overnight.  
• Centrifugation is then employed to recover the solid, which is subsequently washed with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone to remove residual impurities.

#### 3. Drying and Calcination
• The recovered solid is dried overnight at 110 °C in a conventional drying oven to ensure complete removal of solvents.  
• Dry samples are calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 hours in a calcination furnace. This step converts the precursor phases into the desired iron oxide (hematite/Fe₃O₄) forms, which serve as catalysts precursors.

#### Comparative Analysis
• The microemulsion method offers excellent control over particle size and uniform promoter dispersion, markedly enhancing the catalyst’s reproducibility.  
• However, its complexity—requiring precise reagent ratios, extended stirring, overnight decantation, and multiple washing steps—may limit operational scalability compared to simpler techniques such as co‑precipitation or impregnation.

This detailed methodology, with clearly numbered steps and specific reaction conditions, ensures that another researcher can replicate the synthesis accurately. Subsequent reduction (in a 10% H₂–90% N₂ environment for 3 hours) and synthesis gas activation (H₂/CO = 1 at 270 °C for 24 hours) further prepare the catalyst for its fixed-bed reactor evaluation.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe/4Cu

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 100Fe (from FeCl₃·6H₂O), 4Cu (introduced as Cu salt), SDS (surfactant), hydrazine (25–30% as precipitating agent); solvents include deionized water, 1-butanol, and chloroform mixed in a 1:1 ratio.
- **Conditions**: Stirring at room temperature for 4 hours; decant overnight; pH adjusted dropwise if needed.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O in deionized water.
  - Mix the aqueous solution with the oil phase (1-butanol/chloroform) and add SDS.
  - Stir vigorously for 4 hours to ensure homogeneous distribution.
  - Add hydrazine slowly to initiate precipitation at a controlled pH.
  - Let the mixture decant overnight.
- **Key Steps**: Uniform microemulsion formation; careful pH control via dropwise addition.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Purification
- **Reagents**: Promoter for Cu already incorporated in the precursor solution.
- **Conditions**: Centrifugation (speeds as per standard lab protocols) and sequential washing with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.
- **Process**:
  - Recover the solid via centrifugation.
  - Wash repeatedly to remove residual impurities.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry the washed precipitate in an oven at 110 °C for 15 hours.
- **Calcination**: Heat in air at 400 °C for 4 hours to form hematite/Fe₃O₄ precursor phases.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Process**: Reduce in a 10% H₂–90% N₂ environment for 3 hours; follow with synthesis gas treatment (H₂/CO = 1.1) at 270 °C under atmospheric conditions and later at 18 bar in the fixed-bed reactor.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: 100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba

For the promoted catalysts, repeat Steps 1–4 with the following modifications:

- **Precursor Preparation**: In addition to the base Fe and Cu precursors, add La and/or Ba precursors in specific molar ratios (2La for 100Fe/4Cu/2La; 1La and 1Ba for 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba; 2Ba for 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba). Ensure complete dissolution and uniform distribution in the microemulsion.
- **Catalyst Assembly**: The promoters are integrated prior to the purification stage. This step is critical for ensuring enhanced basicity and improved reduction profiles.
- **Key Steps & Caution**: Monitor stirring durations, dropwise reagent additions for pH control, and ensure thorough washing for reproducibility.

These detailed, sequential steps guarantee that starting from the raw precursor solutions, one can reliably replicate both the catalyst’s precursor synthesis and the subsequent processing stages, ultimately achieving the desired nanostructured iron phases for optimized Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is performed in a two-step procedure designed to gradually reduce the precursor phases and condition the catalyst surface under controlled, synthesis-relevant conditions.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for ensuring efficient reduction and surface restructuring of the catalyst precursor stages.
- **Experimental Conditions:** During the synthesis gas treatment phase, the catalyst is activated at 270 °C.
- **Observations:** This moderate temperature supports the transformation of iron oxide precursors into active metallic phases while preventing excessive sintering.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Maintaining appropriate pressure during activation governs the gas-solid interactions and influences reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Both the initial reduction and synthesis gas treatment steps are performed under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** This gentle pressure condition facilitates controlled reduction and preserves the nanostructured characteristics essential for catalytic performance.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The composition of the activation gas determines the redox environment and directly impacts the reduction efficiency of the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation involves an initial reduction using a gas mixture of 10% H₂ and 90% N₂, followed by a treatment with synthesis gas having an H₂/CO ratio of 1.
- **Observations:** The initial mild hydrogen atmosphere initiates precursor reduction effectively, while the synthesis gas treatment further tailors the catalyst surface by promoting CO dissociative adsorption.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** This parameter quantifies the flow rate of the activation gas per unit catalyst weight, ensuring consistent gas-solid contact.
- **Experimental Conditions:** During the synthesis gas treatment, the activation is carried out at a space-time velocity of 1.5 nl/(h·g).
- **Observations:** The controlled gas flow minimizes the risk of incomplete activation and helps maintain the structural stability of the catalyst.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** Duration of activation ensures complete catalyst conversion to its active state without inducing detrimental sintering effects.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst undergoes a 3-hour reduction in the 10% H₂–90% N₂ stream, followed by a 24-hour synthesis gas treatment at 270 °C.
- **Observations:** This two-step process, totaling 27 hours, is designed to optimize reduction while preserving the favorable nanostructure and promoting enhanced catalytic performance in subsequent Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, as it not only determines the activation of synthesis gas but also influences the catalyst's performance and stability. In the present study, the reaction was maintained at 290 °C throughout the catalytic tests. This temperature was chosen to ensure sufficient activation of the iron-based catalyst while balancing the risk of sintering or deactivation over prolonged operation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure plays a vital role in adjusting the syngas concentration and directing product selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch reactions. The tests were conducted at a pressure of 1.8 MPa. Operating under this pressure facilitates higher syngas conversion rates and promotes chain growth, thereby favoring the formation of heavier hydrocarbons. Such conditions also contribute to improved heat management within the fixed-bed system.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio directly impacts the hydrocarbon distribution and overall selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. In these experiments, an H₂/CO ratio of 1.1 was employed. This specific ratio was found to favor the formation of heavier hydrocarbons over methane, aligning with the overall strategy of achieving enhanced CO conversion while minimizing light product formation. The chosen ratio supports balanced chain growth reactions, crucial for the observed catalytic performance.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity and Duration  
Space-time velocity (STV) dictates the residence time of reactants in contact with the catalyst. Here, a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 3 nl/(g·h) was utilized, ensuring a controlled exposure time conducive to effective catalytic conversion. The reaction was continuously operated for 72 hours, a duration that allowed thorough assessment of both catalyst performance and long-term stability under the stated conditions.

These meticulously defined reaction parameters—290 °C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO ratio of 1.1, and a GHSV of 3 nl/(g·h) over 72 hours—highlight the reproducibility and precision of the experimental setup, ensuring reliable comparisons across different catalyst formulations.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying active phases and assessing the impact of promoters on crystallinity.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns for all catalyst samples exhibited a characteristic peak at 2θ = 33.3°, corresponding to the (104) plane of hematite (Fe₂O₃), confirming the cubic hematite crystal structure in accordance with the JCPDS database. No new crystalline phases were detected upon the addition of La and Ba promoters, indicating that the promoters did not alter the primary iron oxide phase.
- **Observations:** The retention of the hematite structure across all formulations suggests that promoter incorporation does not disrupt the bulk crystal phase, which is critical for maintaining catalytic activity.

#### 6.1.2 Particle Size and Morphology
- **Description:** Particle size and morphology were assessed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and XRD (via the Scherrer equation), providing insights into nanoscale features that influence catalytic performance.
- **Findings:** TEM analysis revealed nanoparticle sizes in the range of 10–30 nm for all catalysts, with no significant morphological differences observed among the variants. The Scherrer equation, applied to the XRD peak at 2θ = 33.3°, yielded consistent particle size estimates within the same range.
- **Observations:** The microemulsion synthesis method enabled uniform distribution of metal precursors, resulting in homogeneous nanoparticle formation. The consistent nanoscale morphology across samples supports reliable comparison of catalytic properties.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were measured by N₂ adsorption (BET analysis), which are key parameters affecting reactant accessibility and catalyst dispersion.
- **Findings:** The addition of La, Ba, or both promoters led to a decrease in BET surface area and pore volume compared to the unpromoted catalyst. This reduction is attributed to aggregation of crystallites and partial pore blockage by the promoters. The catalysts did not exhibit high surface areas relative to silica-based materials.
- **Observations:** Lower surface area and pore volume in promoted catalysts may influence diffusion and accessibility but are offset by enhanced basicity and active site properties.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility (H₂-TPR Analysis)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) profiles were used to evaluate the reducibility of metal oxides, which is crucial for activating the catalyst under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** Three main reduction peaks were observed: the first (282–300°C) for CuO to Cu, the second (323–412°C) for Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄, and the third (522–680°C) for Fe₃O₄ to Fe. The presence of La and Ba shifted these peaks to higher temperatures, indicating increased reduction temperatures and altered reducibility.
- **Observations:** Copper facilitated the reduction of iron oxides, while La and Ba promoters increased the reduction temperature, reflecting stronger metal-support interactions and modified activation behavior.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Basicity (CO₂-TPD Analysis)
- **Description:** CO₂-temperature programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) was employed to assess surface basicity, which affects CO adsorption and product selectivity in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.
- **Findings:** Promoted catalysts exhibited higher CO₂ desorption values, indicating increased surface basicity. The double-promoted 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba catalyst showed a CO₂ desorption amount of 747 mmol/gcat, surpassing single-promoted (575 mmol/gcat for 100Fe/4Cu/2La) and unpromoted (123 mmol/gcat) samples. Ba addition increased the desorption temperature and broadened the high-temperature peak, signifying stronger basic sites.
- **Observations:** Enhanced basicity, especially in the double-promoted catalyst, correlates with improved CO adsorption and catalytic performance, demonstrating a synergistic effect between La and Ba.

#### 6.1.6 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Elemental analysis (atomic absorption) confirmed the incorporation and loading of promoters (Cu, La, Ba) in the catalyst formulations.
- **Findings:** The intended loadings of Cu, La, and Ba were verified for all catalyst variants (100Fe/4Cu, 100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba), confirming successful synthesis and promoter dispersion.
- **Observations:** Accurate promoter incorporation is essential for correlating structural properties with catalytic performance.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 290 °C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.1, and GHSV = 3 nl/(g·h), the CO conversion values after 72 h on stream were:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 59.6%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 68.5%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 71.8%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 79.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Ba and La promoters, especially in combination, significantly enhanced CO conversion. The 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba catalyst exhibited the highest conversion, demonstrating a synergistic effect between La and Ba.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting water–gas shift (WGS) activity:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity (mol%) for each catalyst:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 17.29%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 20.31%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 23.43%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 25.03%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition increased CO₂ selectivity, with the highest value for the dual-promoted catalyst, indicating enhanced WGS activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity (mol%):
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.96%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 10.44%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 12.32%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 10.81%
- **Observations and Trends:** All promoted catalysts exhibited reduced methane selectivity compared to the unpromoted sample, with Ba and La suppressing CH₄ formation and favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity and Hydrocarbon Distribution
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity reflects the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, indicative of chain growth. Product distribution is analyzed via the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model.
- **Experimental Data:** Selectivity for C₅–C₁₂ hydrocarbons increased with promoter addition, with the 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba catalyst showing the highest fraction of heavier hydrocarbons.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced basicity (as measured by CO₂-TPD) in promoted catalysts correlates with increased C₅⁺ selectivity, supporting improved chain growth and suppressed methane formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the ASF equation, quantifies the probability of chain growth:
  \[
  x_i = (1 - \alpha)\alpha^{i-1}
  \]
  where \( x_i \) is the mole fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( i \).
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 0.61
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 0.71
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 0.66
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 0.68
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition increased α, with Ba having the strongest effect. The dual-promoted catalyst also exhibited a high α, confirming the synergistic enhancement of chain growth.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over 72 h. The deactivation rate is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
