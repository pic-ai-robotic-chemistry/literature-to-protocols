## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The synthesis methods for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts are detailed with an emphasis on reproducibility, reaction precision, and clear step ordering. The following procedures summarize the extraction from the article:

#### Fe@Si Catalyst (Two‑step Strategy: Solvothermal Synthesis + Stöber Method)
1. • Precursor Mixing: Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) in an appropriate solvent. A yellow suspension confirms Fe₂O₃ nanoparticle formation preventing aggregation. Equipment: Stainless autoclave.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition: Immediately following precursor mixing, initiate the Stöber process by dropwise addition of 25 mL tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) into a deionized water and ethanol solution containing CTAB as a template. Maintain continuous stirring for 6 hours.
3. • Drying: The resulting solid is dried at 120 °C for 24 hours.
4. • Calcination: Calcine the dried product at 550 °C for 6 hours in air to form a SiO₂ shell encapsulating the Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles.

#### Fe/Si Catalyst (Impregnation Method)
1. • Precursor Mixing: Impregnate m-SiO₂ with an ethanol solution of Fe(NO₃)₃ (2 M) with CTAB added as a structure directing agent to promote uniform mesopore formation.
2. • Equipment & Conditions: Conduct the impregnation in a Teflon‑lined autoclave at 180 °C for 18 hours.
3. • Post‑processing: Follow with similar drying (120 °C, 24 h) and calcination (550 °C, 6 h) steps to ensure consistency.

#### Fe‑Si Catalyst (Co‑precipitation Method)
1. • Precursor Mixing: Combine Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and silica sol (30 wt.%) in distilled water.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition: Adjust the solution to a constant pH of 9.0 ± 0.1 using NH₃⋅H₂O at 85 °C, ensuring uniform distribution of iron species.
3. • Drying & Calcination: Recover the precipitate, dry at 120 °C, and calcine at 550 °C for 6 hours.

### Comparative Analysis
• Advantages and disadvantages are discussed in terms of synthesis complexity, scalability, cost, and equipment:  
 – The Fe@Si strategy offers enhanced stability due to the protective SiO₂ shell but is more complex.  
 – The impregnation method (Fe/Si) presents simpler processing albeit with potential agglomeration.  
 – Co‑precipitation (Fe‑Si) ensures a robust Fe‑SiO₂ interaction promoting uniform active site distribution. 

This structured approach ensures that each step—from precursor handling through post‑synthetic thermal treatments—is precisely detailed, enabling accurate replication by other researchers.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Si Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 20.6 g Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and 7.21 g polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, M.W. = 58,000) dissolved in 800 mL of anhydrous ethanol.
- **Conditions**: Stir the solution at room temperature for 5 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve the Fe(NO₃)₃∙9H₂O and PVP in ethanol to obtain a homogeneous solution.
  - Transfer the mixture into a stainless steel autoclave.
  - Heat the autoclave at a controlled rate of 1 °C/min up to 180 °C and maintain that temperature for 18 hours under continuous stirring.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and controlled heating to foster uniform Fe₂O₃ nanoparticle formation.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly (SiO₂ Shell Formation)
- **Reagents**: Post-solvothermal precursor, add 25 mL of tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) and 6 g of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB). For the Stöber process, prepare a solution with 144 mL NH₃·H₂O (25%), 1656 mL deionized water, and 1276 mL ethanol.
- **Conditions**: Ambient temperature with pH maintained at 9.0 ± 0.1.
- **Process**:
  - Add TEOS dropwise to the Fe₂O₃ suspension while stirring steadily.
  - Introduce CTAB as a structure-directing agent to prevent particle aggregation.
  - Stir the mixture initially for 6 hours and then continue for an additional 48 hours to complete the SiO₂ shell formation via controlled TEOS hydrolysis.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain the slow addition of TEOS and verify pH carefully to ensure reproducible shell formation.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Purification Process**: Filter and wash the catalyst thoroughly with distilled water.
- **Drying**: Dry the product in an air oven at 120 °C for 24 hours.
- **Calcination**: Heat the dried sample to a final temperature of 550 °C for 6 hours under an air flow.
- **Key Steps**: Observe drying conditions precisely and prevent sintering during calcination.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Process**: Activate the catalyst by heating at 350 °C for 24 hours in a syngas environment (H₂/CO = 2) at atmospheric pressure.
- **Key Considerations**: Strict control of temperature and gas composition is critical to convert the precursor to its active phase and ensure reliable catalytic performance.

This detailed sequential workflow, integrating precise reagent quantities, controlled conditions, and step-by-step operational guidance, facilitates reproducibility and highlights the importance of each stage—from precursor preparation to final activation—in the synthesis of the Fe@Si catalyst.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)  
- **Operational Description**: Utilized to identify crystalline phases, especially assessing well‐crystallized hematite (α‑Fe₂O₃) in the Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts.  
- **Instruments and Models**: D8 Advance Diffractometer and FE‑XRD setups.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Co Kα radiation (λ = 1.79026 Å) at 35 kV and 40 mA.  
- **Test Parameters**: Scanning range from 30° to 90° with a step size of 0.04°.  
- **Key Notes**: Proper sample alignment is critical to ensure accurate diffraction profiles.

### 4.2 BET Surface Area Analysis  
- **Operational Description**: Performed to determine specific surface area and pore characteristics, distinguishing mesoporosity in Fe@Si and Fe/Si (Type IV isotherm with H1 hysteresis) from larger, irregular pores in Fe‑Si (H3 isotherm).  
- **Instruments and Models**: ASAP 2420.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: N₂ physisorption conducted at −196 °C.  
- **Test Parameters**: Catalyst samples are degassed under vacuum at 90 °C for 120 minutes and at 350 °C for 480 minutes to eliminate adsorbed species.  
- **Key Notes**: Rigorous pretreatment enhances reproducibility and accurate textural analysis.

### 4.3 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)  
- **Operational Description**: Assesses reduction behavior and the influence of the SiO₂ shell on H₂O diffusion and reduction kinetics.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Autochem II 2920 equipped with a Thermal Conductivity Detector (TCD).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: 10% H₂/90% Ar mixture at a flow rate of 50 mL/min; temperature ramped from 50 to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.  
- **Test Parameters**: Uniform catalyst loading is essential for reliable profiles.  
- **Key Notes**: Highlights differences in reduction profiles between catalysts.

### 4.4 TEM & EDX Mapping  
- **Operational Description**: Confirms nanoparticle isolation within the SiO₂ matrix and evaluates elemental distribution.  
- **Instruments and Models**: FEI Talos F200X.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Catalyst samples are dispersed in ethanol (20 minutes) and deposited onto a carbon membrane.  
- **Test Parameters**: Adequate dispersion and settling time ensure uniform mapping.  
- **Key Notes**: Provides visual confirmation of catalyst morphology.

### 4.5 FT‑IR  
- **Operational Description**: Used to assess surface interactions, particularly Fe–SiO₂ bonding.  
- **Instruments and Models**: VERTEX 70.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Spectra recorded in the 4000–400 cm⁻¹ range at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ after mixing the sample with KBr (1:50 ratio) and pressing into disks.  
- **Test Parameters**: Room temperature measurements with standardized preparation ensure reproducibility.  
- **Key Notes**: Confirms functional group interactions on the catalyst surface.

### 4.6 Mössbauer Spectroscopy  
- **Operational Description**: Analyzes iron species by evaluating isomer shifts, quadrupole splitting, and magnetic hyperfine fields to deduce oxidation states and carbide formation.  
- **Instruments and Models**: MR‑351 Mössbauer spectrometer.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements carried out at 50 K using a constant-acceleration triangular reference signal.  
- **Test Parameters**: Data fitted using nonlinear least-squares methods ensure detailed analysis.  
- **Key Notes**: Provides nuanced insights into the iron environment essential for correlating structure with catalytic performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst pre-reaction activation is a crucial step designed to convert the precursor iron oxide species into their active forms, thereby ensuring high catalytic performance during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. The following subsections detail the reported activation parameters:

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is pivotal for promoting the reduction of Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles and initiating partial carburization, which is essential for creating active iron carbide sites.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalysts are subjected to an activation temperature of 350 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 350 °C provides sufficient thermal energy to overcome the reduction barrier of iron oxides embedded within the SiO₂ matrix, ensuring the transformation of inactive species into catalytically active phases.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The pressure during activation influences the kinetics of the reduction process and gas–solid interactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is conducted under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Using atmospheric conditions creates a realistic pre-reaction environment that aligns with the subsequent Fischer-Tropsch operating conditions, thus helping to maintain catalyst integrity and prevent unfavorable sintering effects.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
- **Definition:** The gas mixture used during activation provides the necessary reducing environment required for effective transformation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with a hydrogen to carbon monoxide ratio of 2 (H₂/CO = 2) is used.
- **Observations:** The hydrogen present in the syngas facilitates the removal of oxygen from Fe₂O₃, while CO assists in partial carburization. This combination is critical for preparing the catalyst’s active sites, particularly in the Fe@Si system where the SiO₂ shell can otherwise hinder the reduction process.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation process ensures that the reduction and partial carburization are completed uniformly.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation process is maintained for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** The extended 24-hour treatment guarantees that the iron oxide species are effectively reduced across all catalyst types, establishing a homogeneous active phase. This prolonged exposure in syngas also mitigates the potential negative impacts of the SiO₂ shell on reducing kinetics, ensuring reproducible catalytic performance in subsequent Fischer-Tropsch tests.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is a critical factor that influences the kinetics of CO hydrogenation and the overall efficiency of the Fischer-Tropsch process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The fixed-bed reactor was maintained at a temperature of 280 °C during catalytic testing.
- **Observations:** At 280 °C, the catalysts—particularly the Fe@Si and Fe/Si variants—exhibited high initial activity, although a gradual deactivation was observed likely due to changes in the iron species’ oxidation state over prolonged reaction periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure affects the concentration of syngas and plays a significant role in determining product selectivity by modifying reaction rates.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The experiments were conducted at a reaction pressure of 2.0 MPa within the fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** Operating at 2.0 MPa provided a favorable environment for enhancing CO conversion rates and promoting the formation of targeted hydrocarbon products, as higher pressures are known to benefit reaction kinetics in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is vital for balancing syngas composition; it directly influences the pathway for CO hydrogenation and subsequent hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A stoichiometric H₂/CO ratio of 2 was employed in all tests.
- **Observations:** This specific ratio optimizes the hydrogenation process, minimizing excessive methane formation while favoring the buildup of longer-chain hydrocarbons. Adjustments to the ratio have been noted in literature to affect product distribution significantly.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) is a measure of the volumetric flow of syngas per unit mass of catalyst, directly impacting reactant contact time and conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was set to 5000 mL∙h⁻¹∙g_cat⁻¹ during the reactions.
- **Observations:** A GHSV of 5000 mL∙h⁻¹∙g_cat⁻¹ provided a controlled environment that balanced sufficient syngas exposure with catalyst stability. Additionally, catalyst activation was carried out at 350 °C for 24 hours in syngas, ensuring that the iron species were primed to achieve optimal performance in the subsequent Fischer-Tropsch reaction.

This structured overview of reaction parameters ensures clarity and reproducibility, allowing future researchers to accurately replicate the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis process under identical conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition of the catalysts was determined by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a D8 ADVANCE diffractometer with Co Kα radiation (λ = 1.79026 Å), operated at 35 kV and 40 mA, scanning from 30° to 90° (step size 0.04°). XRD is essential for identifying crystalline phases and assessing phase stability during synthesis and reaction.
- **Findings:** The Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts exhibited only well-crystallized hematite (α‑Fe₂O₃) as the detectable phase, with diffraction peaks matching the standard JCPDF for hematite. No other crystalline iron phases were observed before reaction. After Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS), the Fe@Si catalyst retained its embedded structure, with no new peaks indicative of sintering or significant phase transformation.
- **Observations:** The stability of the α‑Fe₂O₃ phase and absence of sintering highlight the effectiveness of the SiO₂ shell in preserving the catalyst structure under reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were evaluated by N₂ physisorption at −196 °C (ASAP 2420), with surface areas calculated using the BET method. These parameters are critical for assessing reactant accessibility and catalyst efficiency.
- **Findings:** The Fe@Si catalyst exhibited a high BET surface area of 652 m²/g, a pore volume of 0.64 cm³/g, and an average pore size of 2.62 nm. The isotherm was type IV with an H1 hysteresis loop, indicating uniform cylindrical mesopores. Degassing was performed at 90 °C for 120 min and 350 °C for 480 min prior to measurement.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and uniform mesoporosity, attributed to the use of CTAB and the Stöber process, facilitate improved reactant diffusion and accessibility to active sites, which are beneficial for Fischer-Tropsch performance.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphological analysis was conducted using TEM (FEI Talos F200X at 200 kV) and EDX mapping. These techniques provide direct visualization of particle dispersion, size, and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed that Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles (average size 50.4 nm) were individually embedded and physically separated within the SiO₂ matrix. EDX mapping confirmed a uniform distribution of iron within the SiO₂ shell. After FTS, the Fe@Si catalyst maintained its morphology and particle size (~45.1 nm), indicating minimal sintering.
- **Observations:** The SiO₂ shell effectively prevents aggregation and sintering, ensuring the stability of isolated nanoparticles throughout reaction cycles.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction Behavior
- **Description:** Hydrogen temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) was performed (Autochem II 2920) to assess the reducibility of iron oxides and the influence of the SiO₂ shell.
- **Findings:** The Fe@Si catalyst displayed multiple reduction peaks: peak I at 328 °C (Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄), peak II at 416 °C (partial reduction to FeO), and higher-temperature peaks at 531 °C and 674 °C. The SiO₂ shell significantly inhibited reduction, as evidenced by lower H₂ consumption and shifted peaks.
- **Observations:** The confining effect of the SiO₂ shell increases H₂O diffusion resistance, limiting the reduction of iron oxides and contributing to catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.5 Chemical Interactions and Iron Species
- **Description:** FT-IR and Mössbauer spectroscopy were used to probe Fe–SiO₂ interactions and the chemical environment of iron species.
- **Findings:** FT-IR spectra showed a broad Fe–O band at 538 cm⁻¹, Si–O–Si stretching at 1120 cm⁻¹, and a Fe–O–Si band at 957–977 cm⁻¹, confirming Fe–SiO₂ interactions. Mössbauer spectra at 50 K revealed singlets, doublets, and sextets corresponding to various iron environments, enabling distinction of iron species and their local environments.
- **Observations:** The presence and strength of Fe–O–Si bonds serve as descriptors for metal-support interaction, influencing reducibility and catalytic performance.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS). It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (2.0 MPa, 280 °C, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹), the CO conversion at 48 h on stream (TOS) was:
  - Fe@Si: 47.9%
  - Fe/Si: 55.7%
  - Fe‑Si: Not explicitly reported at 48 h, but noted to increase over time.
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si catalysts exhibit high initial CO conversion, which gradually decreases with time due to deactivation. In contrast, the Fe‑Si catalyst demonstrates increasing CO conversion over time, attributed to progressive iron carbide formation facilitated by strong Fe–SiO₂ interactions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water-gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At TOS = 48 h:
  - Fe@Si: 24.4%
  - Fe/Si: 19.5%
- **Observations and Trends:** The Fe@Si catalyst shows higher CO₂ selectivity, likely due to the SiO₂ shell impeding H₂O removal and promoting iron carbide oxidation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At TOS = 48 h:
  - Fe@Si: 24.4%
  - Fe/Si: 19.5%
  - Fe‑Si: 17.9%
- **Observations and Trends:** Methane selectivity is highest for Fe@Si, indicating a tendency toward lighter hydrocarbon formation, possibly due to the confinement effect of the SiO₂ shell.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
  \[
  \text{C}_3^+ \text{ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}} + F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_2,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4,\text{out}} + 2F_{\text{C}_2\text{H}_6,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** At TOS = 48 h:
  - Fe@Si: 30.2%
  - Fe/Si: 31.0%
  - Fe‑Si: 25.1%
- **Observations and Trends:** Fe/Si achieves the highest C₃⁺ selectivity, with Fe@Si closely following. The Fe‑Si catalyst produces fewer long-chain hydrocarbons under identical conditions.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain growth versus termination, influencing hydrocarbon distribution. It is derived from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory (ASF) model, with values closer to 1 favoring longer chains.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - Fe@Si: 0.74
  - Fe/Si: 0.75
  - Fe‑Si: 0.71
- **Observations and Trends:** The slightly lower α for Fe@Si is attributed to the SiO₂ shell’s confinement effect, which promotes secondary reactions and lighter product formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Deactivation rate quantifies the decline in CO conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@Si and Fe/Si show gradual deactivation (decreasing CO conversion with TOS), while Fe‑Si exhibits increasing activity due to ongoing iron carbide formation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The SiO₂ shell in Fe@Si effectively prevents sintering but increases H₂O diffusion resistance, accelerating iron carbide oxidation and deactivation. In contrast, the Fe‑Si catalyst’s strong Fe–SiO₂ interaction stabilizes iron species, resulting in improved stability and even enhanced activity over time.
