## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis is initiated by preparing a Raney iron precursor from an iron–aluminum alloy, where aluminum is selectively dissolved in a KOH solution. This process follows a well‐defined sequence to ensure reproducibility and phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ formation.

• Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques  
 – The process involves precursor mixing, controlled precipitation–deposition, sequential washing, inert drying, passivation, and in situ carburization.  
 – Comparative analysis shows that while co‑precipitation offers straightforward operation and scalability, the additional impregnation step for manganese or potassium promotion adds complexity but can improve catalytic performance.

### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

#### 1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
1. Mix iron–aluminum alloy powder with an 8 M KOH solution to dissolve aluminum.  
 • Reagents: Iron–aluminum alloy, 8 M KOH, deionized water, ethanol.  
 • Observations: Formation of a precipitate indicating aluminum dissolution.

#### 2. Precipitation–Deposition  
2. Following the co‑precipitation, introduce a precipitation–deposition step by allowing the support precursor (in cases of promoted catalysts) to be added dropwise.  
 • Equipment: Magnetic stirrer with pH meter monitoring (if applicable).  
 • Process: The deposition occurs after the initial mixing and before subsequent washing.

#### 3. Washing and Drying  
3. Rinse the resulting precursor thoroughly (10× with deionized water and 7× with ethanol) to remove residual ions.  
4. Dry the washed precursor in a glove box under an argon flow at room temperature for 6 hours.  
 • For potassium-promoted catalysts, wet impregnation with KNO₃ is performed after washing and before drying, followed by drying in a vacuum oven for 12 hours.

#### 4. Post‑processing: Passivation and Carburization  
5. Passivate the dried precursor by exposing it to a flow of 1% O₂ in He at room temperature for 20 hours.  
6. Carburize in situ using a stainless‑steel tubular fixed‑bed reactor:  
 • Gas mixture: H₂/CO/He with ratios approximately 100/3.2/21.8.  
 • Conditions: Temperature ramp at 0.5°C min⁻¹ up to 350°C, followed by a 6‑hour dwell period to ensure complete transformation into phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂.  
 • For Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂, manganese (via Mn(NO₃)₂ wet impregnation) is introduced immediately after washing and before passivation.

This structured synthesis approach, detailing all reagents, reaction conditions, and equipment, guarantees reproducibility and has been optimized for high catalytic performance in Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

The reagent preparation for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts is organized into three distinct steps—solid weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing—with each procedure tailored to the catalyst variant to ensure reproducibility.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 χ‑Fe₅C₂ Catalyst  
- **Fe–Al Alloy Powder (50:50)**: Weighed using an analytical balance with amounts adjusted to achieve the required stoichiometry for the Raney iron precursor.  
- **KNO₃**: Weighed as a promoter for potassium doping, with the mass calculated to provide a 1 wt% loading relative to the total precursor.

##### 3.1.1.2 Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ Catalyst  
- **Fe–Al Alloy Powder (50:50)**: Weighed under the same conditions as for the χ‑Fe₅C₂ synthesis to maintain consistency.  
- **Mn(NO₃)₂**: Weighed for wet impregnation, with the target manganese loading ranging from 0.5 to 12.5 wt% as dictated by the catalyst design.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 χ‑Fe₅C₂ Catalyst  
- **Deionized Water**: Transferred for sequential washing of the precursor; poured using a graduated cylinder to ensure measured delivery.  
- **Ethanol**: Transferred for final washing steps; poured into the reaction vessel using standard laboratory glassware.

##### 3.1.2.2 Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ Catalyst  
- **Deionized Water**: Transferred to both rinse and condition the impregnated precursor; poured steadily using calibrated glassware.  
- **Ethanol**: Transferred during washing procedures following Mn(NO₃)₂ addition; poured with care to avoid dilution discrepancies.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 χ‑Fe₅C₂ Catalyst  
- **Fe–Al/KNO₃ Suspension**: Mixed with an 8 moll⁻¹ KOH solution under ambient conditions; stirring was continued until the aluminum was fully dissolved, preparing the precursor for passivation.  
- **Precursor Post-Wash**: Dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 hours under an argon atmosphere to ensure the absence of moisture and prevent oxidation.

##### 3.1.3.2 Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ Catalyst  
- **Fe–Al/Mn(NO₃)₂ Suspension**: Prepared by wet impregnation and mixed under identical stirring conditions to promote homogeneous manganese dispersion.  
- **Post-Impregnation Mixture**: Subjected to drying in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 hours under argon to lock in the catalyst structure prior to subsequent processing steps.

This structured approach—detailing specific reagent weighing, controlled liquid transfers, and careful mixing under defined environmental conditions—ensures clarity and full reproducibility in synthesizing both catalyst variants.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: χ‑Fe₅C₂

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Raney Iron Formation  
- **Reagents**:  
  - Iron–aluminum alloy powder (50:50 by weight)  
  - 8 molar KOH solution (quantity adjusted to fully immerse the alloy)  
- **Conditions**:  
  - Temperature: 70°C  
  - Stirring under controlled heating  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve aluminum from the alloy by adding the powder into pre-heated KOH solution under continuous stirring.  
  - Maintain temperature at 70°C until complete partial dissolution, forming a slurry.  
  - Wash the mixture ten times with deionized water and seven times with ethanol to thoroughly remove K⁺ and AlO₂⁻ ions.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Ensure complete aluminum dissolution and extensive washing to generate a porous Raney iron precursor.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Post-Processing  
- **Process**:  
  - Transfer the washed precursor into a glove box and dry in an argon flow at room temperature for 6 hours.  
  - Passivate the dried sample in a controlled flow of 1% O₂ in helium at room temperature for 20 hours to stabilize the surface.  
  - Perform in situ carburization: introduce a syngas mixture (H₂:CO:He = 100:3.2:21.8) in a stainless-steel fixed-bed reactor.  
  - Ramp the temperature at 0.5°C/min to 350°C, then dwell at 350°C for 6 hours to convert the precursor to pure χ‑Fe₅C₂.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Careful control of temperature ramp and dwell time is essential to prevent unwanted phase formations.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Raney Iron with Mn Impregnation  
- **Reagents**:  
  - Prepared Raney iron precursor (as above)  
  - Manganese nitrate (Mn(NO₃)₂) solution (amount based on desired promoter weight, e.g., 0.5–12.5 wt%)  
- **Conditions**:  
  - Ambient conditions for impregnation  
- **Process**:  
  - Directly after washing and drying of the Raney iron, introduce Mn(NO₃)₂ by wet impregnation.  
  - Ensure uniform distribution by stirring the mixture, then dry in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 hours.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Uniform impregnation is critical to achieve consistent promoter dispersion.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Post-Processing  
- **Process**:  
  - Follow the same passivation (1% O₂ in He at room temperature for 20 hours) and in situ carburization procedures as for the unpromoted catalyst.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  - Monitor the carburization to ensure full transformation to Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ while maintaining catalyst structural integrity.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 In‑situ X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
- **Operational Description**: In‑situ XRD was employed to monitor the formation of phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂ during the carburization process. The catalyst sample was first passivated in a 1% O₂ in helium atmosphere at room temperature for 20 hours and then carburized by exposure to an H₂/CO/He syngas mixture at 350°C for 6 hours.
- **Instruments and Models**: Measurements were conducted using a Rigaku D/max‑2600/PC instrument equipped with a copper rotating anode and loaded into an Anton Par XRK‑900 cell.
- **Characterization Conditions**: The instrument operated at 40 kV and 40 mA under a nitrogen flow, and the temperature was gradually ramped up to 350°C.
- **Test Parameters**: XRD data were collected over a defined 2θ range; detailed scan rates and step sizes were provided during data processing with Fullprof software using Rietveld refinement.
- **Key Notes**: The refinement yielded lattice parameters and goodness‑of‑fit values, ensuring phase purity and accurate structural determination.

### 4.2 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
- **Operational Description**: TEM, including high‑resolution TEM (HRTEM), was used to visualize the catalyst morphology and the characteristic lattice spacing (~2.7 Å) indicative of χ‑Fe₅C₂. 
- **Instruments and Models**: An aberration‑corrected FEI Titan ETEM G2 was utilized for imaging under environmental conditions.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were prepared by exposing passivated Raney iron to a syngas mixture (H₂/CO ratio of 30) at 1,200 Pa and an initial temperature of 320°C before raising to 350°C. The sample grid was prepared in a glove box and dried in an argon flow for 6 hours.
- **Test Parameters**: Imaging protocols included the collection of HRTEM images and corresponding IFFT analyses.
- **Key Notes**: These procedures ensured real‑time monitoring of structural transformations under controlled gaseous atmospheres.

### 4.3 Mössbauer Spectroscopy
- **Operational Description**: Mössbauer spectroscopy was applied to confirm the absence of competing iron oxides and to validate the exclusive formation of χ‑Fe₅C₂.
- **Instruments and Models**: Experiments were performed using an in‑situ high‑pressure cell with a 57Co(Rh) source and a sinusoidal velocity spectrometer.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Spectra were collected at −153°C, ensuring consistent temperature for both source and sample.
- **Test Parameters**: The method involved careful calibration and extended exposure times sufficient for detailed spectral analysis, yielding definitive signatures of the desired phase.
- **Key Notes**: This technique provided critical confirmation of phase purity under conditions replicable with precise temperature and calibration control.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis is carried out as a two‐step process involving an initial passivation followed by in situ carburization. This procedure transforms the Raney iron precursor into the active phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂, which is essential for high CO conversion and favorable product selectivity.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- Definition: Activation temperature is critical for both the reduction and carburization steps, ensuring uniform transformation into phase‑pure χ‑Fe₅C₂.
- Experimental Conditions: After passivation, the precursor is heated using a controlled temperature ramp of approximately 0.5–1 °C/min until reaching 350 °C.
- Observations: Maintaining this temperature ensures complete carburization, with in situ X‑ray diffraction confirming the phase purity of the catalyst.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- Definition: The pressure during activation influences the kinetics of the reduction and carburization reactions.
- Experimental Conditions: The carburization dwell period is performed at ambient pressure (or slightly elevated pressures prior to reaction conditions) during the 350 °C phase.
- Observations: Such moderate pressures promote a balanced reduction and prevent undesired phase formation without inducing rapid sintering.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- Definition: The gas mixture plays a dual role by providing both the reducing atmosphere and the carbon necessary for carburization.
- Experimental Conditions: A syngas mixture with a defined composition of H₂/CO/He in the ratio 100/3.2/21.8 is employed.
- Observations: This controlled environment ensures sufficient hydrogen for reduction while leveraging CO for carbide formation, thus preventing the generation of unwanted iron oxide phases.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- Definition: Space-time velocity dictates the exposure rate of the catalyst to the reactive gas, thereby influencing the uniformity of activation.
- Experimental Conditions: The activation process utilizes a gas flow corresponding to a space-time velocity of 75,000 mL/(g_cat·h).
- Observations: This controlled flow rate ensures consistent contact between the gas mixture and the catalyst, aiding in homogeneous carburization.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- Definition: The activation time encompasses the dwell period at the target temperature to achieve complete phase transformation.
- Experimental Conditions: The catalyst dwell time at 350 °C is maintained for 6 hours.
- Observations: This prolonged dwell period guarantees full conversion of the passivated precursor into the active and stable χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase, as evidenced by enhanced CO conversion and reduced CO₂ selectivity during subsequent reaction tests.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature governs the kinetic energy of the reactants, influencing both the activation of syngas and the selectivity of hydrocarbon products.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch catalytic evaluations were performed within a temperature range of 250–320 °C, with optimum activity observed at approximately 290 °C. In long-term tests, reaction temperatures around 320–325 °C were also maintained to assess catalyst durability.  
- **Observations:** An exponential increase in CO conversion is noted with rising temperature, which is crucial for achieving high carbon efficiency. Furthermore, sustained catalyst performance over 225 hours under these conditions underlines both high activity and resistance to deactivation.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure directly affects the concentration of syngas in the reactor, thereby influencing product distribution and conversion rates.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The process was conducted under pressures ranging from 2.3 to 3.0 MPa.  
- **Observations:** Operating at these elevated pressures results in enhanced CO conversion rates and lowered CO₂ selectivity, with the manganese-promoted catalyst displaying a CO conversion of up to 70.7% at optimal conditions.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is a critical variable that affects the chain-growth probability and ultimately the yield of desired linear α‑olefins.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** While no specific numerical value is provided, this parameter is emphasized as crucial for steering the reaction toward higher α‑olefin production and minimizing methane formation.  
- **Observations:** Tuning the H₂/CO ratio enables a balance between suppressing over-hydrogenation and promoting the formation of long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity (STV) reflects the ratio of syngas flow rate to catalyst mass, thereby determining the contact time between the gas and the catalyst.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Reported space velocities vary from 18,000 to 100,000 mL/(g_cat·h).  
- **Observations:** Higher STVs tend to reduce CO conversion slightly but contribute to improved catalyst stability during prolonged reactions, ensuring reproducibility of the Fischer–Tropsch process.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and structural parameters of the catalysts were determined using in situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) with Rietveld refinement (Fullprof software). This approach is critical for confirming the formation and stability of the active χ‑Fe₅C₂ phase, which is essential for Fischer–Tropsch performance.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns collected in an Anton Par XRK-900 cell under nitrogen flow after synthesis revealed phase-pure χ‑Fe₅C₂, with no detectable contributions from iron oxides or other phases. Rietveld refinement yielded a goodness of fit χ² = 7.58%, Rp = 18.7%, Rwp = 15.3%, and Rexp = 5.6%. The refined lattice parameters and space group matched published values for χ‑Fe₅C₂, and atomic positions were consistent with literature reports.
- **Observations:** The transformation from passivated Raney iron to χ‑Fe₅C₂ was complete after a 6-hour dwell at 350°C under syngas, with the presence of manganese not altering the transformation pathway. The phase remained stable under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Phase Evolution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), including high-resolution (HRTEM) and environmental TEM (ETEM), was employed to visualize catalyst morphology and monitor the dynamic transformation from precursor to active phase. These techniques provide direct evidence of phase evolution and structural integrity.
- **Findings:** HRTEM and filtered inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) images captured the progression from amorphous oxide-coated Raney iron to crystalline χ‑Fe₅C₂ grains. The final state, imaged along the (311) direction, exhibited a characteristic lattice spacing of ~2.7 Å, a fingerprint of χ‑Fe₅C₂. The transformation initiated at the inner layers and was completed within 30 minutes at 350°C and 1,200 Pa syngas. TEM of used catalysts confirmed the persistence of well-maintained χ‑Fe₅C₂ structure, with no evidence of carbon deposition or manganese migration.
- **Observations:** The use of manganese as a promoter did not affect the morphology or phase stability, and the catalysts retained their structural features after extended reaction.

#### 6.1.3 Phase Purity and Iron State
- **Description:** In situ Mössbauer spectroscopy was utilized to determine the iron phase composition and oxidation state, providing a sensitive probe for verifying the exclusive presence of χ‑Fe₅C₂ under both synthesis and reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** Mössbauer spectra collected at −153°C in a high-pressure cell confirmed that Hägg carbide (χ‑Fe₅C₂) was the only iron phase present in both unpromoted and manganese-promoted catalysts after carburization and Fischer–Tropsch operation. No signals corresponding to iron oxides or other iron carbides were detected.
- **Observations:** The phase purity and stability of χ‑Fe₅C₂ were maintained throughout long-term catalytic testing, supporting the catalyst’s robust performance.

#### 6.1.4 Elemental Distribution and Dopant Effects
- **Description:** The distribution and impact of manganese and potassium promoters were assessed through the combined use of XRD, TEM, and catalytic performance analysis, as direct elemental quantification was not reported.
- **Findings:** Wet impregnation of manganese (0.5–12.5 wt%) and potassium (1 wt%) did not alter the χ‑Fe₅C₂ crystal structure or morphology, as confirmed by XRD and TEM. Manganese was shown to suppress carbon deposition and enhance catalyst stability, while potassium promotion was associated with improved catalytic performance.
- **Observations:** The structural integrity of χ‑Fe₅C₂ was preserved in the presence of promoters, and no migration of manganese was observed during reaction.

*No explicit data on BET surface area, pore volume, or average pore size were reported in the article.*



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 250°C: 27.8%  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 250°C, 2.3 MPa: 16.0%  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 250°C, 3.0 MPa: 46.1%  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 290°C, 2.5 MPa: 70.7% and 52.8% (varied space velocity)  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 320°C, 2.5 MPa: 91.2%
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Both catalysts exhibit high activity at relatively low temperatures, with Mn promotion significantly enhancing CO conversion, especially at elevated pressures and temperatures. The CO conversion time yields (CTY) are 3–7 times higher than state-of-the-art catalysts at comparable or higher temperatures.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity measures the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, reflecting the extent of unwanted oxidation:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 250°C: 11.2%  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 250°C, 2.3 MPa: 9.3%  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 250°C, 3.0 MPa: 9.4%  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 290°C, 2.5 MPa: 21.9%  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 320°C, 2.5 MPa: 32.0%
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Manganese promotion consistently lowers CO₂ selectivity at low temperatures, indicating improved carbon efficiency. Even at high CO conversion, CO₂ selectivity remains below that of reference catalysts.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  Specific numerical values are not provided; however, the Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalyst produces significantly less CH₄ than predicted by the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Both χ‑Fe₅C₂ and Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ suppress methane formation, especially at lower temperatures and with promoter addition, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₃⁺ selectivity represents the percentage of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms:
\[
\text{C₃⁺ Selectivity} = 100 - \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}} + F_{\text{CH₄,out}} + 2F_{\text{C₂H₂,out}} + 2F_{\text{C₂H₄,out}} + 2F_{\text{C₂H₆,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  The Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ catalyst achieves up to 51% selectivity toward C₂–C₁₀ linear α‑olefins (LAOs) at 290°C, 2.5 MPa.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  High C₃⁺ selectivity is maintained across a range of conditions, with product distributions favoring valuable LAOs and minimal light hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination, influencing hydrocarbon length distribution. Values range from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (maximum chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂: α = 0.82–0.86 (e.g., 0.86 at 290°C, 2.5 MPa)  
  - Reference catalysts: α = 0.77 (Mn‑Na‑Co₂C) to 0.91 (FeMn@Si)
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  High α values for χ‑Fe₅C₂ systems correlate with enhanced selectivity for long-chain olefins and reduced methane formation, closely matching ASF predictions for optimal LAO production.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate reflects the decline in CO conversion over time:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
\]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  Both χ‑Fe₅C₂ and Mn‑χ‑Fe₅C₂ maintain stable CO conversion and selectivity over 200–225 hours at 320–325°C and 2.3–3.0 MPa, indicating negligible deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The catalysts exhibit exceptional long-term stability, with structural integrity and performance reproducible over extended operation, supporting their suitability for industrial syngas conversion.
