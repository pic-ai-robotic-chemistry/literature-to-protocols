## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalysts were synthesized using a microemulsion method, integrating a systematic approach to ensure reproducibility and uniform incorporation of promoters. This section details the step‐by‐step experimental procedures as reported in the article.

#### Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation
1. A water solution of FeCl₃·6H₂O is prepared and mixed with an oil phase composed of 1-butanol and chloroform (1:1 ratio).  
   • Reagents: FeCl₃·6H₂O, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as a surfactant.  
   • Conditions: Hydrazine (25%–30%) is added as a precipitating agent.  
   • Equipment: Stirring apparatus ensures uniform mixing over 4 hours.  
   • Observation: Formation of a precipitate indicative of successful co-precipitation.

#### Precipitation–Deposition
2. After initial mixing, copper salts are introduced as promoters to facilitate hematite reduction. Lanthanum and barium dopants are also incorporated at specific ratios (100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba), ensuring optimal catalytic performance.  
   • Step Position: This step is performed immediately after precursor mixing and before drying.  
   • Procedure: The support precursor is added dropwise under continuous stirring, maintaining homogeneity.

#### Drying
3. The precipitate is left to decant overnight before being recovered via centrifugation.  
   • Washing: The solid is washed sequentially with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.  
   • Drying Conditions: The sample is dried at 110 °C overnight, which is critical for solvent removal and precursor stabilization.

#### Calcination
4. The dried material is calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 hours.  
   • Purpose: This step facilitates the formation of a cubic hematite phase as confirmed by XRD analysis.  
   • Observations: Post-calcination, the catalyst exhibits a well-defined crystalline structure.

#### Additional Post‑treatment
5. A reduction step is performed using a 10% H₂–90% N₂ gas mixture at 270 °C for 3 hours, preparing the catalyst for subsequent activation and reaction testing.

This carefully structured protocol, emphasizing the sequential order of precursor mixing, precipitation–deposition, drying, calcination, and post‑treatment, ensures high reproducibility and precise control over the catalyst’s physical and chemical properties.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe/4Cu  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • FeCl₃·6H₂O (iron precursor in a water solution)  
  • SDS as a surfactant  
  • Cu salt (4 parts relative to Fe)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Prepare a water phase by dissolving FeCl₃·6H₂O under continuous stirring at ambient temperature for 4 hours.  
  • Simultaneously, prepare an oil phase (1-butanol and chloroform in 1:1 ratio).  
- **Process**:  
  • Combine the water phase with the oil phase to form a stable microemulsion.  
  • Add hydrazine solution dropwise to initiate controlled precipitation.  
  • Stir the mixture for 4 hours ensuring complete mixing before allowing it to decant overnight.  
  • Recover the solid via centrifugation and wash thoroughly with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Ensure complete dissolution and uniform mixing of metal salts to promote effective Cu-induced hematite reduction.

##### Step 2: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  • Dry the recovered solid in an oven at 110 °C overnight.  
  • Calcine the dried material in air at 400 °C for 4 hours to obtain the desired cubic hematite phase.

##### Step 3: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • Activate the catalyst by reduction in a fixed-bed stainless steel reactor using a 10% H₂–90% N₂ gas mixture at 270 °C.  
- **Key Considerations**:  
  • Maintain precise gas flow rates and temperature control to ensure reproducibility.

## 4. Characterization Methods

- **Instruments and Models**: Philips PW1840 X‑ray diffractometer with monochromatized Cu (Kα) radiation.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Prior to measurement, a 0.5 g catalyst sample is degassed in a Micromeritics ASAP 2010 system (100 °C for 1 hour, then 300 °C for 2 hours).  
- **Test Parameters**: Although the scan range, step size, and scanning rate are not explicitly detailed, the Scherrer equation analysis requires the X‑ray wavelength, FWHM (β) of the target peak, and diffraction angle (θ).  
- **Key Notes**: Correct sample alignment and complete degassing are essential for accurate phase identification.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: This technique evaluates the reduction behavior of the catalysts by monitoring H₂ consumption.  
- **Instruments and Models**: A micro fixed‑bed reactor coupled with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Pre‑treatment involves degassing the catalyst (0.5 g, 100 °C for 1 hour, then 300 °C for 2 hours). The TPR is performed on a 50 mg sample in a 5% H₂/95% Ar gas mixture.  
- **Test Parameters**: Temperature ramp from 50 to 900 °C at 10 °C/min.  
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst loading and precise temperature control are critical to obtaining reproducible reduction profiles.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)
- **Operational Description**: CO‑TPR assesses the reduction stages, including transitions from CuO to Cu and Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄ then Fe, providing insights into catalyst redox behavior.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Similar reactor setup as for H₂‑TPR.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Operated under a 5% H₂/95% Ar atmosphere, ramping from 50 to 900 °C at 10 °C/min using a 50 mg sample.  
- **Test Parameters**: Consistent ramp rate and gas flow ensure detection of multiple reduction stages.  
- **Key Notes**: Observing distinct peaks allows for clear identification of redox transitions in the catalyst.

### 4.4 BET Surface Area Analysis
- **Operational Description**: BET analysis quantifies surface area and pore volume, which are affected by promoter-induced crystallite aggregation.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2010 automated system.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: A 0.5 g sample is degassed at 100 °C for 1 hour and 300 °C for 2 hours prior to analysis.  
- **Test Parameters**: Standard nitrogen physisorption parameters are employed.  
- **Key Notes**: Lower surface areas in promoted catalysts indicate aggregation effects.

### 4.5 Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

- **Test Parameters**: Direct imaging coupled with size distribution analysis.

### 4.6 CO₂ Temperature‑Programmed Desorption (CO₂‑TPD)
- **Operational Description**: CO₂‑TPD evaluates the surface basicity by quantifying CO₂ desorption.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical preparatory step in ensuring that the Fischer-Tropsch catalysts reach their full potential by exposing the active sites necessary for high-performance conversion. After an initial reduction under a 10% H₂–90% N₂ flow, the catalyst undergoes a syngas activation step in a fixed-bed stainless steel reactor.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** Activation temperature is vital for completing the reduction of residual oxides and for generating the active metallic phases without inducing sintering of the iron nanoparticles.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated at 270 °C.
- **Observations:** This temperature is chosen to promote efficient reduction while maintaining the nanostructured morphology, thereby ensuring the stability of active sites.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** The pressure during activation governs the interaction between the catalyst and the activation gas, impacting the kinetics of the reduction process.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed under atmospheric pressure.
- **Observations:** Operating at atmospheric pressure provides a controlled environment that minimizes the risk of particle agglomeration and maintains uniform reduction.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
- **Definition:** The composition of the activating gas determines the reducing environment needed to convert any remaining iron oxide species into the active metallic phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Syngas with an H₂/CO ratio of 1 is used.
- **Observations:** This balanced gas mixture simulates the Fischer-Tropsch reaction atmosphere, ensuring that neither overly reducing nor oxidizing conditions dominate, thereby facilitating the optimal formation of active sites.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity reflects the flow rate of the activation gas per unit mass of the catalyst, ensuring sufficient contact during activation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The syngas is introduced at a space-time velocity of 1.5 nl/(h·g).
- **Observations:** This parameter ensures adequate dispersion of the gas through the catalyst bed, contributing to uniform activation.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is crucial for achieving complete phase conversion without extending the process to a point where sintering might occur.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is maintained for 24 hours.
- **Observations:** The extended activation period under these controlled conditions helps in achieving a robust and reproducible catalytic state for subsequent Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter that affects the activation of syngas by promoting efficient CO dissociative adsorption and subsequent chain growth in the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. In the reported experimental protocol, the reactor is maintained at 290 °C during the catalytic evaluation. This temperature ensures that the molecular activity is sufficiently high to drive the formation of hydrocarbons, with the elevated condition favoring the initiation of the chain growth reactions. Although specific observations on catalyst deactivation at this temperature are not provided, the chosen condition reflects an optimization aimed at balancing conversion efficiency with catalyst integrity over the reaction period.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
The total system pressure directly influences the concentration of syngas, which is essential for achieving desirable reaction rates and product yields. In this study, the reaction is conducted at a pressure of 1.8 MPa using a fixed-bed stainless steel reactor. This elevated pressure assists in maintaining an adequate reactant density on the catalyst surface, thereby supporting higher conversion levels and enhanced product formation. The pressure setting is aligned with strategies to optimize the interaction between reactant molecules and the active catalyst sites during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO ratio is pivotal in steering the product distribution by influencing the hydrogenation efficiency and chain growth probabilities. Here, a molar ratio of 1.1 is utilized, a condition that tends to favor the formation of heavier hydrocarbons by limiting excessive hydrogenation that can lead to increased methane yield. This parameter is carefully adjusted to maintain a balance between hydrocarbon chain termination and growth, tailoring the product slate toward higher-value fractions.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
Expressed as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), this parameter defines the volume of syngas passing through a unit mass of catalyst per hour, thereby determining the contact time for the reaction. In the experimental setup, a GHSV of 3 nl/(g·h) is maintained. This setting ensures controlled exposure of the reactants to the catalyst, which is essential for optimizing reaction kinetics and achieving the desired selectivity, while also contributing to the overall stability and performance profile of the evaluated catalysts.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Composition
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for identifying the active phases relevant to Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS).
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of all synthesized catalysts revealed a cubic hematite (Fe₂O₃) structure, with a characteristic (104) reflection at 2θ = 33.3°. No new crystalline phases were detected upon the addition of La or Ba promoters, indicating that the primary phase remained unchanged after promoter incorporation and calcination.
- **Observations:** The retention of the hematite structure across all samples suggests that promoter addition does not alter the bulk phase but may influence surface properties and reducibility.

#### 6.1.2 Particle Size and Morphology
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to assess nanoparticle size and morphology, while the Scherrer equation was applied to XRD data for average crystallite size estimation.
- **Findings:** TEM images showed that all catalysts, regardless of promoter type or ratio, consisted of nanoparticles with sizes ranging from 10 to 30 nm. Scherrer analysis of the XRD (104) peak corroborated these findings, indicating consistent nanoscale crystallite dimensions.
- **Observations:** The microemulsion synthesis method yielded uniform particle size distributions and morphologies across all samples, with no significant differences observed between promoted and unpromoted catalysts.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen physisorption (BET) analysis was conducted to determine surface area, pore volume, and average pore size, parameters critical for catalytic activity and reactant diffusion.
- **Findings:** The addition of La, Ba, or their combination as promoters led to a decrease in both BET surface area and pore volume compared to the unpromoted 100Fe/4Cu catalyst. This reduction is attributed to increased aggregation of crystallites and partial pore blocking.
- **Observations:** Although promoter addition reduced surface area and porosity, the catalysts maintained sufficient nanostructure for effective FTS performance.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction Behavior (H₂-TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction with hydrogen (H₂-TPR) was used to evaluate the reducibility of the catalysts, a key factor influencing the formation of active iron phases.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR profiles exhibited three main reduction stages: (1) CuO to Cu, (2) Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄, and (3) Fe₃O₄ to Fe. Promoted catalysts showed higher reduction temperatures and increased H₂ consumption (mol H₂/mol Fe) compared to the unpromoted sample. The reduction peaks shifted to higher temperatures with La and/or Ba addition.
- **Observations:** The presence of promoters increases the reduction temperature, indicating stronger metal-support interactions and altered reducibility, which can stabilize active phases during FTS.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Basicity (CO₂-TPD)
- **Description:** CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) was performed to quantify surface basicity, which affects CO adsorption and hydrocarbon chain growth in FTS.
- **Findings:** CO₂-TPD results demonstrated that surface basicity increased in the order: 100Fe/4Cu < 100Fe/4Cu/2La < 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba < 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba. Promoted catalysts exhibited significantly higher CO₂ desorption capacities, with Ba-containing samples showing the highest basicity.
- **Observations:** Enhanced basicity correlates with improved CO adsorption and selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons. The dual-promoted catalyst (1La/1Ba) displayed a synergistic increase in basicity and catalytic performance.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under fixed-bed reactor conditions (290 °C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.1, GHSV = 3 nl/(g·h)), the following CO conversion values were observed after 72 h on stream:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 59.6%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 68.5%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 71.8%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 79.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of La and Ba promoters significantly enhances CO conversion, with the dual-promoted 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba catalyst exhibiting the highest activity. This improvement is attributed to increased surface basicity and modified CO adsorption, facilitating chain growth reactions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting water–gas shift (WGS) activity. It is typically calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂ selectivity values (mol%) for each catalyst:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 17.29%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 20.31%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 23.43%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 25.03%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition, especially the combination of La and Ba, increases CO₂ selectivity, indicating enhanced WGS activity alongside FTS.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity (mol%) for each catalyst:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.96%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 10.44%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 12.32%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 10.81%
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of La and/or Ba suppresses methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 C₂–C₄ and C₅⁺ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** Selectivity for C₂–C₄ and C₅⁺ hydrocarbons reflects the catalyst’s ability to promote chain growth. Selectivity is reported as the mol% of each hydrocarbon fraction in the product stream.
- **Experimental Data:** For 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba:
  - C₂–C₄: 25.30%
  - C₅–C₁₂: 21.80%
  - C₁₃–C₁₉: 10.50%
  - C₁₉⁺: 6.56%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoted catalysts, particularly with Ba and La, show increased selectivity toward heavier hydrocarbons (C₅⁺), indicating improved chain growth.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, represents the probability of chain propagation versus termination:
  \[
  x_i = (1 - \alpha)\alpha^{i-1}
  \]
  where \( x_i \) is the mole fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( i \).
- **Experimental Data:** For 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba at various temperatures:
  - 295 °C: α = 0.29
  - 400 °C: α = 0.41
  - 412 °C: α = 0.41
  - 620 °C: α = 0.54
  - 680 °C: α = 0.43
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values at elevated temperatures correspond to increased selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over 72 h. The deactivation rate is inferred from the decline in conversion:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time (h)}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Promoted catalysts (especially 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba) maintain higher CO conversion over time, indicating slower deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The dual-promoted catalyst exhibits superior stability, while copper alone accelerates deactivation.

**Summary:** The combined use of La and Ba promoters in nanostructured iron catalysts significantly enhances CO conversion, increases selectivity for heavier hydrocarbons, raises the α factor, and improves catalyst stability, demonstrating a clear synergistic effect in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
