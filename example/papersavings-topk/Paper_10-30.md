## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The Fischer–Tropsch catalysts (100Fe/4Cu, 100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba) were synthesized using a water‑in‑oil microemulsion method. This method was selected for its ability to control metal dispersion and produce nanostructured catalysts; however, it requires strict control of reaction parameters and may present challenges for large‑scale synthesis.

#### Water-in‑Oil Microemulsion Method
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   • An aqueous solution containing FeCl₃·6H₂O (primary Fe precursor) is prepared and mixed with an organic phase comprising 1‑butanol and chloroform with SDS as the surfactant.  
   • Copper oxide is introduced concurrently as a promoter.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   • Hydrazine (25–30% concentration) is added as the precipitating agent.  
   • The mixture is stirred for 4 hours, allowing the formation of a solid catalyst precursor (evidenced by a color change and precipitate formation), and then decanted overnight for phase separation.
3. • Drying  
   • The solid precipitate is recovered by centrifugation, washed sequentially with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone, and subsequently dried at 110°C overnight.
4. • Calcination  
   • The dried material is calcined in air at 400°C for 4 hours to promote the formation of active crystalline structures.
5. • Additional Post‑treatments for Promoted Catalysts  
   • For catalysts containing La and/or Ba, an extra drying at 110°C for 15 hours is performed followed by a second calcination at 400°C for 4 hours.

### Comparative Analysis of the Methods

The microemulsion technique yields catalysts with highly uniform metal dispersion and controlled nanoparticle sizes, which enhance both Fischer–Tropsch and water–gas shift activities. However, its complexity in handling multiple solvents and strict temperature and time controls may limit scalability and increase operational costs. The step‑by‑step precision—from precursor mixing through the post‑treatment—ensures reproducibility, making it suitable for research environments where detailed catalyst performance is essential.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

The following procedures detail the step‐by‐step synthesis for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, starting from raw precursors and following a strict workflow: Precursor Preparation → Catalyst Assembly → Purification → Drying & Calcination → (Optional) Activation. Each catalyst synthesis is described individually below.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe/4Cu

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, an appropriate amount of nanostructured copper oxide (for a 100Fe/4Cu atomic ratio), 50 mL deionized water; for the organic phase: 10 mL 1‐butanol, 10 mL chloroform, 0.5 g SDS.
- **Conditions**: Room temperature; vigorous stirring for 4 hours.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O in water.
  - Prepare the organic phase by mixing 1‐butanol, chloroform, and SDS.
  - Gradually combine the aqueous and organic phases to form a stable water‐in‐oil microemulsion.
  - Introduce the copper oxide precursor.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure homogeneous dispersion and stable microemulsion.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Precipitation
- **Reagents**: 25–30% hydrazine solution.
- **Process**:
  - Add hydrazine dropwise to the microemulsion for controlled precipitation.
  - Maintain stirring for the full 4‐hour period.
- **Key Steps**: Prevent rapid precipitation by controlled reagent addition.

##### Step 3: Purification
- Allow the mixture to decant overnight.
- Recover precipitate by centrifugation and wash sequentially with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Overnight at 110°C.
- **Calcination**: Heat in air at 400°C for 4 hours (controlled ramp rate preferred).

##### Step 5: Activation
- Optional reduction step under H₂ flow may be applied prior to catalytic evaluation.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Promoted Catalysts (100Fe/4Cu/2La, 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba)

Follow the same Steps 1 and 2 as for 100Fe/4Cu with the following modifications during catalyst assembly:

- **Promoter Incorporation**: After initial mixing of iron and copper precursors, add the promoter solution:
  - For 100Fe/4Cu/2La and 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: Add lanthanum oxide or barium oxide to achieve a “2” loading.
  - For 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: Add both promoters in balanced amounts.
- Proceed with the precipitation using hydrazine as above.

For purification, drying, and calcination:
- **Additional Drying**: For promoted catalysts, perform an extra drying at 110°C for 15 hours.
- **Second Calcination**: Calcine again at 400°C for 4 hours to ensure optimal promoter interaction.

These sequential, detailed steps ensure reproducibility and clarity in preparing catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard Fischer–Tropsch conditions (290°C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.1, GHSV = 3 nl/(g·h)), the following CO conversion values were observed:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 59.6%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 68.5%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 71.8%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 79.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** Both Ba and La promoters individually enhance CO conversion compared to the unpromoted catalyst, with La showing a slightly greater effect. The combined promoter system (1La/1Ba) yields the highest conversion, demonstrating a clear synergistic effect.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating water–gas shift (WGS) activity. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 17.29%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 20.31%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 23.43%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 25.03%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition increases CO₂ selectivity, with the highest value for the dual-promoted catalyst, indicating enhanced WGS activity.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.96%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 10.44%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 12.32%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 10.81%
- **Observations and Trends:** All promoted catalysts exhibit reduced CH₄ selectivity compared to the unpromoted sample, with Ba-containing catalysts showing the lowest values. This shift favors longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 Hydrocarbon Product Distribution and C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** Product selectivity is reported as the molar percentage of each hydrocarbon fraction. C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of heavier hydrocarbons, reflecting chain growth.
- **Experimental Data (C₅–C₁₂ selectivity):**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.79%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 26.46%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 22.13%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 25.18%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition shifts selectivity from light (C₂–C₄) to heavier (C₅⁺) hydrocarbons, with the highest C₅–C₁₂ selectivity observed for Ba-promoted and dual-promoted catalysts.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor represents the probability of chain growth versus termination, calculated using the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model:
  \[
  x_i = (1 - \alpha)\alpha^{i-1}
  \]
  where \( x_i \) is the mole fraction of hydrocarbon with carbon number \( i \).
- **Experimental Data:**
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 0.61
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 0.71
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 0.66
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba: 0.68
- **Observations and Trends:** The α factor increases with promoter addition, indicating enhanced chain growth and a higher yield of long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability (Deactivation Rate)
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over 72 hours. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Promoted catalysts, especially 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba, maintain higher CO conversion with minimal decline over 72 hours, indicating superior stability.
- **Observations and Trends:** The dual-promoted catalyst exhibits the lowest deactivation rate, attributed to improved CO adsorption and reduced water-induced deactivation.
