## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The article describes two distinct synthesis routes for Fischer–Tropsch catalysts: one based on the controlled pyrolysis of MIL-101(Fe) and the other via a co‑precipitation method for the unsupported-Fe catalyst. Both methods emphasize reproducibility through clearly defined sequential steps.

#### Controlled Pyrolysis of MIL-101(Fe)
1. • Precursor Mixing  
   – Reagents: 166 mg terephthalic acid, 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O, 5 ml nitric acid, and 70 ml DMF.  
   – Process: Stirring for 2 h produces a homogeneous solution, which is then transferred to a Teflon‑lined autoclave.
2. • Hydrothermal Synthesis & Washing  
   – Conditions: Reaction at 120 °C for 48 h.  
   – Post‑reaction washing with DMF and ethanol, followed by drying at 80 °C overnight.
3. • Pyrolysis  
   – Equipment: Controlled atmosphere furnace with a continuous flow of 2% C₂H₂ in helium.  
   – Conditions: Heating at a rate of 5 °C/min to target temperatures (500, 600, or 700 °C) and maintaining for 1 h.
4. • Post‑processing  
   – For the Fe@C‑600‑H₂ variant: The catalyst undergoes a hydrogen reduction at 400 °C for 24 h (gas flow: 50 ml/min) to modify the active phase without compromising the carbon shell integrity.

#### Unsupported‑Fe Catalyst via Co‑precipitation
1. • Precursor Mixing  
   – Reagents: Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ solution (1 mol/L) and ammonia solution (3 mol/L) are combined in a 100‑ml beaker at 80 ± 1 °C.  
   – Process: Ammonia is added dropwise to achieve and maintain the pH between 8.2 and 8.5.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   – The iron species precipitate during a controlled aging period of 2 h under constant stirring.
3. • Drying and Calcination  
   – Drying: Carried out for 12 h at 120 °C.  
   – Calcination: Conducted at 500 °C for 5 h in static air to stabilize the iron phase.
4. • Reduction  
   – Final step: Reduction in hydrogen (flow: 100 ml/min) at 350 °C for 24 h.

This structured step-by-step protocol, including the carefully positioned precipitation–deposition step, ensures both methods are reproducible and that the formation of h‑Fe₃C and the development of corresponding carbon shells closely reflect the prescribed synthesis conditions.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@C Catalyst (MIL-101(Fe)-Derived)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 606 mg Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O  
  • 166 mg terephthalic acid  
  • 5 mL nitric acid (mineralizer)  
  • 70 mL N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Stirring for 2 hours at room temperature  
  • Transfer to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heat at 120 °C for 48 hours  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and terephthalic acid in DMF with the added mineralizer, ensuring complete mixing.  
  2. Transfer the homogeneous solution into an autoclave for hydrothermal synthesis.  
  3. Post-synthesis, wash the product with DMF followed by ethanol to remove impurities and residual solvents.  
  4. Dry the washed material at 80 °C overnight.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly (Pyrolysis)  
- **Reagents & Additional Conditions**:  
  • Use the dried MIL-101(Fe) as both iron and carbon precursor.  
  • Pyrolyze in a tube furnace under a flow of 2% C₂H₂ in helium (50 mL/min).  
- **Process**:  
  1. Heat the material at a ramp rate of 5 °C/min to target temperatures (500, 600, or 700 °C).  
  2. Maintain the set temperature for 1 hour to facilitate controlled formation of h-Fe₃C and development of the carbon shell (amorphous at 500 °C; graphene at 600 °C; graphite at 700 °C).

##### Step 3: Post-Processing and Activation  
- **For Fe@C-600 Variant**:  
  • **Activation Step**: Reduce the Fe@C-600 sample under H₂ (flow rate: 50 mL/min) at 400 °C for 24 hours to obtain the Fe@C-600-H₂ catalyst.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  – Ensure accurate control over temperature and gas composition during pyrolysis for reproducibility.  
  – The stepwise process from precursor synthesis to final reduction is critical for achieving desired catalyst phases and carbon shell characteristics.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Unsupported-Fe Catalyst

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Aqueous Fe(NO₃)₃ solution  
  • Ammonia solution  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Dropwise co-precipitation at 80 °C  
  • pH maintained between 8.2–8.5  
- **Process**:  
  1. Slowly add ammonia to the Fe(NO₃)₃ solution under constant stirring to precipitate iron hydroxide.

##### Step 2: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  1. Wash the precipitate several times with distilled water and filter.  
  2. Calcine the purified precursor at 500 °C for 5 hours in static air.  
  3. Follow with reduction at 350 °C for 24 hours under hydrogen (flow rate: 100 mL/min).

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is a critical pretreatment step designed to transform the catalyst precursor into an active phase suitable for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This section details the hydrogen reduction activation employed for the Fe@C catalysts as described in the article.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
Activation temperature is pivotal for the effective reduction of the iron precursor and formation of the active iron carbide (h‑Fe₃C) phase. In the preparation of the Fe@C-600-H₂ catalyst, the precursor material is subjected to a hydrogen reduction step at precisely 400 °C. This temperature is carefully selected to promote complete reduction of the iron species while preserving the integrity of the protective graphene shell, which inhibits nanoparticle agglomeration. In contrast, the unsupported‑Fe catalyst undergoes a similar activation process at 350 °C, a slightly lower temperature that may yield distinct metal dispersion and phase outcomes.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Gas
The activation process employs hydrogen gas, a highly effective reducing agent that facilitates the removal of oxygen from the iron precursor. The use of hydrogen not only ensures the formation of the h‑Fe₃C phase but also contributes to a controlled structural transformation of the catalyst. During the reduction step, hydrogen is introduced at a controlled flow of 50 mL/min, which maintains a stable reducing environment throughout the treatment.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Time
The duration of the activation step is set at 24 hours for both the Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported‑Fe catalysts. This extended period guarantees that the reduction process is thoroughly completed, ensuring uniform conversion of the precursor into active catalytic sites. A 24‑hour activation regime is crucial for achieving high catalyst activity and stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, as it permits the formation of a robust active phase and prevents incomplete reduction or excessive sintering.

These activation parameters—temperature, hydrogen atmosphere, controlled flow rate, and extended duration—are integral to tailoring the catalyst structure. They ensure that the Fe@C-600-H₂ catalyst possesses a well‐defined iron carbide phase and a stable, protective carbon shell, which are essential for optimized performance in Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer-Tropsch reaction was carried out in a fixed-bed reactor under meticulously controlled conditions to ensure reproducibility and reliable catalytic performance. The experimental setup and parameters are detailed below.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reactor temperature is the operational parameter that activates the syngas and influences the reaction kinetics, directly impacting CO conversion and hydrocarbon chain growth.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction was performed at a temperature of 300 °C.  
- **Observations:** At 300 °C, the h-Fe₃C dominated Fe@C catalysts exhibited high catalytic activity, with optimum syngas activation and CO conversion rates reaching up to 78.7%, while supporting an efficient shift toward long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure determines the concentration of feed gases in the reactor, thereby affecting product distribution and catalyst surface interactions.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The process was conducted at 2.0 MPa within the fixed-bed reactor.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 2.0 MPa enhances selectivity towards long-chain hydrocarbons (C₅⁺ selectivity between 57%–60%) by favoring the formation of heavier products over lighter molecules such as methane.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
- **Definition:** The hydrogen-to-carbon monoxide ratio is critical in tuning the reaction mechanism, influencing both the conversion efficiency and product distribution.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A syngas mixture with an H₂/CO molar ratio of 1.0 was employed.  
- **Observations:** This ratio, typical for coal- or biomass-derived feeds, limits methane production (CH₄ selectivity of approximately 10.5–12.4%) while promoting the formation of long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** This parameter, expressed as weight hourly space velocity (WHSV), represents the flow rate of syngas relative to the catalyst mass, impacting contact time and conversion efficiency.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** For MOF-derived catalysts, a WHSV of 8000 mL/(g·h) was maintained, whereas an unsupported-Fe catalyst was evaluated at 3000 mL/(g·h).  
- **Observations:** The controlled space-time velocities support reproducibility in CO conversion and catalyst performance assessments, ensuring that variations in specific activity are attributed primarily to catalyst structure.

The fixed-bed reactor is configured with an isothermal bed length of 5 cm, incorporating 0.5 g of catalyst diluted with 2.0 g of SiC, with the remaining volume filled with SiC particles. This uniform setup enhances synergy between reaction parameters and catalyst performance, enabling detailed evaluation of the Fe@C system under realistic Fischer-Tropsch operating conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy (MES), providing insights into the evolution of iron species and the formation of active phases.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the MIL-101(Fe) precursor display characteristic peaks at 2θ = 3.8°, 5.9°, and 10.4°, confirming its MOF structure. Upon pyrolysis in 2% C₂H₂/He at increasing temperatures (500, 600, 700 °C), these peaks disappear, and new reflections corresponding to α-Fe and h-Fe₃C emerge. MES quantifies the h-Fe₃C content, which increases from 68.2% in Fe@C-500 to 95.5% in Fe@C-700. After Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS), h-Fe₃C remains the dominant phase (about 88% in Fe@C-600 after 50 and 120 h FTS).
- **Observations:** Higher pyrolysis temperatures promote the reduction and carburization of iron, leading to increased h-Fe₃C content and enhanced graphitization of the carbon shell. The dominance and stability of h-Fe₃C are directly linked to catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Textural properties were assessed by N₂ physisorption (BET method), measuring surface area (SBET), pore volume (Vp), and mean pore size.
- **Findings:** MIL-101(Fe) exhibits a high SBET of 1632 m²/g, Vp of 0.83 cm³/g, and mean pore size of 1.3 nm. After pyrolysis, Fe@C catalysts show reduced surface areas (Fe@C-500: 135 m²/g; Fe@C-600: 280 m²/g; Fe@C-700: 165 m²/g) and increased mean pore sizes (3.3–3.8 nm), with Vp values of 0.23–0.28 cm³/g. Unsupported-Fe displays much lower porosity (Vp = 0.04 cm³/g).
- **Observations:** The collapse of the MOF structure during pyrolysis reduces surface area but increases mesoporosity, facilitating improved reactant diffusion in Fe@C catalysts.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphological features and particle size distributions were examined by SEM and TEM, with elemental mapping (EDS) confirming spatial composition.
- **Findings:** MIL-101(Fe) crystals are octahedral (~150 nm). After pyrolysis, Fe@C catalysts consist of iron nanoparticles uniformly encapsulated in carbon shells. Fe@C-600 exhibits smaller, well-dispersed particles due to the graphene shell, while Fe@C-700 shows larger particles (~40 nm) from aggregation. Elemental mapping demonstrates the overlap of Fe, C, and O, confirming the core–shell structure.
- **Observations:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 effectively inhibits nanoparticle aggregation, maintaining uniform dispersion and enhancing catalyst stability.

#### 6.1.4 Carbon Shell Structure
- **Description:** Raman spectroscopy was used to distinguish between amorphous carbon, graphene, and graphite shells based on D and G band features.
- **Findings:** Fe@C-500 contains predominantly amorphous carbon (higher ID/IG ratio), Fe@C-600 forms a graphene shell (lower ID/IG, presence of 2D band), and Fe@C-700 develops a graphite shell (further decreased ID/IG).
- **Observations:** The nature of the carbon shell is controlled by pyrolysis temperature, with the graphene shell providing optimal protection and stability for the iron core during FTS.

#### 6.1.5 Thermogravimetric Analysis
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric analysis (TG) monitored weight loss during heating to assess decomposition and carbon deposition.
- **Findings:** Two main weight loss stages were observed: solvent removal below 300 °C and linker decomposition above 300 °C. In C₂H₂, carbon deposition is evidenced by reduced weight loss compared to inert gas, especially at higher temperatures.
- **Observations:** Ethyne-assisted pyrolysis enhances carbon shell formation, crucial for stabilizing the active phase.

#### 6.1.6 Elemental Composition and Distribution
- **Description:** Elemental mapping (EDS) provided spatial distribution of Fe, C, and O within the catalysts.
- **Findings:** EDS mapping confirms uniform encapsulation of iron nanoparticles within the carbon matrix, with Fe-rich regions corresponding to lower carbon intensity.
- **Observations:** This encapsulation is significant for maintaining active sites and preventing sintering, directly supporting high Fischer–Tropsch activity and selectivity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is typically calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (300 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.0), Fe@C-600-H₂ achieved a CO conversion of 78.7% after 24 hours on stream. The Fe@C-600 catalyst exhibited 65.0% conversion at 24 hours, increasing to 74.5% at 184 hours. Fe@C-500 and Fe@C-700 showed lower conversions and greater deactivation, while unsupported-Fe reached 75.3% at 24 hours.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal CO conversion was observed for Fe@C-600-H₂, attributed to the hydrogen reduction step. Fe@C-600 demonstrated stable or even increasing conversion over extended operation, indicating excellent long-term activity.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** For Fe@C-x catalysts, CH₄ selectivity ranged from 10.5% to 12.4% at 24 hours. Fe@C-600-H₂ exhibited a higher CH₄ selectivity of 23.4%, while unsupported-Fe reached 28.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of h-Fe₃C and a graphene shell in Fe@C-600 suppressed methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbons. Hydrogen reduction increased CH₄ selectivity, indicating a shift in product distribution.

#### 6.2.3 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity denotes the fraction of converted CO yielding hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, reflecting chain growth efficiency.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-x catalysts achieved C₅⁺ selectivity between 59.0% and 60.4% at 24 hours. Fe@C-600-H₂ showed a reduced C₅⁺ selectivity of 38.6%, and unsupported-Fe was significantly lower at 14.7%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest C₅⁺ selectivity was observed for Fe@C-600, correlating with the presence of h-Fe₃C and a graphene shell. Hydrogen reduction and the absence of a carbon shell both led to diminished long-chain hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination, derived from the slope of ln(Wₙ/n) versus n, where Wₙ is the mass fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length n.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-x catalysts exhibited α values around 0.67–0.69, supporting high C₅⁺ selectivity. Fe@C-600-H₂ and unsupported-Fe had lower α values of 0.49 and 0.30, respectively.
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values in Fe@C-x catalysts are directly linked to enhanced long-chain hydrocarbon production and lower methane selectivity.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring the decline in CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is calculated as the percentage decrease in CO conversion during the test period.
- **Experimental Data:** Over 184 hours, Fe@C-500 and Fe@C-700 deactivated by 13.1% and 20.1%, respectively, while unsupported-Fe deactivated by 47.9%. Fe@C-600 showed stable or increasing CO conversion, indicating minimal deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The graphene shell in Fe@C-600 effectively inhibited nanoparticle aggregation, resulting in superior stability and sustained activity.

#### 6.2.6 Iron Time Yield (FTY)
- **Description:** FTY measures catalytic activity as μmol CO converted per gram of iron per second.
- **Experimental Data:** Fe@C-x catalysts achieved FTY values of 161–176 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹ at 24 hours, with Fe@C-600-H₂ reaching 203.0 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹ and unsupported-Fe at 39.5 μmolCO·gFe⁻¹·s⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** The high FTY of Fe@C catalysts underscores the efficiency of MOF-derived core–shell structures, with Fe@C-600-H₂ showing the highest activity but less favorable selectivity.

**Summary:**  
The h-Fe₃C dominated Fe@C core–shell catalysts, particularly Fe@C-600, exhibit a balanced combination of high CO conversion, superior long-chain hydrocarbon selectivity, high chain growth probability, and exceptional stability. The graphene shell plays a pivotal role in maintaining nanoparticle dispersion and catalytic performance over extended operation.
