## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the microemulsion synthesis method used for preparing the nanostructured iron catalysts promoted with Cu, La, and Ba. The method emphasizes reproducibility through precise control of reaction conditions, reagent concentrations, and post‑processing steps.

#### Catalyst Synthesis via the Microemulsion Approach

• **Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques**  
 – The microemulsion method employs a water-in-oil system to achieve controlled nanoparticle formation, ensuring uniform morphology and size.  
 – This method offers advantages such as the precise control of reaction conditions, although it may increase processing complexity and challenges for scale-up.

#### Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. **Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation**  
 • Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O along with the Cu precursor in distilled water.  
 • Prepare an oil phase by mixing 1‑butanol and chloroform (1:1 ratio) and add sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as a surfactant.  
 • Combine both phases and stir for 4 hours to ensure homogeneous mixing.  
 • Observed Phenomenon: Upon addition, a slight color change is noted as hydrazine (25–30%) is introduced, triggering the formation of a precipitate.

2. **Precipitation–Deposition**  
 • Immediately after the initial mixing, add hydrazine dropwise under continuous stirring.  
 • Allow the mixture to decant overnight, promoting complete deposition of the catalyst precursors.

3. **Drying**  
 • Recover the solid by centrifugation and wash with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.  
 • Dry the washed precipitate at 110 °C overnight.

4. **Calcination**  
 • Calcine the dried catalyst in air at 400 °C for 4 hours.  
 • This step facilitates phase transformation and stability in the catalyst structure.

5. **Post‑processing Treatments**  
 • Reduce the calcined powders by flowing a 10% H₂–90% N₂ gas mixture for 3 hours.  
 • Subsequent activation in a controlled synthesis gas (H₂/CO) environment ensures the catalysts are ready for Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

Each step is executed with specific parameters (e.g., temperatures, durations, concentrations) and equipment (e.g., controlled stirring systems, centrifuge) to ensure replicability. ### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 100Fe/4Cu

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 10.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 4.0 g Cu(NO₃)₂, 50 mL 1-butanol, 50 mL chloroform, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as surfactant, and hydrazine solution (25–30%).  
- **Conditions**: Stirring at room temperature for 4 hours; allow decantation overnight.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve the Fe and Cu salts in deionized water.  
  - Mix the aqueous solution with the organic phase (1-butanol and chloroform, 1:1) containing SDS to create a water-in-oil microemulsion.  
  - Add hydrazine dropwise while stirring vigorously to precipitate nano-sized metal hydroxide precursors.  
  - Leave the mixture undisturbed overnight to ensure complete nanoparticle formation and precursor stabilization.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain constant stirring to ensure homogeneity and precise timing to control droplet size within the microemulsion.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly & Purification  
- **Process**:  
  - Recover the solid product via centrifugation and wash it sequentially with distilled water, ethanol, and acetone.  
  - This thorough washing removes any residual surfactant and unreacted chemicals, ensuring purity.  
- **Key Steps**: Optimize centrifugation speed and solvent volumes for complete removal of impurities.

##### Step 3: Drying & Calcination  
- **Drying**: Dry the washed catalyst overnight at 110 °C to eliminate moisture.  
- **Calcination**: Calcine in air at 400 °C for 4 hours with a controlled ramp rate to develop the desired crystalline phases.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor temperature carefully during calcination to preserve the nanostructure.

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Process**:  
  - Load ~1.1 g of the calcined catalyst in a fixed-bed stainless steel reactor.  
  - Reduce the catalyst under a flow of 10% H₂–90% N₂ at 270 °C for 3 hours.  
  - Following reduction, expose the catalyst to a synthesis gas stream (H₂/CO ratio of 1) for 24 hours to fully activate it for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.  
- **Key Steps**: Precise control of gas flow and temperature during activation is critical for reproducibility.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase composition of the catalysts were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Philips PW1840 diffractometer employing monochromatized Cu (Kα) radiation. The Scherrer equation was applied to estimate average crystallite sizes from the diffraction patterns.
- **Findings:** All catalysts, regardless of promoter composition, exhibited the cubic hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) crystal structure, as confirmed by characteristic peaks (notably at 2θ = 33.3°, corresponding to the (104) plane). No new crystalline phases were detected upon addition of La or Ba promoters. The average crystallite sizes, calculated via the Scherrer equation, were consistent with nanoscale dimensions.
- **Observations:** The preservation of the hematite phase across all samples indicates that promoter addition does not alter the fundamental crystal structure. The nanoscale crystallite size is expected to enhance catalytic activity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and pore volume were measured by N₂ physisorption using the BET method on a Micromeritics ASAP 2010 system. Samples were degassed at 100 °C for 1 h and 300 °C for 2 h prior to analysis.
- **Findings:** The addition of La, Ba, or their combination led to a decrease in both BET surface area and pore volume compared to the unpromoted catalyst. This reduction is attributed to aggregation of crystallites and partial pore blocking induced by the promoters.
- **Observations:** Lower surface area and pore volume may influence reactant diffusion but are offset by the beneficial electronic and structural effects of the promoters.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a LEO 912AB instrument was employed to assess particle morphology and size distribution.
- **Findings:** TEM images revealed uniformly distributed nanoparticles with sizes in the range of 10–30 nm across all catalyst samples. No significant differences in particle size or morphology were observed with varying promoter compositions.
- **Observations:** The microemulsion synthesis method effectively produces nanosized particles, and promoter addition does not disrupt the uniformity or size distribution of the catalyst nanoparticles.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility (H₂-TPR Analysis)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) was conducted on a Micromeritics TPD-TPR 290 system to evaluate the reducibility of catalyst components.
- **Findings:** Three distinct reduction stages were observed: (1) CuO to Cu, (2) Fe₂O₃ to Fe₃O₄, and (3) Fe₃O₄ to Fe. The addition of La and Ba promoters increased the reduction temperatures, indicating a decrease in reducibility at higher basicity.
- **Observations:** Enhanced reduction temperatures with promoter addition suggest stronger metal-support interactions and modified electronic environments, which can influence catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Basicity (CO₂-TPD Analysis)
- **Description:** Surface basicity was assessed by CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD), with CO₂ chemisorbed at 100 °C and desorption monitored from 80 to 430 °C.
- **Findings:** Surface basicity increased with promoter addition in the order: 100Fe/4Cu < 100Fe/4Cu/2La < 100Fe/4Cu/1La/1Ba < 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba. Quantitative CO₂ desorption values were 123, 575, 747, and 1062 mmol/g_cat, respectively. Ba-promoted catalysts exhibited broader and higher-temperature desorption peaks, indicating more abundant and stronger basic sites.
- **Observations:** Increased surface basicity enhances CO adsorption and is correlated with improved catalytic activity and selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of carbon monoxide in the feed that is transformed into products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is typically calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under reaction conditions of 290°C, 1.8 MPa, H₂/CO = 1.1, and GHSV = 3 nl/(g·h), the CO conversion values after 72 hours on stream were:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 59.6%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 68.5%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 71.8%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 79.6%
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of Ba and/or La promoters significantly enhances CO conversion, with the dual-promoted 1Ba/1La catalyst exhibiting the highest activity. This improvement is attributed to synergistic effects between the promoters, which enhance surface basicity and facilitate CO adsorption and activation.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Reported CO₂ selectivities (mol%) are:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 17.29%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 20.31%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 23.43%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 25.03%
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition increases CO₂ selectivity, indicating enhanced WGS activity. The highest CO₂ selectivity is observed for the 1Ba/1La catalyst, consistent with its superior CO conversion.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Measured CH₄ selectivities (mol%) are:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 15.96%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 10.44%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 12.32%
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 10.81%
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of Ba and/or La suppresses methane formation, with the lowest CH₄ selectivity observed for the Ba- and La-promoted catalysts. This suppression is linked to increased chain growth probability and improved catalyst basicity.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Hydrocarbon Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbon atoms, a key metric for liquid fuel production. It is calculated by summing the selectivities for C₅–C₁₂, C₁₃–C₁₉, and C₁₉⁺ fractions.
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivities (mol%) are:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 33.89% (C₅–C₁₂: 15.79%, C₁₃–C₁₉: 10.41%, C₁₉⁺: 7.69%)
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 45.19% (C₅–C₁₂: 26.46%, C₁₃–C₁₉: 11.41%, C₁₉⁺: 7.32%)
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 38.01% (C₅–C₁₂: 20.92%, C₁₃–C₁₉: 10.59%, C₁₉⁺: 6.50%)
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 38.90% (C₅–C₁₂: 21.80%, C₁₃–C₁₉: 10.50%, C₁₉⁺: 6.60%)
- **Observations and Trends:** Promoter addition, especially Ba, increases C₅⁺ selectivity, favoring the production of longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model, represents the probability of chain growth versus termination:
  \[
  x_i = (1 - \alpha)\alpha^{i-1}
  \]
  where \( x_i \) is the mole fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( i \).
- **Experimental Data:** Reported α values:
  - 100Fe/4Cu: 0.61
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2Ba: 0.71
  - 100Fe/4Cu/2La: 0.66
  - 100Fe/4Cu/1Ba/1La: 0.68
- **Observations and Trends:** Higher α values in promoted catalysts indicate a greater tendency for chain growth, correlating with increased selectivity for heavier hydrocarbons and reduced methane formation.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over a 72-hour time-on-stream period. Deactivation rate is inferred from the decline in CO conversion.
- **Experimental Data:** All catalysts maintained stable performance over 72 hours, with promoted catalysts exhibiting slower deactivation and higher sustained activity compared to the unpromoted sample.
- **Observations and Trends:** The dual-promoted 1Ba/1La catalyst demonstrated the highest stability, attributed to enhanced surface basicity and improved resistance to deactivation mechanisms.

---
**Summary:** The systematic addition of Ba and La promoters to nanostructured Fe/Cu catalysts markedly improves CO conversion, enhances selectivity for longer-chain hydrocarbons, increases chain growth probability, and boosts catalyst stability. The combined Ba/La system (1Ba/1La) delivers the most favorable performance metrics, underscoring the synergistic effect of dual promotion in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
