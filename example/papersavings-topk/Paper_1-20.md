## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis involves a multi‐step process encompassing hydrothermal nanoparticle formation, deposition–precipitation of manganese, and subsequent SiO₂ coating. Two distinct synthesis paths are described for the hydrophilic (Fe@Mn@xSi) and hydrophobic (Fe@Mn@xSi‐c) catalysts.

#### Hydrothermal Synthesis of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
  – Dissolve 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O in 110 mL water.  
  – Add 12.4 g sodium acetate trihydrate and 3.8 g hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide; stir for 0.5 h.  
  – Introduce 26 mL of ethylenediamine to the mixture.
2. • Hydrothermal Treatment  
  – Heat the sealed reactor to 200 °C for 10 h.
3. • Drying and Calcination  
  – Wash the product, then dry at 100 °C for 12 h.  
  – Calcine the dried material at 500 °C for 5 h.  

This method ensures well‐defined iron oxide precursors, with high temperature and extended reaction times contributing to reproducibility but requiring specialized high‑pressure equipment.

#### Mn Deposition and SiO₂ Coating
1. • Manganese Deposition by Deposition–Precipitation  
  – Disperse 0.5 g of the calcined Fe₂O₃ in 300 mL ethanol.  
  – Add 5 mL of a potassium permanganate aqueous solution dropwise.  
  – Stir the mixture at 30 °C for 2 h, then collect by centrifugation, wash, and dry.
2. • SiO₂ Coating via Modified Stöber Process  
  – Disperse the Fe@Mn nanoparticles in 150 mL ethanol using ultrasonication.  
  – Add the TEOS precursor (0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL depending on desired shell thickness) while stirring at 30 °C.  
  – Introduce 2.5 mL ammonia solution (25–28%) and 10 mL water; stir for 3 h, followed by an additional 4 h.  
  – Collect the coated catalyst by centrifugation, wash with ethanol, and dry at 100 °C for 12 h.
3. • Post‑silanization Treatment (Hydrophobic Variant – Fe@Mn@xSi‑c)  
  – After drying, add 0.5 mL chlorotrimethylsilane and subject the mixture to ultrasonic treatment at room temperature for 1 h.

A comparative analysis shows that while the hydrothermal and deposition–precipitation methods offer robust formation of active phases, the SiO₂ coating step—with or without post‑silanization—provides versatility in tuning catalyst surface properties. The hydrophilic route is straightforward but may lead to enhanced oxidation, whereas the hydrophobic modification protects the active iron carbide phase, thereby improving selectivity and long‐term stability.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic)
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O**: Weighed 7.5 g using a laboratory balance.
- **KMnO₄**: Weighed 0.078 g for manganese promotion.

##### 3.1.1.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic)
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O**: Weighed 7.5 g using a laboratory balance.
- **KMnO₄**: Weighed 0.078 g for manganese promotion.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic)
- **Ethanol**: Transferred 150 mL using a graduated cylinder to disperse the Fe@Mn precursor.
- **TEOS**: Added in variable volumes (0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL) directly to the ethanol dispersion for precise control of the SiO₂ shell thickness.
- **Ammonia solution (25–28%)**: Added dropwise (2.5 mL) using a peristaltic pump to facilitate TEOS hydrolysis.
- **Water**: Poured 10 mL into the reaction mixture to complete the hydrolysis step.

##### 3.1.2.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic)
- **Ethanol, TEOS, Ammonia solution, and Water**: Transferred with the same volumes and methods as for the hydrophilic catalyst.
- **Chlorotrimethylsilane**: Added post-coating (quantity adjusted as necessary) to impart hydrophobicity, ensuring subsequent modification of the SiO₂ shell.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic)
- **SiO₂ coating mixture**: Stirred at 30 °C for 3 hours to ensure a uniform deposition of the amorphous shell, followed by ultrasonic treatment for 1 hour at room temperature to enhance homogeneity.

##### 3.1.3.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic)
- **Post-silanization mixture**: After the addition of chlorotrimethylsilane, the composite was similarly stirred at 30 °C for 3 hours and subsequently subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 1 hour at room temperature, ensuring effective surface silanization and reproducible catalyst performance.

These detailed reagent preparation steps—organized by weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing—ensure clarity, precision, and reproducibility in synthesizing both the hydrophilic and hydrophobic catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Synthesis of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles  
- **Reagents**: 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 12.4 g sodium acetate trihydrate, 3.8 g hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide, and an appropriate volume of deionized water.  
- **Conditions**: Hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 10 hours with continuous stirring to ensure a homogeneous mixture.  
- **Process**:  
  • Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O, sodium acetate trihydrate, and hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide in water.  
  • Stir the solution to achieve complete dissolution before subjecting it to hydrothermal treatment.  
  • After treatment, collect the precipitate and proceed to calcination at 500 °C for 5 hours in an appropriate atmosphere.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and maintain precise temperature control during both hydrothermal and calcination steps.

##### Step 2: Mn Deposition via Deposition‐Precipitation  
- **Reagents**: 0.5 g calcined Fe₂O₃, 5 mL aqueous KMnO₄ solution (0.078 g KMnO₄ in water), and 300 mL ethanol.  
- **Conditions**: Maintain stirring at approximately 30 °C for 2 hours during the slow dropwise addition of the KMnO₄ solution.  
- **Process**:  
  • Disperse Fe₂O₃ in ethanol using mechanical stirring.  
  • Add KMnO₄ solution dropwise with controlled stirring to ensure uniform deposition of Mn.  
- **Key Steps**: Control the addition rate to avoid local supersaturation and ensure even distribution.

##### Step 3: SiO₂ Coating via Modified Stöber Process  
- **Reagents**: The Fe@Mn composite from Step 2, 150 mL ethanol, TEOS (variable: 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL), 2.5 mL ammonia solution (25–28%), and 10 mL water.  
- **Conditions**: Initial stirring at 30 °C for 3 hours after TEOS addition, followed by an additional 4 hours after adding ammonia and water.  
- **Process**:  
  • Disperse Fe@Mn in ethanol via ultrasonication.  
  • Add TEOS and stir, then adjust pH by adding ammonia and water.  
  • Collect the product by centrifugation, wash with ethanol, and dry at 100 °C for 12 hours.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor pH adjustment carefully to ensure proper hydrolysis and condensation of TEOS.

---

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic)

##### Step 4: Post‐Silanization Process  
- **Reagents**: 0.5 g Fe@Mn@xSi, 70 mL n-hexane, and 0.5 mL chlorotrimethylsilane.  
- **Conditions**: Pre-dry catalyst in a vacuum oven at 120 °C for 10 hours; perform ultrasonic treatment at room temperature for 1 hour; post-washing, dry in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 6 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  • After cooling the pre-dried Fe@Mn@xSi, suspend it in n-hexane.  
  • Add chlorotrimethylsilane and ultrasonicate the mixture to enable surface modification.  
  • Centrifuge, wash with n-hexane, and dry as prescribed.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete silanization to achieve the desired hydrophobicity while protecting the core catalyst structure.

This stepwise workflow, covering precursor synthesis through metal deposition, SiO₂ coating, and post-silanization, has been designed for clarity and reproducibility in Fischer–Tropsch catalyst synthesis.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD) and In‑situ XRD  
• **Operational Description**: The crystalline phases of the Fischer–Tropsch catalysts are monitored via both XRD and in‑situ XRD. Prior to measurement, the catalyst’s iron species are converted to iron carbide by reduction in pure H₂ at 350 °C for 20 hours followed by carbonization under a 31.6% CO/68.4% N₂ mixture at 320 °C for 20 hours.  
• **Instruments and Models**: Ultima IV‑185 diffractometer equipped with an HT‑XRD‑900 reactor chamber (Beijing Scistar Technology Co., Ltd.).  
• **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements are performed with a ceramic sample stage; 2θ range of 29°–79°, a scanning step of 0.02° and a rate of 5°/min. In the in‑situ setup, CO + H₂O is introduced (with water vapor generated via a glass wash bottle at 40 °C) to simulate reaction conditions.  
• **Key Notes**: Ensuring correct sample mounting is critical for diffraction accuracy.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)  
• **Operational Description**: A 50 mg sample is loaded in a quartz tube, pre‑treated in N₂ at 350 °C for 1 hour then cooled to 80 °C.  
• **Instruments and Models**: A micro fixed‑bed reactor with thermal conductivity detection (TCD).  
• **Characterization Conditions & Test Parameters**: 5% H₂/95% N₂ gas at 30 mL/min; temperature ramped to 800 °C at 10 °C/min.  
• **Key Notes**: Uniform loading and strict temperature control are essential.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)  
• **Operational Description**: Similar to H₂‑TPR but employing 5% CO/95% N₂ after the N₂ pre‑treatment.  
• **Test Parameters**: Ramp from 80 °C to 800 °C at 10 °C/min under a 30 mL/min gas flow.

### 4.4 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)  
• **Operational Description**: Direct analysis of the powder sample is conducted on a NICOLET 5700 FTIR Spectrometer without wafer preparation.  
• **Key Notes**: Although resolution details are not specified, this method identifies surface chemical groups.

### 4.5 Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS)  
• **Operational Description**: Powder samples are placed in an IR cell and pre‑treated in Ar at 320 °C for 120 minutes (5 °C/min ramp). After background collection, the sample is exposed to CO at 320 °C for 120 minutes then purged in Ar for 120 minutes.

### 4.6 TEM and EDS  
• **Operational Description**: TEM imaging combined with EDS mapping (using a FEI Talos F200x) confirms the core–shell morphology and elemental distribution.  
• **Key Notes**: Sample preparation follows the catalyst synthesis route.

### 4.7 X‑ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)  
• **Operational Description**: Surface chemistry is probed using Thermo Scientific K‑Alpha and ESCALAB Xi+ instruments.  

### 4.8 Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP)  
• **Operational Description**: Catalyst samples are dissolved and analyzed on an Agilent 5110 ICP for compositional determination.

### 4.9 Water‑droplet Contact Angle Measurements

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is integrated with the pre‐reaction treatment to convert the iron oxide precursor into the desired active iron carbide phases. This two‐step procedure involves an initial reduction in pure H₂ followed by a syngas activation step.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Following the reduction step—which is conducted at 350 °C for 20 hours in a pure H₂ atmosphere—the catalyst undergoes an activation treatment at 320 °C. This controlled activation temperature is chosen to promote the selective carburization of the reduced iron species. Operating at 320 °C with syngas ensures that the temperature is sufficient to drive the transformation into active carbide phases (e.g., χ‑Fe₅C₂ and θ‑Fe₃C) without inducing excessive sintering or oxidation.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
Both the reduction and subsequent activation procedures are performed under a controlled pressure of 0.1 MPa. This relatively low pressure is critical to moderating the reduction kinetics and ensuring a gradual phase transformation. By carefully controlling the pressure during the activation step, the process minimizes aggressive gas–solid interactions that could otherwise lead to catalyst agglomeration or excessive reduction.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
After reduction, the activation is carried out using syngas with a defined H₂/CO ratio of 2. This specific gas composition is essential for achieving controlled carburization: H₂ helps to maintain a reducing environment, while CO supplies the necessary carbon for the formation of iron carbide phases. The tailored gas mixture thus directs the phase evolution towards the active sites required for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
The syngas activation step is conducted for 5 hours. This duration has been optimized to ensure complete transformation of the reduced iron species into the active carbide phases while preserving catalyst stability. The protocol, distinctly separated from characterization procedures, enables reproducible synthesis of both hydrophilic (Fe@Mn@xSi) and hydrophobic (Fe@Mn@xSi-c) catalysts, with the latter showing enhanced phase preservation under similar conditions.

Overall, this activation strategy—integrating precise temperature, pressure, gas composition, and timing—ensures that the catalyst is effectively pre-conditioned to deliver optimum performance in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 5.2 Reaction

The Fischer-Tropsch catalytic evaluation was conducted under rigorously controlled reaction conditions to ensure reproducibility and meaningful interpretation of catalyst performance.

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature dictates the energy available for activating the CO molecule and influences both the kinetics of hydrocarbon chain growth and the phase transformation within the catalyst.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reaction temperature was maintained at 320 °C throughout the tests.
- **Observations:** Operating at 320 °C facilitated the conversion of syngas into hydrocarbons by enabling efficient activation of carbon monoxide and hydrogenation, while also promoting the transformation of iron phases in the catalyst.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure controls the concentration of reactant gases in the reactor, thus impacting the equilibrium and kinetics of the Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The fixed-bed reactor was operated at a pressure of 2.0 MPa.
- **Observations:** The elevated pressure improved the overall syngas conversion efficiency by maintaining high reactant concentrations, which is essential for driving hydrocarbon chain formation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The molar ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed gas plays a crucial role in determining the selectivity of the hydrocarbon products.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 2 was used in the catalytic evaluation.
- **Observations:** This stoichiometric balance favored hydrogenation reactions, leading to a product distribution skewed toward lower carbon number hydrocarbons, and significantly influenced the formation of specific catalytic active phases.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity, expressed as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), represents the volume of the feed gas passing through the catalyst per unit time and mass, thereby affecting contact time and reaction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was set to 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹.
- **Observations:** Maintaining this flow rate ensured adequate mass transfer within the reactor; however, variations in catalyst morphology—such as differences between hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si and hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c—resulted in distinct CO conversion profiles and selectivity patterns between methane and higher olefins.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and phase evolution of the Fischer–Tropsch catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), including both ex situ and in situ measurements under reaction-relevant atmospheres. In situ XRD was performed to monitor phase transitions during exposure to CO, H₂, and H₂O at elevated temperatures.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that, after pre-reduction in H₂ at 350 °C and subsequent carbonization in CO at 320 °C, the catalysts predominantly contained χ-Fe₅C₂. Upon exposure to CO + H₂O at 320 °C, hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalysts exhibited a rapid decrease in Fe₅C₂ peaks and the emergence of Fe₃O₄ peaks, indicating oxidation of iron carbide by water. In contrast, hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalysts maintained stable Fe₅C₂ peaks with no Fe₃O₄ formation under identical conditions.
- **Observations:** The results demonstrate that surface hydrophobization effectively inhibits water-induced oxidation, preserving the active iron carbide phase during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Phase Composition Quantification (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to quantify the relative amounts of iron phases present in the catalysts after reaction.
- **Findings:** For Fe@Mn@0.2Si (hydrophilic), the phase composition after reaction was 59.7% χ-Fe₅C₂, 34.3% Fe₃O₄, and 6.0% Fe³⁺(spm). For Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c (hydrophobic), the catalyst consisted of 74.1% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ-Fe₃C, with no Fe₃O₄ detected.
- **Observations:** The hydrophobic modification leads to a higher proportion of iron carbide phases and suppresses the formation of iron oxides, correlating with improved catalytic selectivity and stability.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Elemental Distribution (TEM and EDS)
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were used to assess the core–shell morphology and elemental distribution of the catalysts.
- **Findings:** TEM images and EDS mapping confirmed that Fe@Mn nanoparticles were uniformly encapsulated by an amorphous SiO₂ shell in both hydrophilic and hydrophobic catalysts. The shell thickness was tunable by varying the TEOS dosage during synthesis. Post-reaction analysis showed that the core–shell structure was preserved.
- **Observations:** The homogeneous and stable core–shell architecture is critical for controlling phase evolution and protecting the active core during reaction.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Hydrophobicity (Contact Angle Measurement)
- **Description:** Water-droplet contact angle measurements were performed to evaluate the surface hydrophobicity of the catalysts.
- **Findings:** Hydrophobic Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalysts exhibited significantly higher water contact angles compared to their hydrophilic counterparts, confirming successful surface modification via silanization.
- **Observations:** Enhanced hydrophobicity restricts water diffusion into the catalyst core, thereby stabilizing the iron carbide phase under reaction conditions.

#### 6.1.5 Reduction and Carburization Behavior (TPR)
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) and carburization (CO-TPR) experiments were conducted to assess the reducibility and carburization characteristics of the catalysts.
- **Findings:** Increasing SiO₂ shell thickness (via higher TEOS dosage) shifted reduction peaks to higher temperatures and decreased peak areas, indicating reduced accessibility of internal iron species to CO and H₂.
- **Observations:** Thicker SiO₂ shells hinder gas diffusion, affecting the reduction and carburization processes and, consequently, the catalyst’s phase evolution during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.6 Elemental Composition (ICP)
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis was used to determine the elemental composition of the catalysts.
- **Findings:** The Fe, Mn, and Si contents were consistent with the intended synthesis ratios, confirming successful incorporation of promoters and shell material.
- **Observations:** Accurate elemental composition supports the reproducibility and reliability of the catalyst synthesis protocol.

#### 6.1.7 Surface Chemistry (FTIR and XPS)
- **Description:** Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were utilized to probe surface functional groups and chemical states.
- **Findings:** FTIR and DRIFTS analyses confirmed the presence of surface silanol and methyl groups after silanization, while XPS detected chlorine residues and provided qualitative information on iron oxidation states.
- **Observations:** Surface chemical modifications are directly linked to hydrophobicity and phase stability under reaction conditions.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction, calculated as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under CO + H₂O conditions at 320 °C and 2.0 MPa (H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst achieved a CO conversion of approximately 30%. In contrast, the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst exhibited a markedly lower CO conversion of less than 2% under identical conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The higher CO conversion in the hydrophilic catalyst is attributed to facile water diffusion, which promotes both CO hydrogenation and the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction. The hydrophobic modification restricts water ingress, thereby suppressing both CO conversion and WGS activity.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the WGS side reaction:
\[
\text{CO₂ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CO₂,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst exhibited a CO₂ selectivity of 28.9%, while the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst showed a significantly reduced value of 7.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The pronounced CO₂ formation in the hydrophilic catalyst confirms substantial WGS activity, whereas the hydrophobic shell effectively suppresses this side reaction.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
\[
\text{CH₄ Selectivity} = \frac{F_{\text{CH₄,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity was 21.5% for Fe@Mn@0.2Si and 14.1% for Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic catalyst demonstrates lower methane selectivity, favoring the formation of longer-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.4 Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Olefin selectivity represents the percentage of olefins among total hydrocarbon products, reflecting the catalyst’s C–C coupling efficiency.
- **Experimental Data:** Olefin selectivity increased from 49.8% (hydrophilic) to 64.3% (hydrophobic).
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced olefin selectivity in the hydrophobic catalyst is linked to the stabilization of iron carbide phases and suppression of secondary hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended reaction times, with deactivation rate inferred from the decline in conversion.
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst maintained stable performance over 110 h of continuous operation, with negligible loss in CO conversion or selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic shell preserves the active FexC phase, minimizing deactivation and ensuring sustained catalytic activity.

#### 6.2.6 Influence of SiO₂ Shell Thickness
- **Description:** The TEOS dosage controls SiO₂ shell thickness, impacting phase evolution and performance.
- **Experimental Data:** Increasing shell thickness (higher TEOS) in hydrophilic catalysts reduced CO conversion (from 53.8% at 0.05 mL TEOS to 24.3% at 2.8 mL) and olefin selectivity (from 66.8% to 27.1%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Thicker shells hinder CO diffusion and favor Fe₃O₄ formation, leading to higher CH₄ selectivity and lower olefin yields.

---

**Summary:**  
Hydrophobic surface modification of Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts markedly enhances olefin selectivity and catalyst stability by preserving the active iron carbide phase and suppressing water-induced oxidation and WGS side reactions. Shell thickness and hydrophobicity are critical parameters for optimizing Fischer–Tropsch performance.
