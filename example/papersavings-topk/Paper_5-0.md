## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis comprises two interconnected procedures: the synthesis of the sodium‐doped ferric carbide (Na‐FeC*x*) and the production of its zeolite promoter. Each method is reported with full experimental details to ensure reproducibility and clarity.

#### • Na‐FeC*x* Synthesis via Precipitation–Deposition and Syngas Treatment

1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: FeCl₃·6H₂O and FeCl₂·4H₂O dissolved in deionized water with HCl.  
   - pH Control: NaOH is added dropwise using a pH meter to achieve a target pH of 10, facilitating the precipitation of iron oxides.  
   - Observable Phenomena: Formation of a distinct precipitate accompanied by a color change indicating precursor formation.

2. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - After thorough mixing, additional NaOH is introduced to foster uniform precipitation; this step is critical before the drying process.

3. Drying  
   - Conditions: Dried at 100 °C overnight to remove residual moisture, preparing the material for subsequent steps.

4. Calcination and Post‑treatment  
   - Conversion: The dried Na‐Fe₃O₄ is treated in a stainless-steel fixed-bed reactor under syngas (CO/H₂, 1:1) at 300 °C for 24 h (gas flow rate: 50 mL/min).  
   - Outcome: XRD confirms the formation of the Fe₅C₂ phase, while ICP verifies a Na loading of ca. 5.35 wt%.

#### • Zeolite Promoter Synthesis via Urea‑Assisted Hydrothermal Method

1. Precursor Preparation  
   - Reagents: Tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS), tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH), urea, and for s‑S1‑OH, an organosilane (DEMS) are mixed in defined proportions.

2. Hydrothermal Treatment  
   - Reactor: Conducted in a Teflon‑lined autoclave.  
   - Conditions: Crystallization at 180 °C for 2–3 days, ensuring proper nanosheet formation with a b‑axis thickness of 90–110 nm.

3. Calcination  
   - Conditions: Final calcination at 550 °C for 4 h to remove organic templates and secure the pure zeolite structure.

Throughout the synthesis, careful monitoring of reagent concentrations, pH, and temperature—as well as equipment such as pH meters and autoclaves—ensures reproducibility and scalability of both the Na‐FeC*x* and zeolite promoter preparations.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation 1: Na‐FeC*x* (via Na‐Fe₃O₄ Precursor)

##### Step 1: Precursor Synthesis – Na‐Fe₃O₄
- **Reagents**:  
  • 30.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O  
  • 11.9 g FeCl₂·4H₂O  
  • 200 mL deionized water  
  • 5.0 mL aqueous HCl (12.0 M)  
  • NaOH aqueous solution (2.0 M, added dropwise)
- **Conditions**:  
  • pH adjusted to 10 under vigorous stirring  
  • Temperature at room temperature during reagent dissolution  
  • Stirring maintained for an additional 1 hour after pH adjustment
- **Process**:
  1. Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O and FeCl₂·4H₂O in deionized water with HCl to ensure complete dissolution.
  2. Under vigorous stirring, add NaOH dropwise until the mixture reaches pH 10 to precipitate Na‐Fe₃O₄.
  3. Continue stirring for 1 hour to ensure homogeneous precursor formation.
  4. Wash the precipitate with deionized water (up to six times) and perform centrifugation to separate the solids.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Monitor pH closely during precipitation and ensure thorough washing to avoid residual impurities.

##### Step 2: Drying and Calcination – Formation of Na‐FeC*x*
- **Reagents & Equipment**:  
  • Dried Na‐Fe₃O₄ precursor; drying oven; syngas (CO/H₂, 1:1)
- **Conditions**:  
  • Dry at 100 °C overnight  
  • Calcine under syngas at 300 °C for 24 hours (flow rate 50 mL/min)
- **Process**:
  1. Dry the Na‐Fe₃O₄ precipitate at 100 °C overnight.
  2. Subject the dried precursor to a syngas atmosphere (1:1 CO:H₂) at 300 °C for 24 hours to convert the oxide precursor into the active carbide phase.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Ensure controlled syngas flow and maintain a consistent temperature profile during conversion.

#### Catalyst Preparation 2: Zeolite Promoter (s‐ZSM‐5 / s‐S1‐OH)

##### Step 1: Precursor Synthesis – MFI Zeolite Nanosheets
- **Reagents**:  
  • 2.8 g tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS)  
  • 4.0 g TPAOH (25% aqueous solution)  
  • 5.1 g deionized water  
  • 0.65 g urea  
  • For s‐S1‐OH, include DEMS as per required proportion
- **Conditions**:  
  • Stir mixture vigorously at room temperature (s‐S1‐OH: additional 12-hour stirring)  
  • Transfer to an autoclave and crystallize at 180 °C (2 days for s‐ZSM‐5; 3 days for s‐S1‐OH)
- **Process**:
  1. Mix all reagents to form a homogeneous precursor solution.
  2. For s‐S1‐OH, ensure extended stirring for 12 hours before autoclaving.
  3. Autoclave the mixture at 180 °C for the designated duration.
  4. Wash the crystallized product, dry in air, and calcine at 550 °C for 4 hours in an air atmosphere.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Careful control of crystallization time and calcination temperature is crucial; monitor nanosheet thickness (90–110 nm).

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly and Activation
- **Process**:
  1. Physically mix the Na‐FeC*x* with the prepared zeolite (s‐ZSM‐5 or s‐S1‐OH), ensuring controlled Na-loading (~5.35 wt%).
  2. Activate the assembled catalyst by pre-reduction under H₂ at 400 °C for 6 hours (flow rate: 10 mL/min).
- **Key Steps & Cautions**:  
  Uniform mixing, precise temperature control, and thorough pre-reduction are essential to achieve reproducible catalytic performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

In the preparation of the Na‐FeC*x* Fischer–Tropsch catalysts, the activation step is essential to transform the initially synthesized Na‐Fe₃O₄ precursor into the active ferric carbide (Fe₅C₂) phase. Two distinct activation treatments are reported that together ensure complete reduction and carburization while preserving catalyst morphology.

#### 5.1.1 Syngas Treatment for Carburization
• • •  
The primary activation is accomplished by exposing the dried Na‐Fe₃O₄ precursor to a syngas atmosphere. This treatment utilizes a CO/H₂ gas mixture in a strict 1:1 molar ratio, which serves a dual role. First, the CO acts as a carbon source for carburization; second, the H₂ provides the reducing environment necessary for the phase transformation. The reaction is carried out at 300 °C under atmospheric pressure, typically maintained for 24 hours. This controlled syngas treatment is responsible for converting the oxide precursor into the dominant Fe₅C₂ phase—an outcome later confirmed by the disappearance of oxide peaks and the emergence of carbide signals in X‐ray diffraction analyses. The precise temperature not only drives the reduction but also ensures uniform transformation, promoting a high dispersion of active sites essential for efficient CO conversion and olefin selectivity.

#### 5.1.2 Pre‐Reduction Under Pure Hydrogen
• • •  
Complementary to the syngas carburization, a pre‐reduction step is performed to further stabilize the catalyst’s active phase. In this procedure, the catalyst is treated under a pure hydrogen flow at 400 °C for 6 hours, again at atmospheric pressure. This step reinforces metal reduction and has been observed to enhance the dispersion of the active sodium‐doped ferric carbide sites without inducing excessive sintering. The combination of these activation procedures ensures reproducible formation of the desired Fe₅C₂ phase, thereby optimizing the catalyst’s performance under Fischer–Tropsch conditions.

Together, these activation parameters are carefully integrated into the catalyst synthesis workflow to achieve a well‐defined active phase, ultimately leading to enhanced CO conversion and high olefin productivity during the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases of the catalysts were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD), which is essential for determining the active phase responsible for Fischer–Tropsch activity.
- **Findings:** XRD analysis of the Na‐FeCₓ catalyst before and after reaction revealed a transformation from ferric oxides (Fe₃O₄ and Fe₂O₃) in the precursor to Fe₅C₂ as the dominant phase after exposure to syngas and catalytic testing. The characteristic diffraction peaks matched those of Fe₅C₂ (PDF #20-0509), with oxide phases becoming nearly undetectable after reaction, confirming complete carbonization.
- **Observations:** The formation of Fe₅C₂ is directly linked to high catalytic activity in low-temperature Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Sodium Distribution
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis quantified elemental composition, while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) assessed surface sodium migration and distribution.
- **Findings:** ICP determined a sodium loading of 5.35 wt% (±3%) in Na‐FeCₓ. XPS revealed distinct Na 1s binding energies: 1071.8 eV for Na on FeCₓ and 1073.3 eV for Na on zeolite. After catalytic testing, the Na 1s peak position indicated the extent of Na migration, with minimal migration in granule-mixed catalysts and extensive migration in powder-mixed systems.
- **Observations:** Uniform sodium and iron distribution was confirmed by STEM-EDS mapping, supporting the catalyst’s compositional homogeneity.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen sorption (BET) measurements provided surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, critical for reactant diffusion and catalyst accessibility.
- **Findings:** For s‐ZSM‐5, the BET surface area was 337.3 m²/g with a pore volume of 0.21 cm³/g (Si/Al = 26.2). The nanosheet zeolite morphology contributed to enhanced porosity. Other zeolites showed surface areas ranging from 388.7 to 542.9 m²/g and pore volumes from 0.13 to 0.27 cm³/g.
- **Observations:** The high surface area and pore volume of the nanosheet zeolites facilitate rapid olefin desorption and improved catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM, TEM, HAADF-STEM) characterized particle morphology, size, and aggregation state.
- **Findings:** SEM and TEM images confirmed that s‐ZSM‐5 and s‐S1‐OH zeolites possess a nanosheet morphology with a b-axis thickness of 90–110 nm and minimal aggregation. Na‐FeCₓ particles were well-dispersed, with clear lattice fringes corresponding to Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The nanosheet structure provides short diffusion paths for olefin escape, correlating with enhanced selectivity and activity.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Chemistry and Olefin Adsorption
- **Description:** DRIFTS/FTIR and temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) studies probed olefin adsorption/desorption behavior on catalyst surfaces.
- **Findings:** After olefin pre-adsorption, IR bands (e.g., =CH₂ at 2986 cm⁻¹) decreased more rapidly on Na‐FeCₓ/s‐ZSM‐5 than on Na‐FeCₓ alone, indicating accelerated olefin desorption. TPD experiments ramped from 40 °C to 700 °C at 10 °C/min confirmed these trends.
- **Observations:** Enhanced olefin desorption from the catalyst surface is attributed to the nanosheet zeolite promoter, which shifts the adsorption–desorption equilibrium and suppresses undesired hydrogenation.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
  where \( F_{\text{CO,in}} \) and \( F_{\text{CO,out}} \) are the inlet and outlet molar flow rates of CO, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under low-temperature Fischer–Tropsch to olefins (LT-FTO) conditions (260 °C, 2.0 MPa, CO/H₂/Ar = 45/45/10, GHSV 2,400 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹_FeCₓ_), the bare Na‐FeCₓ* catalyst exhibits a CO conversion of 1.9%. When physically mixed with s‐ZSM‐5 nanosheets, CO conversion increases dramatically to 82.5%. At 300 °C, the bare Na‐FeCₓ* catalyst achieves 25.6% conversion.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of s‐ZSM‐5 nanosheets as a promoter is critical for high CO conversion at low temperature, far surpassing the performance of the unpromoted catalyst even at elevated temperatures.

#### 6.2.2 Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Olefin selectivity represents the proportion of hydrocarbon products that are olefins (C₂–C₁₀), calculated on a carbon basis:
  \[
  \text{Olefin Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{olefins}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst achieves an olefin selectivity of 72.0% at 260 °C. The s‐S1‐OH variant further enhances α-olefin selectivity to 81.7% (C₄⁺), compared to 52.1% for s‐ZSM‐5.
- **Observations and Trends:** Zeolite morphology and acidity significantly influence olefin selectivity, with nanosheet and siliceous variants favoring α-olefin production and suppressing isomerization.

#### 6.2.3 Methane (CH₄) Selectivity
- **Description:** Methane selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding CH₄:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 at 260 °C, CH₄ selectivity is 2.9–3.6%. In contrast, the bare Na‐FeCₓ* catalyst exhibits higher methane selectivity (10.8% at 5.35 wt% Na).
- **Observations and Trends:** The zeolite promoter effectively suppresses methane formation, favoring olefin production.

#### 6.2.4 C₅–C₁₀ and C₁₀⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₅–C₁₀ selectivity indicates the fraction of converted CO forming hydrocarbons with 5–10 carbons:
  \[
  \text{C}_5\text{–C}_{10} \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_5\text{–C}_{10},\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
  C₁₀⁺ selectivity is similarly defined for C₁₀ and higher hydrocarbons.
- **Experimental Data:** Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 achieves C₅–C₁₀ selectivity of 39.9% and C₁₀⁺ selectivity of 8.2% at 260 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** The product distribution is narrowly centered on C₂–C₁₀ olefins, with minimal formation of long-chain hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.5 Chain Growth Factor (α Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation in the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) model:
  \[
  W_n = (1 - \alpha) \alpha^{n-1}
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with chain length \( n \).
- **Experimental Data:** For Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5, α = 0.62; for comparison, Na‐FeCₓ*/Beta, α = 0.61; Na‐FeCₓ*/ZSM‐22, α = 0.65; Na‐FeCₓ*/MOR, α = 0.67.
- **Observations and Trends:** Moderate α values favor the formation of light olefins, aligning with the observed product distribution.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over time. The deactivation rate is:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time (h)}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** After an initial activation (~50 h), Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 maintains CO conversion at 68.1%, with only a slight decrease to 67.6% after 600 h.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibits excellent long-term stability, with negligible deactivation over extended operation.

#### 6.2.7 Olefin Productivity
- **Description:** Olefin productivity quantifies the amount of olefins produced per gram of catalyst per hour:
  \[
  \text{Olefin Productivity} = \frac{\text{mmol olefins}}{\text{g}_{\text{FeC}_x} \cdot \text{h}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 achieves 15.3 mmol·g⁻¹_FeCₓ_·h⁻¹ at 260 °C.
- **Observations and Trends:** This productivity is substantially higher than that of the unpromoted catalyst, underscoring the promotional effect of the zeolite nanosheets.

---
**Summary:**  
The Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5 catalyst demonstrates outstanding performance under LT-FTO conditions, with high CO conversion, exceptional olefin selectivity, low methane formation, and robust long-term stability. The use of MFI zeolite nanosheets as a physical promoter is pivotal in achieving these results, primarily by facilitating rapid olefin desorption and maintaining active catalytic sites.
