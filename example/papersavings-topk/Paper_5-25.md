## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

• The synthesis of Na‐FeC*x* involves a sequence that ensures reproducibility and precision. Initially, precursor mixing is performed by dissolving 30.0 g FeCl₃·6H₂O and 11.9 g FeCl₂·4H₂O in 200 mL deionized water with 5.0 mL of aqueous HCl. Next, a 2.0 M NaOH solution is added dropwise under constant stirring until the pH reaches 10, achieving homogeneous co‑precipitation of the metal salts.  

• Immediately after co‑precipitation, the precipitation–deposition step is implemented. Here, the solid precipitate is exposed to syngas (CO:H₂, 1:1) to induce the formation of the sodium‑doped ferric carbide phase (dominated by Fe₅C₂). This critical step is positioned after precursor mixing and before drying to ensure uniform deposition and proper phase conversion.

• The solid is then dried at 100°C overnight to remove residual moisture. Subsequent calcination is conducted at 450°C for 5 hours to stabilize the catalyst structure. In some protocols, a pre‑reduction at 400°C for 6 hours in pure H₂ is performed, followed by syngas treatment at 300°C for 24 hours to further activate the Na‐FeC*x* phase.

• For the zeolite component, two methods are used. The conventional hydrothermal method for s‑ZSM‑5 involves preparing a precursor gel (typically containing TEOS and TPAOH in deionized water) and crystallizing it in a Teflon‑lined autoclave at 180°C for 3 days. For s‑S1‑OH, diethoxydimethylsilane (DEMS) is incorporated into the precursor gel to introduce silanol groups, yielding nanosheets with a controlled b‑axis thickness of 90–110 nm. Post‑synthesis, the zeolite is washed, dried, and calcined at 550°C for 4 hours.

• Finally, granule‑mixing of the pre‑synthesized Na‐FeC*x* with the zeolite promoter is performed at a zeolite-to‑Na‐FeC*x* mass ratio of 3, ensuring optimal catalyst performance and product selectivity.

### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** The crystalline phases of the Na‐FeCₓ/zeolite catalysts and their supports were identified using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). XRD is essential for confirming the formation of the active Fe₅C₂ phase and verifying the integrity of the MFI zeolite framework.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns of the Na‐FeCₓ catalyst after LT-FTO reaction display sole signals corresponding to Fe₅C₂ (PDF #20-0509), with negligible oxide phases detected, confirming complete transformation to the active carbide. Both s‐ZSM‐5 and s‐S1‐OH zeolite supports exhibit characteristic reflections of the MFI structure (PDF #42-0024), indicating preserved crystallinity and framework integrity after synthesis and catalyst preparation.
- **Observations:** The dominance of Fe₅C₂ in the active phase is directly linked to high Fischer–Tropsch activity, while the retention of the MFI structure in the zeolite supports ensures optimal diffusion properties.

#### 6.1.2 Elemental Composition and Valence States
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze surface elemental composition and oxidation states, with particular attention to Fe and Na species. The Fe 2p₃/₂ region is used to distinguish between Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺, and FeCₓ, while the Na 1s peak monitors sodium migration between catalyst components.
- **Findings:** XPS spectra confirm the presence of Fe in multiple oxidation states, with the Fe 2p₃/₂ region analyzed for Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺, and FeCₓ species. The Na 1s peak appears at 1071.8 eV for Na on FeCₓ and at 1073.3 eV for Na on zeolite, enabling tracking of sodium migration in granule- and powder-mixed samples.
- **Observations:** The detection of FeCₓ species corroborates the formation of the active carbide phase. XPS analysis also reveals that the mixing method influences sodium distribution, which is critical for catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen sorption isotherms were measured using the BET method to determine surface area and pore volume of the zeolite supports, which are key for reactant diffusion and catalyst dispersion.
- **Findings:** The s‐ZSM‐5 zeolite exhibits a BET surface area of 337.3 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.21 cm³/g. For comparison, n‐ZSM‐5 shows 388.7 m²/g and 0.13 cm³/g, respectively. The s‐S1‐OH zeolite also demonstrates high surface area and porosity, supporting efficient mass transport.
- **Observations:** The large surface area and pore volume of the zeolite nanosheets facilitate rapid olefin diffusion, suppressing secondary reactions and enhancing selectivity.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM/HAADF-STEM) were used to assess catalyst morphology, particle size, and elemental distribution.
- **Findings:** SEM images show that both Na‐FeCₓ and zeolite granules retain uniform particle sizes (20–40 mesh) and distinct morphologies after mixing. TEM/HAADF-STEM with EDS mapping confirms homogeneous distribution of Fe and Na within the catalyst granules.
- **Observations:** The uniform morphology and controlled nanosheet thickness (90–110 nm for s‐ZSM‐5) are indicative of successful synthesis and mixing, contributing to catalyst stability and performance.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Acidity
- **Description:** NH₃-temperature programmed desorption (NH₃-TPD) was conducted to evaluate the presence of acid sites in the zeolite supports, which can influence olefin isomerization and selectivity.
- **Findings:** s‐ZSM‐5 displays NH₃ desorption peaks at 200 °C (weak acid sites) and 440 °C (strong acid sites), confirming the presence of acidity. In contrast, s‐S1‐OH shows almost undetectable NH₃ desorption, indicating the absence of Brønsted acid sites and the successful introduction of silanol groups.
- **Observations:** The lack of acidity in s‐S1‐OH is crucial for suppressing undesired olefin isomerization, thereby improving selectivity in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.6 Olefin Adsorption–Desorption Properties
- **Description:** DRIFT spectroscopy was used to study the adsorption–desorption kinetics of olefins (e.g., ethene) on the catalyst surface, providing insight into product desorption and catalyst selectivity.
- **Findings:** DRIFT experiments, conducted after Ar pre-treatment at 400 °C and ethene adsorption at 30 °C, reveal rapid desorption of olefins from the zeolite surface, particularly for s‐S1‐OH.
- **Observations:** Fast olefin desorption from the zeolite nanosheets supports high olefin selectivity by minimizing secondary hydrogenation and isomerization.




### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Bare Na‐FeCₓ* catalyst: 1.9% at 260 °C, 2.0 MPa, GHSV 2,400 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹, CO/H₂/Ar 45/45/10.
  - Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5: up to 82.5% under identical conditions.
  - Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐S1‐OH: 82.6% at 260 °C, 2.0 MPa, GHSV 2,400 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹, H₂/CO ratio of 3, granule-mixed.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Incorporation of MFI zeolite nanosheets as promoters dramatically enhances CO conversion (over 40-fold increase), with both s‐ZSM‐5 and s‐S1‐OH variants achieving high conversion under low-temperature Fischer–Tropsch conditions.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO forming CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift reaction:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - CO₂ selectivity typically exceeds 45% for all Na‐FeCₓ*/zeolite catalysts.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The high CO₂ selectivity is characteristic of Fe-based Fischer–Tropsch to olefins (FTO) systems. Zeolite addition does not significantly reduce CO₂ formation, as the primary role of the zeolite is to modulate hydrocarbon selectivity rather than suppress the water-gas shift reaction.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐S1‐OH: 6.0% under optimized conditions (H₂/CO = 3).
  - Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐ZSM‐5: as low as 3.0–3.8% during steady-state operation.
  - Powder-mixed catalysts: up to 18.8% (with excessive Na migration).
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Zeolite-promoted catalysts, especially with granule mixing, exhibit significantly reduced methane selectivity compared to bare or powder-mixed systems, highlighting the importance of catalyst architecture in suppressing undesired methane formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₁₀⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** C₁₀⁺ selectivity indicates the fraction of products with carbon numbers greater than 10, reflecting the breadth of the hydrocarbon distribution:
  \[
  \text{C}_{10+} \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{C}_{10+},\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐S1‐OH: 6.3% under standard conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Both zeolite-promoted catalysts maintain a narrow product distribution dominated by C₁–C₁₀ hydrocarbons, with low C₁₀⁺ selectivity, which is advantageous for targeted olefin production.

#### 6.2.5 Olefin/Alkane (o/a) Ratio and Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** The o/a ratio is the molar ratio of olefins to alkanes in the product stream, serving as a direct indicator of olefin selectivity.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Na‐FeCₓ*/s‐S1‐OH: o/a ratio of 3.8 at H₂/CO = 3.
  - Long-term (600 h) operation: o/a ratio of 4.2–5.2; C₂–C₁₀ olefin selectivity of 67.8–71.0%.
- **Observations and Trends:**

- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion and selectivity over prolonged operation (up to 600 h).

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The catalysts demonstrate excellent durability, with negligible deactivation and consistent product selectivity, underscoring their suitability for long-term Fischer–Tropsch to olefins applications.
