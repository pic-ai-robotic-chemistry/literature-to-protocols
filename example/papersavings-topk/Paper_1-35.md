## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the methods used for preparing Fe@Mn@xSi (hydrophilic) and Fe@Mn@xSi-c (hydrophobic) Fischer–Tropsch catalysts. Two primary synthesis routes are reported: the hydrothermal method for forming Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles followed by manganese deposition, and the subsequent coating and hydrophobic modification via a modified Stöber and surface silanization process. A comparative analysis shows that while the hydrothermal approach yields well-crystallized Fe₂O₃ with controlled morphology, the deposition–precipitation and Stöber strategies offer scalability and tunable shell thickness. The advantages include precise control over phase transitions and uniform Mn promotion, whereas the challenges lie in the added complexity and potential for chlorine residues during silanization.

#### • Hydrothermal Synthesis and Fe₂O₃ Formation
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: FeCl₃·6H₂O (7.5 g), sodium acetate (12.4 g), hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (3.8 g), ethylenediamine (26 mL)  
   - Mix with stirring (0.5 h for sodium acetate and surfactant, then 1 h after adding ethylenediamine).  
2. Hydrothermal Treatment  
   - Reactor: Teflon‑lined autoclave  
   - Conditions: 200 °C for 10 h  
3. Post‑processing  
   - Washing: Water and ethanol  
   - Drying: 100 °C for 12 h  
   - Calcination: 500 °C for 5 h

#### • Mn Promotion via Deposition–Precipitation
1. Precipitation–Deposition (inserted after precursor processing)
   - Disperse 0.5 g of Fe₂O₃ in 300 mL ethanol.  
   - Reagent addition: 5 mL KMnO₄ solution added dropwise under stirring at 30 °C for 2 h.  
   - Post‑processing: Centrifugation, washing with water, drying at 100 °C for 12 h

#### • SiO₂ Coating and Hydrophobic Modification
1. Modified Stöber Method  
   - Disperse Fe@Mn in 150 mL ethanol; add TEOS (0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL to modulate shell thickness)  
   - After 3 h stirring at 30 °C, add 2.5 mL ammonia solution and 10 mL water; stir for an additional 4 h  
2. Surface Silanization  
   - Treat the dried SiO₂‑coated catalyst with chlorotrimethylsilane in n‑hexane to prepare the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@xSi-c variants, noting that residual chlorine can affect catalytic performance

This structured methodology, with clearly defined numerical values and equipment parameters, ensures accuracy and reproducibility in synthesizing catalysts for Fischer–Tropsch reactions.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

This section details the preparation procedures for both the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@xSi catalyst and the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalyst. The procedures are organized by weighing solid reagents, transferring liquid reagents, and mixing to obtain homogeneous mixtures.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 Fe@Mn@xSi
- **FeCl₃·6H₂O**: Weighed 7.5 g using an analytical balance.
- **Sodium acetate trihydrate (CH₃COONa·3H₂O)**: Weighed 12.4 g.
- **Hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (C₁₉H₄₂BrN)**: Weighed 3.8 g.

##### 3.1.1.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c
- The same solid reagents as for the hydrophilic catalyst are weighed with identical quantities for initial precursor formation.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 Fe@Mn@xSi
- **Deionized water**: Poured 10 mL into the reaction solution to facilitate precursor dissolution.
- **Ethanol**: Poured 150 mL into the dispersion vessel for effective mixing during the deposition–precipitation of the manganese promoter.
- **Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS)**: Added dropwise in volumes of 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.8 mL using a micropipette to control the SiO₂ shell thickness.

##### 3.1.2.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c
- **n-Hexane**: Transferred 70 mL into the reaction vessel.
- **Chlorotrimethylsilane (C₃H₉ClSi)**: Poured 0.5 mL into the n-hexane solution prior to surface silanization.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 Fe@Mn@xSi
- **Fe₂O₃-based precursor dispersion**: Ultrasonically treated for 1 hour at room temperature to ensure uniform dispersion of the iron-based nanoparticles.
- **TEOS-based coating mixture**: After addition of TEOS, the suspension is stirred at 30 °C for 3 hours to promote hydrolysis. Following this, ammonia solution and water are introduced, and the mixture is further stirred at 30 °C for 4 hours to complete the formation of the SiO₂ shell.
- **Manganese promoter deposition**: With a dropwise addition of a KMnO₄ solution, the precursor is stirred at 30 °C for 2 hours to ensure homogeneous promoter distribution.

##### 3.1.3.2 Fe@Mn@xSi-c
- **Surface silanization mixture**: The dried Fe@Mn@xSi catalyst is combined with the n-hexane/chlorotrimethylsilane solution and then ultrasonically treated for 1 hour at room temperature, ensuring complete surface modification and the development of hydrophobic properties.

These detailed procedures, with precise reagent quantities and controlled transfer and mixing parameters, ensure full reproducibility in the preparation of the Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Mn@xSi (Hydrophilic)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation – Hydrothermal Synthesis of Fe₂O₃  
- **Reagents**: 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 12.4 g sodium acetate trihydrate, 3.8 g hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide, 26 mL ethylenediamine, and 110 mL deionized water.  
- **Conditions**: Stirring at room temperature initially (0.5 h), then hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 10 h in a hydrothermal reactor.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O in deionized water; add sodium acetate, surfactant, and stir for 0.5 h.  
  - Introduce ethylenediamine and stir for an additional 1 h to ensure homogeneity.  
  - Transfer the solution to a hydrothermal reactor and treat at 200 °C for 10 h.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete dissolution and strict temperature control during hydrothermal treatment.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly – Manganese Deposition and SiO₂ Coating  
- **Reagents (Mn Deposition)**: 0.5 g synthesized Fe₂O₃ dispersed in 300 mL ethanol; 5 mL aqueous solution containing 0.078 g KMnO₄.  
- **Conditions**: Dropwise addition using a peristaltic pump at 30 °C under stirring for 2 h.  
- **Process**:  
  - Disperse Fe₂O₃ in ethanol.  
  - Add KMnO₄ solution dropwise while stirring at 30 °C to deposit manganese.  
- **Reagents (SiO₂ Coating)**: Varying volumes of TEOS (x = 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.8 mL), ammonia solution, and water added to 150 mL ethanol-dispersed Fe@Mn.  
- **Conditions**: Stir at 30 °C; TEOS addition stirred for 3 h followed by 4 h after ammonia addition.  
- **Process**:  
  - Combine and stir to promote controlled hydrolysis of TEOS, forming a SiO₂ shell around the catalyst.  
- **Key Steps**: Monitor TEOS volume to balance shell thickness and CO diffusion.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination  
- **Process**:  
  - Recover the catalyst via centrifugation and wash with water.  
  - Dry the product at 100 °C for 12 h in a vacuum oven.  
  - Calcine at 500 °C for 5 h (ramp rate controlled) to complete phase stabilization.  

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe@Mn@xSi-c (Hydrophobic)

##### Step 4: Surface Silanization  
- **Reagents**: 0.5 mL chlorotrimethylsilane in n-hexane (post drying).  
- **Conditions**: Ultrasound treatment at room temperature for 1 h; subsequent centrifugation, wash with n-hexane, then dry at 80 °C for 6 h.  
- **Process**:  
  - After drying the hydrophilic catalyst, disperse in n-hexane, add chlorotrimethylsilane and sonicate to promote hydrophobic modification.  
- **Key Steps & Cautions**:  
  - Monitor for chlorine residue formation, which may impair CO conversion.  

This systematic workflow—from primary precursor synthesis through catalyst assembly, purification, and modification—ensures reproducibility while emphasizing temperature control, reagent drop rates, and critical pH adjustments via ammonia addition.

- **Operational Description**: A 50 mg sample is pretreated in N₂ at 350 °C for 1 hour, cooled to 80 °C, then exposed to 5% H₂/95% N₂. The temperature is ramped from 80 °C to 800 °C at 10 °C/min while a TCD monitors H₂ consumption.
- **Instruments and Models**: Micro fixed‑bed reactor coupled with a thermal conductivity detector.
- **Characterization Conditions**: Gas flow rate of 30 mL/min; temperature ramp as described.
- **Test Parameters**: Precise control of sample loading and pretreatment ensures reproducibility.
- **Key Notes**: Uniform catalyst packing is critical to obtain accurate reduction profiles.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** Temperature during activation is critical for reducing iron oxides to lower oxidation states and facilitating the subsequent carburization into active iron carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is first reduced in pure H₂ at 350 °C for 20 hours, followed by an activation step in syngas at 320 °C for 5 hours.
- **Observations:** The 350 °C reduction provides sufficient thermal energy for transformation without excessive sintering, while the syngas treatment at 320 °C promotes effective carburization toward the desired active phases.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Pressure impacts the kinetics of reduction and the effective penetration of reactive gases into the catalyst bed.
- **Experimental Conditions:** Both the reduction and syngas activation steps are conducted at a pressure of 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** Maintaining a low pressure ensures controlled reduction kinetics, minimizes unwanted gas-phase interactions, and reduces the risk of catalyst agglomeration during activation.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The gas composition used during the activation process determines the reduction environment and the extent of carburization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The initial reduction is carried out using pure H₂, ensuring complete conversion of Fe₂O₃ to lower oxidation states. Subsequently, a syngas mixture with an H₂/CO ratio of 2 is used.
- **Observations:** Pure hydrogen facilitates effective reduction, while the syngas environment introduces CO necessary for carburization, leading to the formation of active iron carbide phases.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity controls the contact time between catalyst particles and the activating gases, influencing activation efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is performed under a gas flow rate of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g_cat⁻¹ in a stainless fixed-bed reactor.
- **Observations:** This high flow rate ensures uniform gas distribution, enhancing reduction and carburization while preventing over-reduction.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of each activation step is vital for achieving complete phase transformation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A total activation time of 25 hours is applied (20 hours under pure H₂ followed by 5 hours under syngas).
- **Observations:** This sequential activation protocol ensures that the Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles are entirely converted into the active iron carbide phases, with prolonged activation beyond these times not significantly altering the phase composition.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
The reaction temperature is defined as the specific temperature required for the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis to activate syngas conversion and promote the formation of the active iron carbide phase. In this study, the reaction was conducted at 320 °C within a stainless fixed-bed reactor. This temperature facilitates the conversion of Fe₂O₃ to the active phase (χ-Fe₅C₂) through effective carburization by CO. Stable CO conversion and consistent hydrocarbon product distributions over extended reaction times (with observations reported up to 110 hours) underscore the adequacy of this temperature setting for achieving desired catalyst performance.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
Reaction pressure plays a crucial role by enhancing the solubility and concentration of syngas within the catalytic bed, thereby influencing both the reaction kinetics and selectivity of hydrocarbon products. The experimental conditions specified a reaction pressure of 2.0 MPa. This elevated pressure not only supports enhanced CO conversion but also favors the formation of long-chain olefins. The controlled pressure condition is particularly important in maintaining the stability of the active phases and ensuring reproducible performance during continuous operation.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
The H₂/CO ratio is a critical experimental parameter that determines the extent of hydrogenation during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. A fixed ratio of 2 was maintained throughout all catalytic tests. This ratio is instrumental in balancing the rates of CO hydrogenation and hydrocarbon chain growth. Notably, while a higher H₂/CO ratio can lead to increased methane formation, maintaining the ratio at 2 has been shown to optimize the formation of long-chain olefins while minimizing excessive hydrogenation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) quantifies the ratio of the syngas flow rate relative to the catalyst mass, directly affecting the contact time between the reactants and the active catalytic sites. The experiments were performed at a GHSV of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹. This parameter was selected to ensure sufficient residence time for achieving optimal CO conversion while simultaneously preserving catalyst stability and product selectivity, as excessive rates could lead to incomplete conversion and a shift toward lower carbon number hydrocarbons.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and transformations of the catalysts were monitored using X-ray diffraction (XRD), including in situ XRD under reactive atmospheres, to track the evolution from Fe₂O₃ to iron carbide (χ-Fe₅C₂, θ-Fe₃C) and Fe₃O₄.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns revealed that, after Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst exhibited a mixture of Fe₅C₂ and Fe₃O₄ phases, indicating partial oxidation of the active carbide. In contrast, the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst maintained a stable iron carbide phase, with Mössbauer spectroscopy quantifying 74.1% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ-Fe₃C. In situ XRD under CO + H₂O atmosphere showed that hydrophilic catalysts underwent oxidation of iron carbide to Fe₃O₄, while hydrophobic catalysts preserved the iron carbide phase, demonstrating the protective effect of surface hydrophobization.
- **Observations:** The presence and stability of iron carbide phases are directly linked to enhanced olefin selectivity and catalyst durability. Increased SiO₂ shell thickness, as controlled by TEOS dosage, inhibited carburization and favored Fe₃O₄ formation.

#### 6.1.2 Reduction and Carburization Behavior (TPR Analysis)
- **Description:** H₂–TPR and CO–TPR analyses were conducted to evaluate the reducibility and carburization behavior of the catalysts, with a focus on the influence of SiO₂ shell thickness.
- **Findings:** Thicker SiO₂ shells, resulting from higher TEOS dosages, shifted CO–TPR reduction/carburization peaks to higher temperatures and reduced their area, indicating hindered accessibility of CO to internal iron species. This led to decreased iron carbide content and increased Fe₃O₄ after reaction.
- **Observations:** Optimal shell thickness is critical; excessive thickness impedes carburization and reduces catalytic activity, while insufficient thickness fails to protect the active phase from oxidation.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Composition and Elemental Analysis (XPS, ICP)
- **Description:** Surface elemental composition was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), with bulk composition determined by ICP.
- **Findings:** XPS detected chlorine residues on Fe@Mn@0.05Si-c catalysts due to silanization, which correlated with inhibited iron carbonization and poor catalytic performance. No chlorine was detected on Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c, indicating effective prevention of contamination with appropriate shell thickness. ICP confirmed the intended elemental ratios.
- **Observations:** Chlorine contamination from hydrophobic modification can poison the catalyst, emphasizing the need for controlled shell thickness to prevent surface exposure.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Core–Shell Structure (TEM, EDS)
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were used to assess morphology and confirm SiO₂ encapsulation.
- **Findings:** TEM images and EDS mapping demonstrated homogeneous, amorphous SiO₂ shells encapsulating the Fe@Mn core. The core of Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c was observed to shrink after reaction, reflecting phase evolution.
- **Observations:** Uniform encapsulation is essential for phase stability and performance; shell thickness directly correlates with TEOS dosage.

#### 6.1.5 Surface Hydrophobicity (Contact Angle)
- **Description:** Hydrophobicity was evaluated by water droplet contact angle measurements using a Kruss DSA100 goniometer.
- **Findings:** Hydrophobic catalysts (Fe@Mn@xSi-c) exhibited increased water contact angles, confirming successful surface modification.
- **Observations:** Enhanced hydrophobicity restricts water diffusion, protecting the iron carbide phase from oxidation during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Chemistry and CO Adsorption (DRIFTS/FTIR)
- **Description:** In situ DRIFTS/FTIR was employed to study CO adsorption and surface carbonate formation.
- **Findings:** Hydrophilic catalysts showed strong CO adsorption and persistent carbonate species, while hydrophobic or chlorine-containing catalysts exhibited only gaseous CO bands, indicating inhibited CO adsorption.
- **Observations:** Surface chemistry is strongly influenced by hydrophobic modification and chlorine contamination, impacting redox activity and catalytic behavior.

#### 6.1.7 Phase Quantification (Mössbauer Spectroscopy)
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the relative amounts of iron carbide and oxide phases in spent catalysts.
- **Findings:** For Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c after FTS, 74.1% χ-Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ-Fe₃C were detected, confirming the dominance of active carbide phases.
- **Observations:** The quantification supports the XRD findings and correlates with improved catalytic selectivity and stability.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated on a carbon-atom basis as:
\[
\text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (320 °C, 2.0 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst achieves a CO conversion of approximately 30% in the CO + H₂O model. For the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst, CO conversion remains below 2% in the model test but is significantly enhanced during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis due to stabilization of the iron carbide phase. Varying the SiO₂ shell thickness (TEOS dosage from 0.05 to 2.8 mL) results in a decrease in CO conversion from 53.8% (thinnest shell) to 24.3% (thickest shell).
- **Observations and Trends:** Thicker SiO₂ shells inhibit CO diffusion and carburization, reducing CO conversion. Hydrophobic modification stabilizes the active phase, indirectly supporting higher CO conversion to desired hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift (WGS) side reaction:
\[
\text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst exhibits a CO₂ selectivity of 28.9%. In contrast, the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst shows a markedly lower CO₂ selectivity of 7.9%. Increasing SiO₂ shell thickness in hydrophilic catalysts maintains high CO₂ selectivity (up to ~40%).
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic modification suppresses water adsorption and the WGS reaction, thereby reducing CO₂ formation.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
\[
\text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
\]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity is 21.5% for Fe@Mn@0.2Si and decreases to 14.1% for Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c.
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic surface modification reduces methane formation, favoring longer-chain hydrocarbon production.

#### 6.2.4 Olefin Selectivity
- **Description:** Olefin selectivity represents the fraction of hydrocarbon products that are olefins, a key indicator of chain growth and product value.
- **Experimental Data:** Olefin selectivity increases from 49.8% (Fe@Mn@0.2Si) to 64.3% (Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c). For hydrophilic catalysts, increasing SiO₂ shell thickness reduces olefin selectivity from 66.8% (thinnest shell) to 27.1% (thickest shell).
- **Observations and Trends:** Hydrophobic modification and optimal shell thickness enhance olefin selectivity, while excessive shell thickness or chlorine contamination suppresses it.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution, quantifies the probability of chain propagation versus termination (0 < α < 1). Higher α values indicate a greater tendency for long-chain hydrocarbon formation.
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not numerically reported, the increase in olefin selectivity and decrease in methane selectivity for Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c indicate a higher α factor compared to Fe@Mn@0.2Si.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced α factor in hydrophobic catalysts correlates with improved long-chain olefin production.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** The deactivation rate is defined as the decline in CO conversion per hour:
\[
\text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Reaction Time (h)}}
\]
- **Experimental Data:** The Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst demonstrates excellent stability, maintaining CO conversion with negligible deactivation over 110 h on stream.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic SiO₂ shell effectively protects the iron carbide phase from oxidation, resulting in sustained catalytic activity and minimal deactivation.

#### 6.2.7 Effect of Chlorine Contamination
- **Description:** Residual chlorine from silanization can poison the catalyst, inhibiting iron carburization and CO adsorption.
- **Experimental Data:** Catalysts with chlorine contamination (e.g., Fe@Mn@0.05Si-c) exhibit extremely low CO conversion (<2%) and rapid deactivation.
- **Observations and Trends:** Proper removal of chlorine and optimization of shell thickness are critical for achieving high performance and stability.
