## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section summarizes the step‐by‐step procedures for synthesizing Fe@Mn@xSi catalysts, structured to ensure reproducibility and accurate replication.

#### • Hydrothermal Synthesis of Fe₂O₃ Nanoparticles  
1. Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 110 mL water; 12.4 g sodium acetate trihydrate; 3.8 g hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide.  
   - Procedure: Dissolve and stir for 0.5 h, then add 26 mL ethylenediamine; stir for another hour.
2. Hydrothermal Treatment  
   - Equipment: Teflon‑lined autoclave.  
   - Conditions: 200 °C for 10 h.  
3. Post‑Hydrothermal Processing  
   - Actions: Wash with water and ethanol, dry at 100 °C for 12 h, and calcine at 500 °C for 5 h.

#### • Deposition–Precipitation of the Mn Promoter  
1. Precipitation–Deposition (Inserted immediately after mixing):  
   - Process: Disperse 0.5 g of the synthesized Fe₂O₃ in 300 mL ethanol via ultrasonication.  
   - Add aqueous potassium permanganate solution dropwise using a peristaltic pump while stirring at 30 °C for 2 h.  
   - Equipment: Peristaltic pump for controlled addition and centrifuge for product collection.

#### • Formation of the SiO₂ Shell Via Controlled TEOS Hydrolysis  
1. TEOS Addition  
   - Reagent: Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) is added in specific volumes (x = 0.05, 0.2, or 2.8) to determine shell thickness.  
   - Conditions: Controlled hydrolysis with appropriate ammonia and water ratios.
2. Drying and Calcination  
   - Hydrophilic catalysts: Follow drying at 100 °C followed by calcination at 500 °C.  
   - Hydrophobic modification: Post‑calcination, subject the catalyst to surface silanization using chlorotrimethylsilane (evidenced by residual chlorine) to yield Fe@Mn@xSi‑c.

#### • Reduction and Activation  
- Reduction: Conduct in pure H₂ at 350 °C and 0.1 MPa for 20 h.  
- Further treatments (e.g., washing, drying) ensure removal of impurities and stabilization of the iron carbide phases.

This structured workflow, with clearly numbered steps and precise experimental parameters (temperatures, durations, volumes), guarantees accurate replicability and comparative analysis of synthesis complexity, scalability, and overall catalyst performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: Fe@Mn@xSi

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: 7.5 g FeCl₃·6H₂O, 12.4 g CH₃COONa·3H₂O, 3.8 g CTAB (C₁₉H₄₂BrN), and 110 mL deionized water.
- **Conditions**: Hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 10 hours (pH control via intrinsic reaction conditions).
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve FeCl₃·6H₂O, CH₃COONa·3H₂O, and CTAB in deionized water.
  - Transfer the solution to a hydrothermal reactor and heat at 200 °C for 10 hours.
  - Recover Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles, then disperse 0.5 g in 300 mL ethanol using ultrasonication.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure uniform dispersion prior to Mn deposition.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly (Mn Deposition & SiO₂ Shell Formation)
- **Reagents**: 0.078 g KMnO₄ in 5 mL aqueous solution (added dropwise using a peristaltic pump), TEOS (x mL, where x = 0.05, 0.2, or 2.8), 2.5 mL ammonia solution, and 10 mL water.
- **Conditions**: Stir at 30 °C; deposition for 2 hours followed by TEOS hydrolysis (3 hours with TEOS, then 4 hours after ammonia addition).
- **Process**:
  - Add the KMnO₄ solution dropwise to the Fe₂O₃ suspension under continuous stirring at 30 °C.
  - Introduce TEOS into the dispersion; continue stirring for 3 hours.
  - Add ammonia solution and water to initiate controlled hydrolysis, then stir for additional 4 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Controlled dropwise addition and strict stirring maintain uniform Mn deposition and consistent SiO₂ shell formation.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Centrifuge the product and wash sequentially with water and ethanol.
  - No specific centrifugation speeds provided; standard laboratory protocols apply.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete removal of unreacted species and by-products.

##### Step 4: Drying & Calcination
- **Drying**: Dry in a vacuum oven at 100 °C for 12 hours.
- **Calcination**: Heat to 500 °C for 5 hours (ramp rate controlled by furnace settings, in air).
- **Key Steps**: Avoid aggregation and ensure complete phase conversion.

##### Step 5: Activation
- **Process**:
  - Reduce the catalyst in pure H₂ at 350 °C and 0.1 MPa for 20 hours.
  - Subsequently, activate under syngas (H₂/CO = 2) at 320 °C, 2.0 MPa with a GHSV of 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹ for 24 hours.
- **Key Steps**: Precise temperature and pressure control are critical to achieve an optimal active phase (predominantly χ‑Fe₅C₂).

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Fe@Mn@xSi-c (Hydrophobic Variant)

- Follow the same procedures as for the hydrophilic catalyst.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)
• **Operational Description**: The catalyst powder is first transformed into iron carbide by reduction in pure H₂ at 350 °C for 20 hours followed by carbonization in a 31.6% CO/68.4% N₂ atmosphere at 320 °C for 20 hours. The prepared sample is then packed into a ceramic stage and placed in the reactor chamber where it is exposed to a CO + H₂O atmosphere at 320 °C. Continuous XRD patterns are collected.  
• **Instruments and Models**: Ultima IV‑185 diffractometer with HT‑XRD‑900 reactor chamber (Beijing Scistar Technology Co., Ltd.).  
• **Characterization Conditions**: 2θ range from 29° to 79°; gas flow of 20 mL/min for CO/N₂ plus water vapor from a 40 °C wash bottle.  
• **Test Parameters**: Scanning step of 0.02°; scanning rate of 5°/min.  
• **Key Notes**: Sample mounting without tablet preparation ensures proper alignment.

### 4.2 H₂ Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (H₂‑TPR)
• **Operational Description**: Fifty milligrams of catalyst is loaded into a quartz tube, pretreated with N₂ at 350 °C for 1 hour, cooled to 80 °C, then exposed to 5% H₂/95% N₂ at 30 mL/min. The temperature is ramped to 800 °C at 10 °C/min while H₂ consumption is monitored using a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).  
• **Key Notes**: Precise temperature and flow control are critical for accurate reduction profiling.

### 4.3 CO Temperature‑Programmed Reduction (CO‑TPR)
• **Operational Description**: Similar to H₂‑TPR, 50 mg of sample is pretreated with N₂ at 350 °C for 1 hour, cooled to 80 °C, and then subjected to 5% CO/95% N₂ at 30 mL/min, with a temperature ramp of 10 °C/min up to 800 °C.  
• **Key Notes**: This method reveals carburization behavior under controlled CO atmosphere.

### 4.4 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)
• **Operational Description**: Powder samples are loaded into an IR cell, pretreated in Ar at 320 °C for 120 minutes, and the background spectrum is collected. The sample is then exposed to CO at 320 °C for 120 minutes.  
• **Instruments and Models**: Nicolet iS50 FTIR and NICOLET 5700 FTIR Spectrometers.

### 4.5 Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (DRIFTS)
• **Operational Description**: The sample is pretreated in Ar (30 mL/min) at 320 °C for 120 minutes (heating rate: 5 °C/min) and then exposed to CO (30 mL/min) at the same temperature for another 120 minutes.  
• **Instruments and Models**: NICOLET iS50 FTIR Spectrometer.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is critical to convert the reduced iron species into the active carbide phases required for efficient Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. Following the initial reduction in pure hydrogen (350 °C, 0.1 MPa for 20 hours) that transforms Fe₂O₃ into metallic iron, a controlled activation/carbonization step is performed under syngas conditions.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature governs the carburization reaction whereby reduced iron reacts with CO to form stable iron carbide phases.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is exposed to a syngas environment at 320 °C.
- **Observations:** Operating at 320 °C is high enough to promote efficient carburization into phases such as χ‑Fe₅C₂ without inducing excessive sintering or unwanted reactions.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure significantly influences gas-catalyst interactions and the kinetics of the reduction and carburization reactions.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The pre-reaction activation is conducted at 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** A pressure of 0.1 MPa ensures controlled exposure to syngas, facilitating the desired conversion while avoiding over-reduction or catalyst agglomeration. Note that while reaction testing later may involve higher pressures (e.g., 2.0 MPa), the activation step focuses strictly on catalyst pre-conditioning.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas Composition
- **Definition:** The syngas mixture supplies the necessary CO for carburization in addition to hydrogen for stabilization.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The syngas employed has an H₂/CO ratio of 2.
- **Observations:** This composition is pivotal in converting the metallic iron into the active carbide phase, as the CO provides the carbon needed to form χ‑Fe₅C₂, while hydrogen assists in maintaining a reducing environment.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is optimized to ensure uniform phase transformation without causing sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The activation is maintained for 5 hours.
- **Observations:** This time frame has been found sufficient to achieve full carburization and stabilize the active catalyst phases, directly enhancing subsequent catalytic performance.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature is a critical parameter in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, influencing the activation of syngas and the formation of the active iron carbide phase.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reactor was maintained at 320 °C throughout the catalytic evaluation.
- **Observations:** This temperature was selected to optimize catalyst performance, promoting effective C–C coupling while ensuring the stability of the active iron phases.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure
- **Definition:** Reaction pressure directly affects the concentration of syngas in the reactor, which in turn influences conversion rates and product selectivity.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalytic tests were conducted at a pressure of 2.0 MPa.
- **Observations:** Operating at 2.0 MPa was found to enhance syngas conversion, thereby contributing favorably to the overall formation of desired hydrocarbon products.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio
- **Definition:** The ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the feed is pivotal for controlling the distribution of hydrocarbon products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 2 was consistently applied during the reaction.
- **Observations:** This balanced ratio effectively maintained the equilibrium necessary for optimum hydrocarbon distribution, promoting an increase in olefins selectivity while minimizing methane production.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity
- **Definition:** The space-time velocity, expressed as the gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), defines the flow rate of syngas relative to the catalyst mass and impacts contact time and conversion efficiency.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The GHSV was set to 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹.
- **Observations:** This setting ensured adequate exposure of the catalyst to the reactants, contributing to efficient syngas conversion and stable catalytic performance over the reaction period.

#### 5.2.5 Reaction Duration and Reactor Setup
- **Definition:** The overall reaction duration is vital for assessing catalyst stability and performance under continuous operation.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The Fischer–Tropsch reaction was run for 24 hours in a stainless fixed-bed reactor (10 mm inner diameter, 40 cm in length).
- **Observations:** Catalyst stability data were collected after 12 hours at a steady state, and continuous operation over 24 hours provided reproducible performance metrics, demonstrating minimal deactivation under the selected conditions.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and transformations of the Fe@Mn@xSi and Fe@Mn@xSi-c catalysts were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), including in situ XRD under reaction-relevant atmospheres, and Mössbauer spectroscopy for quantitative phase analysis. These techniques are critical for identifying active phases and understanding catalyst stability during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS).
- **Findings:** In situ XRD revealed that, under a CO + H₂O atmosphere at 320 °C, the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst exhibited a gradual decrease in iron carbide diffraction peaks and the emergence of Fe₃O₄ peaks (notably at 35.4°), indicating oxidation of iron carbide to Fe₃O₄. In contrast, the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst maintained stable iron carbide peaks (notably χ‑Fe₅C₂ at 44.2°) with no Fe₃O₄ detected, demonstrating that surface hydrophobization inhibits this phase transformation. After FTS, Mössbauer spectroscopy quantified the phase composition: Fe@Mn@0.2Si contained 59.7% χ‑Fe₅C₂, 34.3% Fe₃O₄, and 6.0% Fe³⁺(spm), while Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c comprised 74.1% χ‑Fe₅C₂ and 25.9% θ‑Fe₃C.
- **Observations:** The hydrophobic modification stabilizes the active iron carbide phase and suppresses Fe₃O₄ formation, directly correlating with improved catalytic performance and selectivity.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were employed to examine catalyst morphology and elemental distribution, focusing on the core–shell architecture and the uniformity of the SiO₂ encapsulation.
- **Findings:** TEM images confirmed that Fe@Mn nanoparticles are homogeneously coated by an amorphous SiO₂ shell, with shell thickness tunable via TEOS dosage (x = 0.05, 0.2, 2.8). EDS mapping demonstrated uniform distribution of Fe, Mn, and Si, verifying successful encapsulation and promoter incorporation.
- **Observations:** The core–shell structure is consistently achieved, and increasing SiO₂ shell thickness reduces CO accessibility to the core, impacting carburization and phase evolution.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Chemical Properties and Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Surface chemical characteristics were probed using FTIR and in situ DRIFTS, with hydrophobicity assessed via contact angle measurements and elemental analysis (XPS, EDS) for chlorine detection.
- **Findings:** DRIFTS on hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.05Si revealed carbonate species (bands at 1498 and 1364 cm⁻¹) upon CO exposure, indicating strong CO adsorption and redox activity. In contrast, the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.05Si-c catalyst exhibited only gaseous CO bands, with no carbonate formation, attributed to surface chlorine (confirmed by XPS Cl 2p peaks) from chlorotrimethylsilane modification. Chlorine was persistent even after washing and reaction.
- **Observations:** Hydrophobic modification suppresses CO adsorption and surface redox activity, stabilizing the iron carbide phase and minimizing deactivation pathways.

#### 6.1.4 Reduction and Carburization Behavior
- **Description:** Temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR and CO-TPR) was used to assess the reducibility and carburization of the catalysts, providing insight into the accessibility of internal iron species.
- **Findings:** As SiO₂ shell thickness increased (higher TEOS dosage), CO-TPR reduction peaks shifted to higher temperatures and peak areas decreased, indicating inhibited CO diffusion and reduced carburization of internal iron species.
- **Observations:** Thicker SiO₂ shells hinder the formation of active iron carbide phases, leading to increased Fe₃O₄ content after reaction.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis and XPS were used to determine elemental composition, including promoter and residual chlorine content.
- **Findings:** ICP confirmed successful Mn promoter incorporation. XPS and EDS detected residual chlorine on hydrophobic catalysts, originating from the hydrophobic modification process.
- **Observations:** The presence of chlorine correlates with suppressed CO adsorption and altered surface chemistry, impacting catalyst performance.

### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard reaction conditions (320 °C, 2.0 MPa, GHSV 5000 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst achieved a CO conversion of 53.8%, while the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst exhibited a CO conversion of 24.3% after 12 hours of stable operation.
- **Observations and Trends:** The optimal SiO₂ shell thickness (x = 0.2) enables high CO conversion by balancing core accessibility and protection. Excessively thin (x = 0.05) or thick (x = 2.8) shells result in lower conversions due to either insufficient protection or hindered CO diffusion.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity reflects the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, indicating the extent of the water-gas shift (WGS) side reaction. It is defined as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si catalyst showed a CO₂ selectivity of 28.9%, whereas the hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst demonstrated a significantly lower value of 7.9%.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic modification effectively suppresses the WGS reaction by limiting water access to the catalyst core, thereby reducing CO₂ formation and stabilizing the active iron carbide phase.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity measures the fraction of converted CO that yields methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophilic catalyst exhibited a CH₄ selectivity of 21.5%, while the hydrophobic variant reduced this to 14.1%.
- **Observations and Trends:** Lower methane selectivity in the hydrophobic catalyst indicates enhanced C–C coupling and suppressed hydrogenation of intermediates.

#### 6.2.4 Olefins and C₃⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:** Olefins selectivity represents the fraction of hydrocarbon products that are olefins, while C₃⁺ selectivity refers to hydrocarbons with three or more carbon atoms. These are typically calculated on a carbon-atom basis.
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst achieved an olefins selectivity of 64.3%, compared to 49.8% for the hydrophilic Fe@Mn@0.2Si. Enhanced C₃⁺ selectivity was also observed for the hydrophobic catalyst, as evidenced by the product distribution.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic shell promotes the formation of longer-chain olefins by stabilizing iron carbide phases and minimizing secondary hydrogenation.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO conversion over extended reaction times. The deactivation rate is defined as:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Overall reaction time}} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst maintained stable CO conversion over 110 hours of continuous operation, with negligible deactivation observed. In contrast, the hydrophilic catalyst showed more rapid deactivation due to the formation of Fe₃O₄.
- **Observations and Trends:** The hydrophobic modification confers superior long-term stability by preventing water-induced oxidation of the active phase.

**Summary:**  
The hydrophobic Fe@Mn@0.2Si-c catalyst demonstrates superior selectivity for olefins and C₃⁺ hydrocarbons, lower CO₂ and CH₄ selectivities, and enhanced stability compared to its hydrophilic counterpart. These improvements are attributed to the stabilization of iron carbide phases and suppression of water-induced deactivation, as confirmed by comprehensive phase and performance analyses.
