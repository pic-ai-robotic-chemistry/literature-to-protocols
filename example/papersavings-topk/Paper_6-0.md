## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

The catalyst synthesis is based on a controlled co‑precipitation protocol to form CoMn oxide from metal nitrate precursors, followed by post‑processing to generate the active carbide phase and the synthesis of a hydrophobic PDVB support. The procedure is reported as follows:

#### Co‑precipitation and Precipitation–Deposition Method
1. • Mixing of Precursors: An aqueous solution containing 29.4 g of Co(NO₃)₂⋅6H₂O and 17.9 g of 50 wt% Mn(NO₃)₂ is prepared.  
   • Simultaneously, a solution of Na₂CO₃ is added dropwise into deionized water to maintain the pH at approximately 8. This step ensures homogeneous mixing of metal ions.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition: Following the precursors’ mixing, vigorous stirring is maintained while the Na₂CO₃ solution is added. A suspension forms, evidenced by a visible precipitate.
3. • Aging Process: The suspension is stirred for 1 hour and then aged for 2 hours at 30°C to promote uniform CoMn oxide formation.
4. • Separation and Washing: The precipitate is centrifuged and washed with deionized water to remove residual ions.
5. • Drying: The washed precipitate is dried at 100°C for 10 hours to eliminate moisture, ensuring stability prior to calcination.
6. • Calcination: The dried material is calcined at 330°C for 3 hours to convert hydroxides into CoMn oxide.
7. • Post‑treatment: A subsequent pre‑reduction treatment in pure H₂ at 300°C for 5 hours is applied to convert the oxide to CoMn carbide (CoMnC).

#### PDVB Synthesis and Final Catalyst Preparation
• PDVB is synthesized via thermal polymerization by adding 0.5 g AIBN to 10 g divinylbenzene. The mixture is heated at 100°C in a Teflon‑lined autoclave for 24 hours (or 12 hours under DMF/EA solvent conditions for nanoporous variants).  
• The final catalyst is prepared by physical mixing (or granulation) of CoMnC with PDVB, using weight ratios of 0.5:1, 1:1, or 1.5:1. This step improves water desorption and enhances catalytic performance, as confirmed by observed increases in CO conversion and light olefin selectivity.

This detailed synthesis protocol, with its precise reagent quantities, temperature controls, and step order, ensures reproducibility and offers a comparative framework for evaluating synthesis complexity, scalability, and performance relative to alternative methods.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: CoMn Oxide Precursor

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:  
  • 29.4 g Co(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and 17.9 g of 50 wt% Mn(NO₃)₂ dissolved in deionized water  
  • Na₂CO₃ added dropwise

  • pH maintained at ~8

- **Calcination**:

#### Catalyst Preparation [2]: Formation of CoMn Carbide (CoMnC)

##### Step 4: Activation/Reduction  
- **Process**:  
  • Reduce the calcined CoMn oxide in a pure H₂ flow at 300°C for 5 hours to form CoMnC via reduction/carbonization.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Monitor gas flow and temperature closely for complete conversion.

#### Catalyst Assembly: PDVB Promoter Synthesis and Mixing

##### Step 5: PDVB Synthesis  
- **Reagents**:  
  • Divinylbenzene, with AIBN as the initiator  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Polymerize at 100°C for 24 hours for nonporous PDVB (or 12 hours with DMF/EA for nanoporous forms) in an autoclave.  
- **Process**:  
  • Conduct thermal polymerization ensuring a robust PDVB formation.

##### Step 6: Physical Mixing  
- **Process**:  
  • Physically mix the synthesized CoMnC with PDVB using weight ratios of 0.5:1, 1:1, or 1.5:1.  
  • Utilize granulation techniques to achieve a uniform catalyst blend.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Optimize the mixing to enhance water desorption and catalytic CO conversion under syngas conditions (250°C, 0.1 MPa, and a GHSV of 1800 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹).

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The catalyst activation process is designed to transform the precursor CoMn oxide into the active cobalt–manganese carbide (CoMnC) phase required for efficient Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. This pre‐reduction step is performed under carefully controlled conditions to ensure optimal metal dispersion without inducing sintering.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for initiating the reduction and controlled carburization of the catalyst precursor.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is pre‑reduced at 300 °C in a pure hydrogen atmosphere.
- **Observations:** Operating at this temperature allows complete conversion of CoMn oxide to CoMnC while maintaining a high dispersion of active sites. The XRD and TEM characterizations post‐activation confirm that no adverse sintering or agglomeration occurs.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure
- **Definition:** Activation pressure influences the interaction of the gas phase with the catalyst surface, which affects the reduction kinetics.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction is performed under atmospheric pressure, approximately 0.1 MPa.
- **Observations:** This mild pressure ensures uniform penetration of H₂ through the catalyst bed, facilitating an even reduction process that avoids localized over‑reduction.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas
- **Definition:** The composition and flow of the activation gas are essential for controlling the reduction environment.
- **Experimental Conditions:** A flow of pure H₂ is maintained at 20 mL/min during the activation process.
- **Observations:** The exclusive use of hydrogen ensures that the reduction proceeds cleanly, effectively converting CoMn oxide into the desired CoMnC phase without introducing contaminants or promoting unwanted side reactions.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step ensures sufficient exposure to the reducing atmosphere to fully convert the precursor while avoiding prolonged thermal stress that might lead to sintering.
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is activated for 5 hours.
- **Observations:** This period has been optimized to achieve complete transformation to the active phase, as evidenced by sustained CO conversion and excellent catalyst stability during subsequent reaction tests.

These defined activation parameters specifically pertain to the catalyst synthesis for Fischer–Tropsch applications and have been standardized to ensure reproducibility and reliable performance outcomes.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
The reaction temperature is a critical parameter in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, as it governs the activation of syngas and the catalyst’s intrinsic activity. In this study, the reactor was maintained at 250 °C. This temperature facilitates effective CO activation and promotes the formation of light olefins, while preventing the degradation of the hydrophobic PDVB promoter. The consistent temperature contributed to a stable catalyst performance over extended reaction periods.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
Reaction pressure determines the concentration of syngas in the reactor and influences product selectivity. The experiments were carried out at a fixed pressure of 0.1 MPa. Operating at this pressure ensured an optimal balance between reactant availability and product distribution, thereby maximizing the overall efficiency of CO conversion. Maintaining this constant pressure was key to achieving reproducible performance across tests.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction H₂/CO Ratio  
The H₂/CO molar ratio is essential in defining the hydrogenation environment of the Fischer–Tropsch reaction. The study employed an H₂/CO ratio of approximately 2. This ratio is optimal for balancing the hydrogenation steps, thereby enhancing hydrocarbon formation—specifically favoring the production of light olefins over methane. Adjustments to this ratio were shown to shift product selectivity, demonstrating clear control over reaction outcomes.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) reflects the flow rate of syngas relative to the catalyst mass, directly affecting residence time and conversion efficiency. In this research, a GHSV of 1800 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹ was utilized. This value provided an effective trade-off between CO conversion and catalyst stability, as evidenced by sustained catalytic activity during prolonged operation (up to 120 hours) with minimal deactivation. The standardized unit ensures that experimental conditions can be accurately reproduced and compared in further studies.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structural Stability
- **Description:** The crystal phase composition and stability of the CoMnC and CoMnC/PDVB catalysts were assessed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) after exposure to syngas and water treatment at 250°C.
- **Findings:** XRD patterns confirm that the active phase in the CoMnC catalyst is cobalt carbide (Co₂C). Under syngas conversion conditions (250°C, H₂/CO = 2, 0.1 MPa), the CoMnC catalyst maintains its carbide phase. Upon water treatment at 250°C, a reduction in Co₂C peaks and the emergence of CoO peaks are observed, indicating partial oxidation. In contrast, the CoMnC/PDVB physical mixture retains the Co₂C phase, with XRD patterns remaining unchanged before and after reaction or water exposure.
- **Observations:** The presence of PDVB effectively prevents water-induced oxidation of the cobalt carbide phase, ensuring structural stability and sustained catalytic activity during Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Nitrogen sorption (BET) measurements were performed to determine the surface area and pore volume of PDVB variants and reference supports.
- **Findings:** Nonporous PDVB exhibits a surface area of less than 5 m²/g. Nanoporous PDVB-EA and PDVB-DMF display significantly higher surface areas of 488.2 m²/g and 623.3 m²/g, with corresponding pore volumes of 0.74 cm³/g and 1.98 cm³/g, respectively. For comparison, SiO₂-Me has a surface area of 278.2 m²/g and a pore volume of 0.67 cm³/g.
- **Observations:** The increased porosity of nanoporous PDVB enhances CO conversion but shifts product selectivity toward heavier olefins, likely due to increased adsorption and retention of reaction intermediates.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Distribution
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to examine the morphology and distribution of catalyst components.
- **Findings:** SEM images reveal that CoMnC and PDVB are tightly and randomly packed within the catalyst granules, with intergranular distances ranging from nanometers to micrometers. TEM images show that the morphology of CoMnC remains unchanged after reaction, and the physical mixing with PDVB does not alter the catalyst’s structure.
- **Observations:** The uniform and stable morphology supports the durability of the catalyst under reaction conditions, with no significant aggregation or sintering observed.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Chemistry and Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), in situ CO adsorption experiments, and water-droplet contact angle measurements were employed to probe surface chemistry and hydrophobicity.
- **Findings:** In situ FTIR spectra indicate that water bands increase on bare CoMnC during reaction, while these signals are extremely weak on CoMnC/PDVB, demonstrating rapid water desorption. CO adsorption experiments show that water suppresses CO adsorption on CoMnC but not on CoMnC/PDVB. Water-droplet contact angle measurements yield a value of 145° for PDVB, confirming strong hydrophobicity.
- **Observations:** The hydrophobic PDVB component accelerates water removal from the catalyst surface, preserves active site accessibility for CO, and directly correlates with enhanced catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.5 Thermal Stability
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric (TG-DSC) and mass spectrometric (TG-MS) analyses were conducted to assess the thermal stability of PDVB under reaction conditions.
- **Findings:** PDVB remains stable at the reaction temperature of 250°C, with no detectable weight loss or decomposition products. Decomposition and endothermal events are only observed above 300–370°C.
- **Observations:** The thermal robustness of PDVB ensures its inertness and structural integrity during prolonged Fischer-Tropsch operation.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO Conversion
- **Description:** CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed into products during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard conditions (250°C, 0.1 MPa, H₂/CO = 2, GHSV = 1800 mL·h⁻¹·g⁻¹), the bare CoMnC catalyst achieves a CO conversion of 32.2%. Physical mixing with nonporous PDVB (1:1 weight ratio) increases conversion to 63.5%. Further increasing PDVB content to a 1.5:1 ratio raises conversion to 70.5%. Nanoporous PDVB variants (PDVB-DMF, PDVB-EA) can yield conversions up to 92.4%, though with altered selectivity profiles.
- **Observations and Trends:** The addition of hydrophobic PDVB nearly doubles CO conversion compared to bare CoMnC. Enhanced water removal by PDVB is directly linked to this improvement, as confirmed by comparative tests with hydrophilic supports and water co-feeding experiments.

#### 6.2.2 CO₂ Selectivity
- **Description:** CO₂ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO that forms CO₂, reflecting the extent of the water-gas shift and oxidation reactions:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CO}_2,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For CoMnC/PDVB catalysts, CO₂ selectivity is reported as 48.2% (0.5:1 PDVB/CoMnC), 46.3% (1:1), and 48.5% (1.5:1).
- **Observations and Trends:** CO₂ selectivity remains relatively stable across varying PDVB loadings, indicating that the primary effect of PDVB is on CO conversion rather than shifting the product distribution toward or away from CO₂.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity is the fraction of converted CO yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CH}_4 \text{ Selectivity (\%)} = \frac{F_{\text{CH}_4,\text{out}}}{F_{\text{CO,in}} - F_{\text{CO,out}}} \times 100
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity for CoMnC/PDVB catalysts ranges from 9.8% (0.5:1) to 4.8% (1:1), with a slight increase to 8.9% at 1.5:1.
- **Observations and Trends:** The lowest CH₄ selectivity is observed at the 1:1 PDVB/CoMnC ratio, coinciding with peak light olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.4 Light Olefin (C₂–C₄) Selectivity
- **Description:** Light olefin selectivity denotes the percentage of converted CO forming C₂–C₄ olefins, a key target in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.
- **Experimental Data:** CO₂-free selectivity to C₂–C₄ olefins is 62.8% (0.5:1), 71.4% (1:1), and 64.5% (1.5:1) PDVB/CoMnC.
- **Observations and Trends:** Selectivity peaks at the 1:1 ratio, suggesting an optimal PDVB content for maximizing light olefin production. Excessive PDVB may promote secondary reactions, slightly reducing olefin selectivity.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Factor)
- **Description:** The α factor reflects the probability of chain propagation versus termination in hydrocarbon formation, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution. Values range from 0 (no chain growth) to 1 (infinite chain growth).
- **Experimental Data:** While explicit α values are not numerically stated, the article notes that increasing PDVB content or hydrophobicity slightly decreases α, favoring light olefin selectivity.
- **Observations and Trends:** Enhanced hydrophobicity (via PDVB) shifts product distribution toward shorter-chain olefins by lowering the α factor.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst deactivation rate measures the decline in CO conversion over time, calculated as:
  \[
  r_d = \frac{\ln(X_0/X_t)}{t} \quad (\text{h}^{-1})
  \]
  where \( X_0 \) and \( X_t \) are initial and final CO conversions, respectively, over time \( t \).
- **Experimental Data:** Over 120 hours of continuous operation, CO conversion for CoMnC/PDVB remains nearly constant (from 64.7% to 62.8%), indicating a negligible deactivation rate.
- **Observations and Trends:** The catalyst exhibits excellent stability, with minimal loss in activity, attributed to effective water management by PDVB.

---
**Summary:** The CoMnC/PDVB catalyst demonstrates significantly enhanced CO conversion and light olefin selectivity compared to bare CoMnC, with optimal performance at a 1:1 PDVB/CoMnC ratio. Hydrophobic PDVB promotes rapid water removal, maintaining active sites and ensuring high stability and selectivity under prolonged operation.
