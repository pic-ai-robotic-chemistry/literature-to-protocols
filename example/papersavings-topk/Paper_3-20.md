## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

This section details the synthesis of Fischer–Tropsch catalysts via co‑precipitation and hydrothermal methods, emphasizing reproducibility through precise experimental control.

#### Co‑precipitation Method
1. • Precursor Mixing / Co‑precipitation  
   - Reagents: Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (total molar amount: 0.18 mol) are dissolved in water.  
   - Conditions: The solution is maintained at 35 °C and stirred at 700 rpm for 30 minutes. Ammonia is slowly added to adjust the pH to 9.
2. • Precipitation–Deposition  
   - After mixing, the suspension is stirred for an additional 1 hour followed by aging under static conditions for 2 hours.
3. • Drying  
   - The precipitate is dried at 100 °C for 12 hours.
4. • Calcination  
   - The dried catalyst is calcined in air at 400 °C for 4 hours.
5. • Post‑treatment (Reduction & Passivation)  
   - Reduction: The calcined catalyst is treated in flowing H₂ (40 mL/min) at 450 °C, 0.2 MPa for 5 hours.  
   - Passivation: Followed by exposure to 1% O₂/99% N₂ at room temperature for 2 hours.

This method, noted for its simplicity, benefits from precise pH control and reproducible mixing conditions, ensuring homogeneous distribution of metal species and improved dispersion (evidenced by an increased surface area from 37 to 47 m²/g with Zn addition).

#### Hydrothermal Method for Hydrophobic Modification and Zeolite Cracking Catalyst
1. • Seed Suspension Preparation  
   - Reagents: 30 g of TEOS, 7.4 g of water, and 26.4 g of TPAOH solution (40 wt%) are mixed at 35 °C overnight to achieve complete hydrolysis.
   - Post‑mixing drying is carried out at 45 °C for 4 hours.
2. • Autoclave Reaction  
   - The mixture is transferred to a Teflon‑lined stainless‑steel autoclave and heated at 70 °C for 72 hours.
3. • Post‑synthesis Treatment  
   - The obtained product is centrifuged, washed with water, dried at 120 °C overnight, and finally calcined at 550 °C for 5 hours.

A comparative analysis highlights that while the co‑precipitation method is simpler and more scalable, the hydrothermal route, though more complex, offers controlled morphology and enhanced hydrophobic modification. Each step—from precursor mixing, precipitation–deposition, to drying, calcination, and post‑treatments—has been carefully optimized to ensure catalyst quality and replicability in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**:

- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve the Fe and Zn nitrates in deionized water, ensuring complete dissolution by stirring at 700 rpm for 30 minutes.  
  2. Slowly add ammonia while monitoring the pH, achieving a stable value of 9.

  • Gradual addition under constant stirring to ensure homogeneous mixing  
- **Process**:  
  1. Impregnate the dried Fe–Zn precursor with the sodium dopant solution.  
  2. Ensure uniform distribution of promoters by maintaining continuous stirring during the addition.

  • Filter and wash the precipitate with deionized water (repeating washing steps until the washings are neutral).

  • Dry at 100 °C for 12 hours in a controlled oven.  
- **Calcination**:

##### Step 4: Activation  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Reduction under flowing H₂ (40 mL/min) at 450 °C for 5 hours under 0.2 MPa  
- **Process**:  
  1. Introduce the catalyst into a fixed-bed reactor.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase and Structure
- **Description:** Crystal phases were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Mössbauer spectroscopy to monitor phase evolution during catalyst preparation and reaction. These techniques are critical for correlating phase composition with Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) activity.
- **Findings:** The fresh 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1 catalyst exhibits a ZnFe₂O₄ (spinel) phase (PDF#77–11) and a weak ZnO peak (PDF#79–208). After H₂ reduction, ZnFe₂O₄ is fully converted to metallic Fe⁰ (PDF#87–721) and ZnO, with Fe⁰ showing higher crystallinity. Post-CO₂-FTS, XRD reveals Fe₃O₄ (PDF#82–1533) and χ-Fe₅C₂ (PDF#89–2544) as dominant phases, with ZnO persisting. In S-doped spent catalysts, Fe⁰, Fe₃C (PDF#23–1113), and Fe₅C₂ are present, but Fe₃O₄ is absent. Mössbauer spectroscopy quantifies these phases: for spent 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1, 32% Fe₃O₄ and 68% χ-Fe₅C₂; for 1.5Na0.2S–Fe1Zn1, 30.7% Fe⁰, 28.0% Fe₃C, and 41.3% χ-Fe₅C₂.
- **Observations:** The coexistence of Fe₃O₄ and χ-Fe₅C₂ is linked to high CO₂ conversion and olefin selectivity. S addition alters phase evolution, suppressing Fe₃O₄ formation and favoring Fe carbides, which impacts catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.2 Reducibility and Redox Properties
- **Description:** H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) assesses the reducibility of iron species, indicating catalyst activation behavior.
- **Findings:** Three main reduction peaks are observed: ZnFe₂O₄→Fe₃O₄ at ~335 °C, Fe₃O₄→FeO at ~425 °C, and FeO→Fe at ~610 °C. Na₂S addition shifts these peaks to lower temperatures.
- **Observations:** The shift to lower reduction temperatures with Na₂S suggests enhanced reducibility, attributed to the electron-withdrawing effect of S. However, this also leads to decreased CO₂-FTS activity due to site poisoning.

#### 6.1.3 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** BET analysis provides surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which influence reactant diffusion and catalyst accessibility.
- **Findings:** Addition of Zn increases surface area from 37 m²/g (1.5Na–Fe) to 47 m²/g (1.5Na–Fe1Zn1). Pore volumes and average diameters (from BJH analysis) are: 1.5Na–Fe: 0.74 cm³/g, 5.90 nm; 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1: 0.49 cm³/g, 3.10 nm; 1.5Na0.1S–Fe1Zn1: 0.56 cm³/g, 3.80 nm; 1.5Na0.2S–Fe1Zn1: 0.51 cm³/g, 3.30 nm.
- **Observations:** Zn promotes higher surface area and smaller pores, enhancing dispersion and potentially improving catalytic performance.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** SEM, TEM, and EDX mapping reveal particle morphology, size, and elemental distribution, which are crucial for understanding catalyst stability and deactivation.
- **Findings:** Fresh 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1 shows block-like morphology with ZnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles and ZnO protrusions. After reduction, large Fe⁰ spheres (260–290 nm) with smaller ZnO (60–70 nm) are observed (Fe⁰@ZnO structure). Spent undoped catalysts develop thick carbon shells (up to 28 nm), while S-doped variants show minimal carbon deposition.
- **Observations:** ZnO enhances Fe dispersion and stability. S doping inhibits carbon shell formation, reducing deactivation and improving long-term stability.

#### 6.1.5 Elemental Composition
- **Description:** ICP-OES quantifies Fe, Zn, Na, and S content, confirming synthesis accuracy and promoter incorporation.
- **Findings:** For 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1: 44.5 wt% Fe, 25.5 wt% Zn, 1.7–1.8 wt% Na; S content in 1.5Na0.1S–Fe1Zn1 is 0.11 wt%.
- **Observations:** Elemental analysis aligns with theoretical values, supporting the role of promoters in modifying catalyst properties.

#### 6.1.6 Surface Chemistry and Hydrophobicity
- **Description:** Raman and FTIR probe carbon species and water adsorption; contact angle measurements assess hydrophobicity.
- **Findings:** Raman spectra show strong D bands (1313 cm⁻¹) in spent undoped catalysts, indicating defective carbon. FTIR reveals reduced H₂O bands when hydrophobically modified SiO₂ is used. Contact angle increases with SiO₂@C16, confirming enhanced hydrophobicity.
- **Observations:** Hydrophobic modification facilitates water removal, mitigating H₂O-induced deactivation and supporting stable FTS operation.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:** CO₂ conversion quantifies the fraction of CO₂ in the feed that is transformed during the reaction. It is calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO}_2 \text{ Conversion} = \frac{(A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{(A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}}} \times 100\%
  \]
  where \(A_{\text{CO}_2}\) and \(A_{\text{N}_2}\) are GC peak areas for CO₂ and N₂, respectively.
- **Experimental Data:** Under standard CO₂-FTS conditions (320 °C, 1 MPa, H₂/CO₂ = 3, GHSV = 1200 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹), the 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1 catalyst achieves a CO₂ conversion of approximately 40%. Increasing the H₂/CO₂ ratio from 1 to 3 raises conversion from 17.5% to 39.0%. Na₂S doping (0.1–0.2 wt%) significantly reduces CO₂ conversion, with higher S content causing a more pronounced decline.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of Zn enhances catalyst stability but does not markedly increase initial conversion. Na₂S addition suppresses activity by inhibiting CO₂/CO adsorption and dissociation.

#### 6.2.2 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:** CH₄ selectivity represents the proportion of converted CO₂ yielding methane, calculated as:
  \[
  S_{\text{CH}_4} = \frac{f_{\text{CH}_4} \cdot A_{\text{CH}_4}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{f_{\text{CO}_2} \cdot \left[(A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}\right]} \times 100\%
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** CH₄ selectivity increases slightly with higher H₂/CO₂ ratios (from 11.6% at ratio 1 to 12.7% at ratio 3). Accumulation of H₂O or higher GHSV also elevates CH₄ selectivity. Na₂S doping in CO₂-FTS further shifts selectivity toward methane.
- **Observations and Trends:** Water accumulation and Na₂S addition both promote methane formation at the expense of olefins.

#### 6.2.3 Lower Olefins Selectivity (C₂–C₄)
- **Description:** Lower olefins selectivity is the fraction of converted CO₂ forming C₂–C₄ olefins, determined by:
  \[
  S_{\text{C}_2-\text{C}_4} = \frac{\sum f_i \cdot A_i/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{f_{\text{CO}_2} \cdot \left[(A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{in}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\text{CO}_2})_{\text{out}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}\right]} \times 100\%
  \]
  where \(i\) denotes C₂–C₄ olefins.
- **Experimental Data:** At optimal conditions, lower olefins selectivity reaches up to 34.5%. Increasing H₂/CO₂ ratio enhances both conversion and olefins selectivity. Na₂S doping reduces olefins selectivity, shifting the product distribution toward alkanes and CO.
- **Observations and Trends:** The highest olefins selectivity is achieved with balanced Fe₃O₄/χ–Fe₅C₂ phases and minimal water accumulation.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity and Chain Growth Factor (α)
- **Description:** C₅⁺ selectivity reflects the fraction of converted CO₂ forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbons. The chain growth factor (α) describes the probability of chain propagation, inferred from the Anderson-Schulz-Flory (ASF) distribution.
- **Experimental Data:** C₅⁺ selectivity decreases with higher H₂/CO₂ ratios and catalyst aging. Higher α values (closer to 1) correspond to increased C₅⁺ selectivity and are favored by optimal Na loading and active iron carbide (χ–Fe₅C₂) content.
- **Observations and Trends:** Catalyst deactivation (e.g., carbon shell formation) lowers α, increasing methane and reducing C₅⁺ selectivity.

#### 6.2.5 Catalyst Stability and Deactivation Rate
- **Description:** Catalyst stability is assessed by monitoring CO₂ conversion over time. The deactivation rate is:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{CO}_2 \text{ Conversion}_{\text{initial}} - \text{CO}_2 \text{ Conversion}_{\text{final}}}{\text{Time (h)}}
  \]
- **Experimental Data:** For 1.5Na–Fe1Zn1, CO₂ conversion drops from 43.3% to 38.3% over 20 h (deactivation rate ≈ 0.25% h⁻¹). Na₂S-doped catalysts exhibit improved stability in CO-FTS, maintaining high conversion (~96%) and selectivity for long-chain α-olefins (C₄⁺) over extended periods (up to 1000 h).
- **Observations and Trends:** Na₂S addition suppresses carbon deposition, significantly enhancing long-term stability in CO-FTS, while in CO₂-FTS, it reduces activity and shifts selectivity.
