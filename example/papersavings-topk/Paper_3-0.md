## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post‑processing Steps

• Overview of Catalyst Synthesis Techniques  
This section details the synthesis of the 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 and S‐modified variants using methods that combine co‑precipitation and incipient wetness impregnation. These techniques ensure homogeneous promoter dispersion and controlled phase evolution. A comparative analysis shows that co‑precipitation is cost‑effective and simple, providing uniform distribution of iron and zinc precursors, while the impregnation step allows precise modulation of Na and S promoter levels.

### Co‑precipitation Method

#### • Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. Precursor Mixing  
   - Reagents: Dissolve Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O in deionized water.  
   - Equipment: Magnetic stirrer set at 700 rpm.  
   - Duration: Stir for 30 minutes.

2. Precipitation  
   - pH Control: Slowly add ammonia to adjust the pH to 9.  
   - Observation: Formation of a stable suspension with precipitate generation.

3. Precipitation–Deposition  
   - Process: Add the support precursor dropwise while stirring continuously for 1 hour to ensure uniform deposition.  
   - Note: This critical insertion ensures molecular-level mixing of Fe and Zn species.

4. Aging, Washing, and Drying  
   - Aging: The suspension is left under static conditions for 2 hours.  
   - Washing: Filter and wash the precipitate using deionized water.  
   - Drying: Dry at 100 °C for 12 hours in a muffle furnace for uniform solvent removal.

5. Calcination  
   - Conditions: Calcine the dried material at 400 °C for 4 hours.  
   - Outcome: Formation of a ZnFe₂O₄ spinel precursor that is essential for subsequent phase evolution.

### Incipient Wetness Impregnation and Post‑Treatment

#### • Step-by-step Experimental Procedures

1. Impregnation  
   - Reagents: Introduce a Na₂CO₃ solution supplemented with Na₂S (0.1–0.3 wt%) onto the calcined precursor.  
   - Process: Allow complete wetting of the solid to enhance promoter dispersion.

2. Post‑Impregnation Drying and Calcination  
   - Drying: Maintain at 100 °C for 12 hours.  
   - Calcination: Heat at 400–550 °C for several hours to finalize the catalyst structure.

3. Reduction  
   - Final Activation: Reduce in a H₂ atmosphere at 450 °C for 5 hours in a fixed‐bed reactor, converting ZnFe₂O₄ to metallic Fe and forming iron carbides.

This stepwise protocol, with its controlled preparation and post‑processing conditions, ensures reproducibility and optimal catalyst performance for Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### Catalyst Preparation [1]: 1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • 30 g tetraethoxysilane (TEOS), 7.4 g H₂O, 26.4 g tetrapropylammonium hydroxide (TPAOH, 40 wt% in H₂O)  
  • Fe(NO₃)₃·9H₂O and Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (total 0.18 mol)  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Dissolving in 50–100 mL deionized water; stirring at 700 rpm at room temperature  
  • pH adjustment to 9 via slow ammonia addition  
  • Static aging at ~70 °C for 2 hours  
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve metal nitrates in deionized water ensuring complete dissolution over 30 minutes.  
  2. Slowly add ammonia while maintaining vigorous stirring to reach a pH of 9.  
  3. Allow the mixture to age under static conditions, promoting the formation of metal hydroxide intermediates that subsequently transform into a ZnFe₂O₄ spinel upon calcination.  
- **Key Steps**:  
  • Precise pH control is crucial to ensure uniform precipitation.  
  • Monitor stirring conditions to prevent localized pH variations.

##### Step 2: Catalyst Assembly
- **Reagents**:  
  • Dried FeZn precursor powder from Step 1  
  • Na precursor: Na₂CO₃ solution targeting a 1.5 wt% loading  
  • For S‐modified variants (e.g., 1.5Na0.1S‐Fe1Zn1), replace part of Na₂CO₃ with Na₂S to achieve 0.1–0.3 wt% S while maintaining total Na content  
- **Conditions**:  
  • Incipient wetness impregnation at room temperature  
- **Process**:  
  1. Impregnate the dried precursor uniformly with the Na₂CO₃ (or mixed Na₂CO₃/Na₂S) solution.  
  2. Mix thoroughly to ensure homogeneous promoter dispersion.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Calcination
- **Purification**:  
  1. Centrifuge and wash the precipitate with deionized water to remove impurities.  
- **Drying & Calcination**:  
  • Dry at 100 °C for 12 hours.  
  • Calcine in air at 400 °C for 4 hours (controlled ramp rate is recommended) to form the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel and ensure proper promoter distribution.

##### Step 4: Activation
- **Conditions**:  
  • Reduction in a fixed-bed reactor under H₂ at 450 °C and 0.2 MPa for 5 hours  
- **Process**:  
  1. Reduce the calcined catalyst to convert ZnFe₂O₄ into metallic Fe and iron carbides (e.g., χ‐Fe₅C₂), forming the desired Fe@ZnO structure.

**Key Considerations**:  
• Ensure all precursor synthesis steps are documented before proceeding further.  
• Monitor stirring rates and pH closely during co‐precipitation to guarantee reproducibility and catalyst performance.

## 5. Catalytic Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
- **Definition:** The activation temperature is critical for the reduction of oxide precursors to yield the active metallic and carbide phases essential for the Fischer–Tropsch reaction.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is reduced in a flowing hydrogen (H₂) atmosphere at 450 °C.  
- **Observations:** Operating at this temperature facilitates the conversion of the ZnFe₂O₄ spinel phase into active metallic iron and subsequently into iron carbide (χ‐Fe₅C₂) under reaction conditions.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
- **Definition:** Activation pressure supports the optimal interaction between the hydrogen gas and the catalyst during the reduction process.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The reduction is performed at 0.2 MPa, ensuring a controlled environment within the fixed-bed reactor.  
- **Observations:** Maintaining this pressure contributes to a uniform reduction process and mitigates risks of pressure‐induced agglomeration.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
- **Definition:** The choice of activation gas determines the reduction pathway and ultimately defines the nature of the active catalytic sites.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Hydrogen (H₂) is employed as the reducing gas. A constant flow of approximately 40 mL/min is maintained during the 5‐hour reduction step.  
- **Observations:** The H₂ atmosphere effectively reduces ZnFe₂O₄ to form metallic Fe, which is necessary for carbide formation and optimal catalytic performance in the Fischer–Tropsch process.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
- **Definition:** The duration of the activation step is optimized to achieve complete reduction while avoiding excessive sintering.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The catalyst is subjected to hydrogen reduction for 5 hours.  
- **Observations:** This period is sufficient to convert the calcined oxide precursors to the active phases without causing significant particle growth or sintering.

#### 5.1.5 Catalyst Passivation  
- **Definition:** Passivation is a critical post-reduction step that protects the freshly reduced catalyst from oxidation during handling and reactor setup.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** Immediately following reduction, the catalyst is cooled and passivated using a 1% O₂/99% N₂ mixture at room temperature.  
- **Observations:** This mild oxidation step preserves the active metallic and carbide phases, ensuring that the catalyst remains in its optimal state for subsequent Fischer–Tropsch testing.

### 5.2 Reaction

#### 5.2.1 Reaction Temperature  
- **Definition:** The reaction temperature establishes the thermal environment needed for effective syngas activation and the formation of active iron carbide phases. It is pivotal in promoting hydrocarbon chain growth while ensuring minimal induction periods.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** For CO₂-FTS catalytic evaluation, the reactor was maintained at 320 °C. This temperature was chosen to optimize CO₂ conversion to lower olefins and enhance catalyst activity.  
- **Observations:** At 320 °C, the catalyst (1.5Na‐Fe1Zn1 and its S‐modified variants) exhibited excellent stability with sustained activity over long-term tests, highlighting its ability to maintain the active iron phase composition.

#### 5.2.2 Reaction Pressure  
- **Definition:** The reaction pressure influences the partial pressures of reactants in the reactor and modulates the equilibrium between reactant consumption and product formation.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The CO₂-FTS experiments were conducted at a pressure of 1 MPa, a value that is critical for achieving the desired syngas conversion.  
- **Observations:** Operating at 1 MPa enhanced CO₂ conversion and favored the consumption of CO, leading to improved product distribution with a shift toward lower olefins.

#### 5.2.3 Reaction Gas Ratios  
- **For CO₂-FTS:**  
  - **Definition:** The H₂/CO₂ ratio directly affects the hydrogenation steps and the resulting product selectivity in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis.  
  - **Experimental Conditions:** An exact ratio of H₂/CO₂ = 3 was employed.  
  - **Observations:** This ratio enabled high CO₂ conversion and optimal formation of light α-olefins while restraining undesired by-product formation.
  
- **For CO-FTS:**  
  - **Definition:** The H₂/CO ratio is central to maintaining a balanced reduction environment and influences the extent of catalyst deactivation.  
  - **Experimental Conditions:** An H₂/CO ratio of 1 was applied.  
  - **Observations:** Although this ratio improved catalyst stability in CO-FTS by mitigating deactivation, it also caused increased selectivity towards methane formation.

#### 5.2.4 Reaction Space-Time Velocity  
- **Definition:** Space-time velocity, expressed as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), is the volume of syngas passed per gram of catalyst per hour. It directly impacts the contact time between the reactants and the catalyst.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** For CO₂-FTS, a GHSV of 1200 mL·gcat⁻¹·h⁻¹ was used.  
- **Observations:** This parameter was meticulously controlled to achieve reproducible conversion rates and to provide stable operation over prolonged reaction tests.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Phase Evolution and Structure
- **Description:** The evolution of crystal phases was monitored using X-ray diffraction (XRD) to track transformations from precursor to active catalyst phases, which are critical for Fischer-Tropsch (FT) activity.
- **Findings:** Fresh 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 catalysts display XRD peaks corresponding to ZnFe₂O₄ spinel (PDF#77–11) and a weak ZnO peak (PDF#79–208). Upon H₂ reduction (450 °C, 0.2 MPa, 5 h), ZnFe₂O₄ is converted to metallic Fe⁰ (PDF#87–721) and ZnO. After CO₂-FTS, Fe⁰ further transforms into Fe₃O₄ (PDF#82–1533) and χ-Fe₅C₂ (PDF#89–2544), which are identified as the primary active phases. In S-promoted catalysts, Fe₃C (PDF#23–1113) and Fe₅C₂ are observed, while Fe₃O₄ is suppressed.
- **Observations:** The sequential transformation from ZnFe₂O₄ to Fe⁰ and then to iron carbides is essential for catalytic performance. S addition alters phase evolution, favoring Fe₃C and Fe₅C₂ over Fe₃O₄.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** N₂ physisorption (BET and BJH methods) was used to determine surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, which influence reactant diffusion and active site accessibility.
- **Findings:** The BET surface area of 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 is 47 m²/g with a pore volume of 0.49 cm³/g and an average pore diameter of 3.10 nm. For comparison, 1.5Na-Fe exhibits a surface area of 37 m²/g, pore volume of 0.74 cm³/g, and average pore diameter of 5.90 nm. S-doping (0.1–0.2 wt% Na₂S) slightly increases average pore size (up to 3.80 nm) but does not significantly affect surface area.
- **Observations:** Zn addition enhances surface area and dispersion, while S-doping maintains textural properties.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Elemental Dispersion
- **Description:** SEM and TEM, combined with EDX mapping, were employed to visualize particle morphology and elemental distribution, which are linked to catalyst stability and activity.
- **Findings:** SEM images show block-like particles with protrusions in fresh catalysts, attributed to ZnFe₂O₄ and ZnO nanoparticles. TEM of reduced 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 reveals large Fe⁰ particles (260–290 nm) decorated with smaller ZnO nanoparticles (60–70 nm), forming a Fe@ZnO structure. EDX mapping confirms Zn and Na are co-located on Fe surfaces. After reaction, carbon shells (up to 28 nm thick) are observed on Fe particles, especially after long-term operation.
- **Observations:** The Fe@ZnO ensemble and uniform promoter dispersion are confirmed. S-containing catalysts show less carbon deposition and aggregation.

#### 6.1.4 Reducibility and Adsorption Properties
- **Description:** H₂-TPR and CO/CO₂-TPD analyses provide insights into reduction behavior and gas adsorption, which affect catalyst activation and FT selectivity.
- **Findings:** H₂-TPR reveals reduction steps at ~335 °C (ZnFe₂O₄→Fe₃O₄), ~425 °C (Fe₃O₄→FeO), and ~610 °C (FeO→Fe⁰). Na₂S addition shifts reduction peaks to lower temperatures, indicating facilitated reduction. CO₂-TPD and CO-TPD show that Na increases high-temperature desorption (stronger adsorption), while S suppresses adsorption and shifts desorption to lower temperatures.
- **Observations:** Na promotes CO₂/CO activation, while S modulates adsorption strength and enhances catalyst stability by suppressing excessive carburization.

#### 6.1.5 Hydrophobic Modification
- **Description:** Water contact angle measurements assess the hydrophobicity of SiO₂ supports, relevant for water management during FT synthesis.
- **Findings:** Hydrophobic SiO₂ (SiO₂@C16) exhibits a contact angle of 125.5°, which remains high (116.8°) after reaction, confirming stable hydrophobicity. FTIR and dehydration tests corroborate reduced water accumulation.
- **Observations:** Hydrophobic modification facilitates water removal, improving catalyst stability and selectivity under CO₂-FTS conditions.

#### 6.1.6 Iron Phase Quantification and Carbon Deposition
- **Description:** Mössbauer spectroscopy quantifies iron phase composition, while Raman and TGA/O₂-TPO analyses detect carbon deposition and deactivation.
- **Findings:** After 20 h CO₂-FTS, 1.5Na-Fe1Zn1 contains 32% Fe₃O₄ and 68% χ-Fe₅C₂; S-containing 1.5Na0.2S-Fe1Zn1 contains 30.7% Fe⁰, 28.0% Fe₃C, and 41.3% χ-Fe₅C₂. After 1000 h, carbon shells and increased defective carbon (Raman D/G ratio) are observed, with TGA/O₂-TPO showing higher carbon accumulation in S-free catalysts.
- **Observations:** Suppressing carbon deposition via S-doping is key to maintaining long-term catalyst activity and selectivity.



### 6.2 Catalyst Performance

#### 6.2.1 CO and CO₂ Conversion
- **Description:**  
  CO conversion quantifies the fraction of CO in the feed that is transformed by the catalyst, calculated as:
  \[
  \text{CO Conversion} = \frac{(A_{\text{CO}}/A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\text{CO}}/A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{(A_{\text{CO}}/A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}}} \times 100\%
  \]
  For CO₂-FTS, CO₂ conversion is similarly defined using GC peak areas for CO₂ and N₂.

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **CO₂-FTS (1.5Na‐Fe₁Zn₁, 320 °C, 1 MPa, H₂/CO₂ = 3, GHSV = 1200 mL·g_cat⁻¹·h⁻¹):**  
    CO₂ conversion = **39.0%**  
  - **CO-FTS (1.5Na‐Fe₁Zn₁, same conditions, H₂/CO = 1):**  
    CO conversion = **96.9%**

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The addition of Na₂S (0.1–0.2 wt%) suppresses CO₂ conversion in CO₂-FTS but maintains high CO conversion and improves stability in CO-FTS.

#### 6.2.2 CO Selectivity (CO₂-FTS)
- **Description:**  
  CO selectivity (S_CO) is the fraction of converted CO₂ forming CO:
  \[
  S_{\text{CO}} = \frac{f_{\text{CO}} \cdot [A_{\text{CO}}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}]}{f_{\text{CO}_2} \cdot [(A_{\text{CO}_2}/A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\text{CO}_2}/A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}]} \times 100\%
  \]

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - 1.5Na‐Fe₁Zn₁: **15.6%**
  - 1.5Na0.1S‐Fe₁Zn₁: **42.6%**
  - 1.5Na0.2S‐Fe₁Zn₁: **71.3%**

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Na₂S addition dramatically increases CO selectivity, shifting the product distribution toward CO and away from hydrocarbons.

#### 6.2.3 CH₄ Selectivity
- **Description:**  
  CH₄ selectivity (S_CH₄) is the proportion of converted CO₂ yielding methane:
  \[
  S_{\text{CH}_4} = \frac{f_{\text{CH}_4} \cdot A_{\text{CH}_4}/(A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}}{f_{\text{CO}_2} \cdot [(A_{\text{CO}_2}/A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{in}} - (A_{\text{CO}_2}/A_{\text{N}_2})_{\text{out}}]} \times 100\%
  \]

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - 1.5Na‐Fe₁Zn₁: **12.7%**
  - 1.5Na0.1S‐Fe₁Zn₁: **55.9%**
  - 1.5Na0.2S‐Fe₁Zn₁: **58.9%**

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Increasing Na₂S content significantly raises CH₄ selectivity, reducing olefin and C₅⁺ hydrocarbon formation.

#### 6.2.4 C₅⁺ Selectivity
- **Description:**  
  C₅⁺ selectivity represents the fraction of converted CO₂ forming hydrocarbons with five or more carbons:
  \[
  S_{\text{C}_5^+} = 100 - S_{\text{CH}_4} - S_{\text{C}_2^0-\text{C}_4^0} - S_{\text{C}_2^= - \text{C}_4^=}
  \]

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - 1.5Na‐Fe₁Zn₁: **48.5%**
  - 1.5Na0.1S‐Fe₁Zn₁: **1.4%**
  - 1.5Na0.2S‐Fe₁Zn₁: **2.1%**

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Na₂S addition sharply decreases C₅⁺ selectivity, favoring lighter products.

#### 6.2.5 α Factor (Chain Growth Probability)
- **Description:**  
  The α factor, derived from the Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution, reflects the probability of chain growth:
  \[
  \text{ASF:} \quad W_n = (1-\alpha)\alpha^{n-1}
  \]
  where \( W_n \) is the weight fraction of hydrocarbons with carbon number \( n \).

- **Experimental Data:**  
  Product distributions fit the ASF law, with higher α values (approaching 1) correlating with increased long-chain olefin selectivity, especially under CO-FTS.

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Catalysts with higher α values (notably S-modified under CO-FTS) yield more long-chain α-olefins.

#### 6.2.6 Catalyst Deactivation Rate
- **Description:**  
  Deactivation rate quantifies the decline in conversion over time:
  \[
  \text{Deactivation Rate} = \frac{\text{Initial Conversion} - \text{Final Conversion}}{\text{Initial Conversion} \times \text{Time on Stream}} \times 100\% \ \text{h}^{-1}
  \]

- **Experimental Data:**  
  S-free catalysts show rapid deactivation in CO-FTS, while S-containing catalysts (0.1–0.2 wt% Na₂S) maintain stable conversion over extended periods (up to 1000 h).

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  Na₂S addition markedly improves catalyst longevity by suppressing carbon deposition and stabilizing active phases.

#### 6.2.7 Additional Performance Indicators
- **Lower Olefins Selectivity:**  
  1.5Na‐Fe₁Zn₁ achieves **34.5%** selectivity to C₂–C₅ olefins under CO₂-FTS.
- **Hydrophobic Modification:**  
  SiO₂@C16 diluent increases water contact angle (from 27.5° to 125.5°), mitigating H₂O accumulation and enhancing catalyst stability.

---
**Summary:**  
The 1.5Na‐Fe₁Zn₁ catalyst and its S-modified variants demonstrate tunable performance in FT synthesis. Na₂S addition suppresses activity and heavy hydrocarbon formation in CO₂-FTS but enhances stability and long-chain α-olefin selectivity in CO-FTS. Hydrophobic support modification further improves operational stability by reducing water-induced deactivation.
