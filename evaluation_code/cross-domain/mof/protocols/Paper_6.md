## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post-processing Steps

For the ionic MOF systems reported in the article, synthesis is achieved via a controlled acid–base reaction, followed by crystallization under ambient or slightly controlled conditions. The methods are directly extracted from the experimental description and adhere to the following standardized sequence:

---

#### • TAPT.Cl

1. **Precursor Mixing / Dissolution**  
   - Dissolve TAPT in tetrahydrofuran (THF) at a concentration (e.g., 5 mg/mL).  
   - Reagent details: TAPT and solvent volumes as specified.

2. **Modulator or Structure-Directing Agent Addition**  
   - Add methanolic HCl dropwise as the acid modulator to protonate the amine.

3. **Crystallization**  
   - Allow the mixture to react at room temperature for approximately 1 hour, facilitating ionic cluster formation.

4. **Cooling and Collection**  
   - Post-reaction, leave the solution undisturbed to promote crystallization.

5. **Washing and Solvent Exchange**  
   - Filter the product; wash with THF to remove residual reactants.

6. **Drying**  
   - Dry the crystals under vacuum.

7. **Activation and Post-Treatment**  
   - Further activation may be conducted under vacuum at 110°C for 16 hours.

*Advantages:* Simple procedure, minimal specialized equipment.  
*Disadvantages:* Rapid precipitation requires strict timing to ensure reproducibility.

---

#### • TT.Br

1. **Precursor Mixing / Dissolution**  
   - Dissolve the trigonal amine linker in a mixture of benzonitrile and methanol.

2. **Modulator Addition**  
   - Add hydrobromic acid (48 wt. % HBr) dropwise to facilitate salt formation.

3. **Crystallization and Cooling**  
   - Rapid precipitation occurs at room temperature within a few hours; in some cases, a slight temperature elevation aids solubility.

4. **Washing and Drying**  
   - Collect via filtration, wash (solvent details as above), and dry under vacuum.

5. **Activation**  
   - Perform activation under conditions similar to TAPT.Cl.

---

#### • TTBT.Cl

1. **Precursor Mixing / Dissolution**  
   - Dissolve TTBT in methyl tetrahydrofuran (MTF).

2. **Modulator Addition**  
   - Introduce aqueous HCl (37%) dropwise.

3. **Crystallization**  
   - Allow slow crystallization at room temperature—with additional low-temperature storage (freezer) for 16–24 hours to obtain high-quality needle-like crystals.

4. **Washing and Drying**  
   - Conduct careful solvent exchange with methyl tetrahydrofuran and controlled drying under vacuum.

5. **Activation**  
   - Activate via heating under vacuum at 110°C for 16 hours.

*Comparative Analysis:*  
Each method emphasizes a controlled dropwise acid addition immediately after precursor dissolution, ensuring reproducibility. The choice of solvent and specific temperature/time conditions are critical to achieve the desired crystallinity and pore formation, while the minimal equipment required renders the protocol scalable.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 TAPT.Cl  
- **TAPT**: Weighed 0.1 g.

##### 3.1.1.2 TT.Br  
- **TT**: Weighed 3 mg.

##### 3.1.1.3 TTBT.Cl  
- **TTBT**: Weighed 5 mg.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 TAPT.Cl  
- **Acetone**: Poured 20 mL into the reaction vessel to dissolve TAPT.  
- **Methanolic HCl (1.25 M)**: Added dropwise 0.7 mL using a precision pipette, ensuring controlled addition at room temperature.

##### 3.1.2.2 TT.Br  
- **Benzonitrile**: Transferred 0.1 mL into the vessel containing TT.  
- **Methanol**: Transferred 0.5 mL to complement the organic solvent system.  
- **HBr (48 wt.% in water)**: Added dropwise 0.15 mL with a syringe to promote the acid–base reaction efficiently.

##### 3.1.2.3 TTBT.Cl  
- **MTF**: Poured 0.3 mL into a small vial to dissolve TTBT.  
- **Aqueous HCl (37%)**: Transferred 0.5 mL into an outer vial to create a controlled environment for crystallization.  
- **Water**: Added 0.5 mL alongside the aqueous HCl to complete the solvent mixture.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 TAPT.Cl  
- **Acetone/TAPT mixture**: Stirred at room temperature for 1 hour immediately following the dropwise addition of methanolic HCl, ensuring thorough homogenization of the ionic components.

##### 3.1.3.2 TT.Br  
- **Organic solvent mixture (Benzonitrile/Methanol/TT solution)**: Mixed with the dropwise addition of HBr at room temperature and stirred for 1 hour to achieve a uniform reaction medium.

##### 3.1.3.3 TTBT.Cl  
- **MTF/TTBT solution**: After mixing with the aqueous HCl and water in the outer vial, the small vial was capped and left undisturbed for 16–24 hours to allow controlled crystallization. Subsequently, the vial was transferred to a freezer where the crystals were maintained for approximately 3 days prior to any further processing.

These detailed reagent preparation steps, grouped by weighing, liquid transfer, and mixing for each non‐metal framework, ensure reproducibility and clarity in replicating the experimental protocol.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

The following protocols detail step‐by‐step procedures for synthesizing three related frameworks using an “inverse” approach. Each synthesis begins from the raw organic amine precursor and follows a strict workflow: Precursor Preparation → MOF Assembly/Crystallization → Purification → Drying & Activation.

#### MOF Preparation [1]: TAPT.Cl  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 2 g TAPT, 0.15 mL methanolic HCl (48 wt. % in water), using tetrahydrofuran (THF) as the solvent.  
- **Conditions**: Dissolve TAPT in THF at a ~5 mg/mL concentration at room temperature with magnetic stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve TAPT completely in THF.
  - Add methanolic HCl dropwise over 5 minutes under vigorous stirring to ensure uniform protonation.  
- **Key Steps**: Control acid addition rate to favor formation of ionic clusters.

##### Step 2: MOF Assembly/Crystallization  
- **Conditions**: Continue stirring at room temperature for 1 hour after acid addition to promote controlled crystallization.  
- **Process**:  
  - Maintain stirring to allow ionic clusters (protonated amines with Cl⁻) to assemble into a crystalline network.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Activation  
- **Process**:  
  - Filter the crystalline material and wash with THF.
  - Remove residual solvent under reduced pressure.
  - Dry under vacuum at 70 °C for 16 hours.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure complete solvent removal while preserving framework integrity.

---

#### MOF Preparation [2]: TT.Br  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 0.1 g TT dissolved in 20 mL acetone, and 0.15 mL hydrobromic acid (48 wt. % in water).  
- **Conditions**: Room temperature stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve TT in acetone.
  - Add HBr dropwise over 1 minute while stirring to protonate the amine.
- **Key Steps**: Rapid acid addition triggers immediate precipitation.

##### Step 2: MOF Assembly and Step 3: Purification & Drying  
- **Process**:  
  - Continue stirring for 1 hour to facilitate framework formation.
  - Filter the product, then wash with acetone.
  - Dry under vacuum, following controlled conditions to prevent framework damage.

---

#### MOF Preparation [3]: TTBT.Cl  
##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: 5 mg TTBT dissolved in methyl tetrahydrofuran (MTF) with an aqueous HCl solution.  
- **Conditions**: Room temperature stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  - Dissolve TTBT in MTF.
  - Slowly add aqueous HCl (as a controlled modulator) to induce protonation.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure homogeneous mixing to avoid impurity formation.

##### Step 2: Assembly/Crystallization & Step 3: Purification  
- **Conditions**: Crystallize by storing the mixture at low temperature (e.g., in a freezer) for 16–24 hours.  
- **Process**:  
  - After crystallization, wash with THF to remove residual solvent.
  - Activate by heating under vacuum (e.g., 353 K for 14 hours).  
- **Key Steps**: Controlled evaporation and low temperature prevent premature crystal degradation.

## 5. Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The study on porous isoreticular non‐metal organic frameworks adopts a distinctive synthetic strategy in which conventional post‐synthesis activation steps are not separately performed. Instead, the protocols for MOF formation rely on in situ conditions during synthesis that inherently balance crystallization and solvent control without invoking additional activation treatments.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature 
Activation temperature is typically used to facilitate solvent removal and open the pore channels in MOF materials. In the reported work, however, the reaction is carried out at room temperature. This condition is maintained throughout the synthesis and crystallization steps, with no dedicated high‐temperature activation process being implemented.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure 
For conventional MOF activation, reduced pressures (or vacuum conditions) are often applied to ensure effective desolvation. In this case, there is no mention of any specific activation pressure setting. The synthesis protocol does not define or require a post‐synthetic vacuum treatment, as the framework assembly directly yields the desired porous structure.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas and Flow Rate 
The use of a specific activation gas (such as N₂) and its corresponding flow rate can be critical in conventional MOF processing to preserve framework integrity. Here, no controlled gas atmosphere or flow rate details are provided for activation. The design strategy focuses entirely on the direct crystallization process by dropwise addition of acid, thereby circumventing the conventional gas‐flow activation requirements.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time 
Often, activation time is optimized to ensure complete guest molecule removal while avoiding framework degradation. The article, however, does not specify a dedicated time setting for a separate activation step. Instead, the timeframe mentioned relates solely to the synthesis and solvent‐evaporation phases during crystal growth.

In summary, while aspects such as temperature, pressure, gas composition, flow rate, and time are fundamental parameters in traditional MOF activation procedures, the work in question integrates pore formation with crystallization under ambient conditions. This approach negates the need for further activation, with performance metrics reflecting adequate pore accessibility and material stability directly from the synthesis process.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
- **Description:** Crystal structure and phase purity were determined using single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). SCXRD provides atomic-level structural information, while PXRD assesses bulk phase purity and crystallinity.
- **Findings:**  
  - **TAPT.Cl:** SCXRD revealed a trigonal crystal system (space group P3m1), with two distinct one-dimensional pore channels. PXRD patterns matched the simulated pattern from computational crystal structure prediction (CSP), confirming high phase purity and the intended framework topology.
  - **TT.Br:** PXRD patterns closely matched those predicted by CSP, supporting its classification as part of an isoreticular series. The experimental and simulated patterns confirmed the intended crystal packing and absence of significant impurities.
  - **TTBT.Cl:** PXRD data, including measurements from capillary-mounted samples, confirmed the global minimum-energy structure predicted by CSP. The experimental pattern matched the simulated structure, indicating high phase purity.
- **Observations:** No impurity peaks or alternative polymorphs were detected in any framework, and reproducibility was confirmed across multiple preparations.

#### 6.1.2 Pore Architecture and Porosity
- **Description:** Pore dimensions and solvent-accessible volumes were determined by structural analysis (SCXRD, PXRD) and computational modeling. These parameters are critical for understanding guest uptake and framework performance.
- **Findings:**  
  - **TAPT.Cl:** The ionic (cylindrical) pore (A) measures 4.86–5.87 Å across; the diamond-shaped organic pore (B) is defined by aromatic linkers. The calculated solvent-accessible pore volume is 31.9% of the unit-cell volume (probe radius 1.2 Å).
  - **TT.Br:** The ionic pore (A) is approximately 4.86–5.87 Å in diameter, with additional larger organic-defined pores. Pore dimensions were confirmed by PXRD and HR-TEM.
  - **TTBT.Cl:** The organic channel (B) is significantly enlarged, with dimensions of 14.3 Å × 8.5 Å. The calculated solvent-accessible pore volume is 43.2% of the unit-cell volume, the highest among the series.
- **Observations:** The frameworks are isoreticular, with systematic increases in pore size and volume from TAPT.Cl to TTBT.Cl, correlating with linker extension.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Crystallinity
- **Description:** Morphology and particle size were assessed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM), providing insight into crystal habit and microstructural features.
- **Findings:**  
  - **TAPT.Cl:** Grown as single crystals suitable for SCXRD; bulk morphology not detailed by SEM/TEM.
  - **TT.Br:** SEM revealed long, thin needle-like crystals, much smaller and more fragile than TAPT.Cl. HR-TEM confirmed high crystallinity and visible pore channels.
  - **TTBT.Cl:** SEM showed small, fragile needle-like crystals. HR-TEM visualized one-dimensional pore channels, with fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis supporting the predicted structure.
- **Observations:** The small size and fragility of TT.Br and TTBT.Cl crystals limited single-crystal analysis but confirmed uniform crystallinity and pore architecture.

#### 6.1.4 Thermal Stability
- **Description:** Thermal stability was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) after activation under vacuum at 110 °C (or 70 °C for iodine removal) for 16 h. SCXRD data were collected at 100 K.
- **Findings:** All frameworks maintained structural integrity during activation and guest uptake experiments, with no evidence of framework decomposition under the reported conditions.
- **Observations:** The frameworks demonstrated sufficient thermal stability for gas sorption and iodine capture applications.

#### 6.1.5 Chemical Composition and Framework Integrity
- **Description:** Elemental composition and framework integrity were confirmed by SCXRD, PXRD, and comparison with CSP-predicted structures.
- **Findings:** All frameworks exhibited the expected stoichiometry of organic cations and halide anions, with no evidence of decomposition or loss of framework integrity during synthesis or activation.
- **Observations:** The absence of metal nodes was confirmed, and the frameworks’ composition was consistent with the intended design.




### 6.2 Performance Results

#### 6.2.1 Gas Adsorption Capacity
- **Description:** Gas adsorption capacity quantifies the amount of gas a material can adsorb per unit mass under specified conditions, typically reported in mmol·g⁻¹. For these frameworks, CO₂ uptake was measured at 195 K using volumetric adsorption analyzers after activation under vacuum.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - **TAPT.Cl:** 4.0 mmol·g⁻¹ CO₂ at 195 K.
  - **TT.Br:** 4.6 mmol·g⁻¹ CO₂ at 195 K.
  - **TTBT.Cl:** 2.4 mmol·g⁻¹ CO₂ at 195 K.
- **Observations and Trends:** TT.Br exhibits the highest CO₂ uptake, while TTBT.Cl shows lower capacity, attributed to partial loss of porosity during activation. The measured values are competitive with or exceed those of many conventional MOFs under similar conditions.

#### 6.2.2 Iodine Capture and Reversibility
- **Description:** Iodine uptake is a critical metric for evaluating the ability of porous materials to capture volatile iodine, reported in wt% and mol per mol. Reversibility is assessed by repeated adsorption–desorption cycles, with retention of capacity indicating stability.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - **TAPT.Cl:** 248 wt% (6.8 mol per mol).
  - **TT.Br:** 213 wt% (4.99 mol per mol).
  - **TTBT.Cl:** 211 wt% (3.83 mol per mol).
  - **Cycling Stability:** All three frameworks maintained 100% iodine uptake efficiency over five cycles.
- **Observations and Trends:** The frameworks outperform benchmark MOFs such as ZIF-8 (125 wt%), Cu-BTC (175 wt%), and NU-1000 (145 wt%) in iodine capture. PXRD and TGA analyses confirm that the frameworks retain their structure and adsorption capacity after multiple cycles, demonstrating excellent durability and reversibility.

#### 6.2.3 Water Stability and Adsorption
- **Description:** Water stability is evaluated by monitoring crystallinity after water exposure, while water adsorption capacity is measured gravimetrically and reported in mmol·g⁻¹.
- **Experimental Data:**
  - **TTBT.Cl:** Retains crystallinity after 48 hours in water; maximum water uptake of 12.4 mmol·g⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** TTBT.Cl demonstrates significant water stability and adsorption capacity, supporting its robustness for practical applications.

#### 6.2.4 Structural Influence on Performance
- **Description:** The relationship between pore architecture and performance is assessed by correlating structural parameters (e.g., pore size, volume) with adsorption metrics.
- **Experimental Data:** TTBT.Cl possesses the largest calculated pore volume (43.2% of unit cell), yet lower CO₂ uptake under harsh activation, but superior iodine capacity and rapid adsorption kinetics under milder conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The frameworks’ highly charged, isoreticular pore channels are directly linked to their exceptional iodine uptake and cycling stability. Structural integrity is maintained after repeated guest adsorption and removal, as confirmed by PXRD.
