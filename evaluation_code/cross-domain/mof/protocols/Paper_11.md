## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post-processing Steps

The MOF synthesis reported in the article employs a solvothermal approach, followed by a hydrothermal conversion step, ensuring high reproducibility and crystallinity. Two variants are described: the pristine b‑PCMOF2 and its 1,2,4‑triazole–loaded counterpart, b‑PCMOF2(Tz)x.

#### Solvothermal Synthesis of b‑PCMOF2
1. • Precursor Mixing/Dissolution  
   - Dissolve 8.053 g (63.86 mmol) of anhydrous phloroglucinol in 200 mL of dry dimethyl carbonate under an inert (Ar) atmosphere. Cool the solution to 0 °C.
2. • Modulator or Structure-Directing Agent Addition  
   - Add 12.43 mL (186.8 mmol; 2.925 equiv.) of chlorosulfonic acid dropwise over two minutes with vigorous stirring to achieve a homogeneous orange solution.
3. • Crystallization  
   - Warm the reaction mixture to ambient temperature and stir for 1.5 h under argon, followed by sealing and continued stirring for an additional 22.5 h.
4. • Cooling and Collection  
   - Remove the solvent using rotary evaporation, then redissolve the resulting viscous brown oil in 150 mL of water.
5. • Washing and Solvent Exchange  
   - Adjust the pH to 2.0 by adding sodium bicarbonate. Precipitate the sulfonated ligand by adding the aqueous solution to 4 L of acetone. Isolate the thick white precipitate via vacuum filtration.
6. • Activation/Post-Treatment  
   - Transfer the isolated a‑phase into a hydrothermal bomb with 5 mL water, 1.5 mL methanol, and 1.5 mL acetone. Heat at 100 °C for 24 h and cool to room temperature (over 12 h) before isolating the final b‑phase product.

#### Incorporation of 1,2,4‑Triazole for b‑PCMOF2(Tz)x
- Following the initial synthesis steps, 1,2,4‑triazole is added in situ to the aqueous solution during precipitation. Controlled loading (x = 0.3, 0.45, and 0.6) is achieved by varying the amounts of triazole while maintaining similar reaction conditions.
- The subsequent hydrothermal conversion ensures that the framework remains isostructural with b‑PCMOF2, with guest molecule replacement enhancing proton conduction under anhydrous conditions.

A comparative analysis suggests that while the solvothermal method emphasizes well-defined reaction parameters—such as precise temperature control (0 °C for sulfonation, 100 °C for hydrothermal treatment) and strict inert conditions—the process benefits reproducibility and product purity. However, scalability and equipment costs (e.g., hydrothermal bombs) remain considerations for broader applications.

##### 3.1.3.1 b‑PCMOF2

- **Modified precursor solution**: Mixed similarly under argon. After the initial reaction, the 1,2,4‑triazole was added and the solution was stirred for an additional 24 hours. These detailed reagent preparation steps—covering solid weighing, accurate liquid transfer, and controlled mixing—provide a reproducible and precise protocol for both MOF variants. Each step is carefully documented to maintain the exact stoichiometry required for achieving the targeted structural and performance characteristics in high-temperature proton conductors.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### MOF Preparation [1]: b‑PCMOF2

##### Step [1]: Precursor Preparation  
- **Reagents**: Anhydrous phloroglucinol (8.053 g, 63.86 mmol), dry dimethyl carbonate (200 mL), chlorosulfonic acid (12.43 mL, 186.8 mmol, 2.925 equiv.), water (150 mL), and sodium bicarbonate (added until pH 2.0).  
- **Conditions**: Reaction performed under an argon atmosphere; initial cooling to 0 °C followed by warming to ambient temperature, with continuous vigorous stirring.  
- **Process**:  
  • Dissolve phloroglucinol in dry dimethyl carbonate and cool the solution to 0 °C under argon.  
  • Add chlorosulfonic acid dropwise over two minutes while stirring vigorously; maintain the system under a steady argon flow.  
  • Warm the reaction to ambient temperature and stir for 1.5 hours, then seal and stir an additional 22.5 hours.  
  • Remove the solvent via rotary evaporation to yield a viscous brown oil.  
  • Dissolve the oil in 150 mL water and adjust the pH to 2.0 using sodium bicarbonate.  
  • Precipitate the ligand by adding the aqueous solution into 4 L of acetone and isolate by vacuum filtration.  
- **Key Steps**: Maintain inert conditions during sulfonation and secure precise pH adjustments during precipitation.

##### Step [2]: MOF Assembly/Crystallization  
- **Reagents**: The newly prepared sulfonated ligand, with requisite metal salts for framework formation.  
- **Conditions**: Solvothermal assembly with subsequent hydrothermal treatment at 80–100 °C for 8–24 hours; a sealed Teflon-lined autoclave or hydrothermal bomb is employed.  
- **Process**:  
  • Mix the ligand and metal precursors; transfer to the reactor and heat at 100 °C for 24 hours to facilitate conversion from the a-phase to the b-phase.  
  • Cool gradually to room temperature over 12 hours to promote crystal growth and framework integrity.  
- **Key Steps**: Ensure proper reactor sealing to maintain necessary pressure and temperature conditions.

##### Step [3]: Purification, Drying & Activation  
- **Process**:  
  • Filter the crystals, wash sequentially with DMF and ethanol, then dry in an oven at 100 °C for 24 hours.  
  • Thermal analysis (e.g., TGA) monitors the removal of guest water, confirming framework activation.

#### MOF Preparation [2]: b‑PCMOF2(Tz)x

##### Step [1]: In Situ Loading of 1,2,4‑Triazole  
- **Reagents**: 1,2,4‑triazole added in controlled amounts (x = 0.3, 0.45, or 0.6 equivalents) alongside the precursor mix.  
- **Conditions**: Ambient temperature stirring for 24 hours following formation of the initial framework (a-phase).  
- **Process**:  
  • Incorporate triazole directly into the aqueous suspension of the framework precursor; stir well to ensure uniform guest loading.  
  • Proceed with hydrothermal conversion and subsequent purification as described for b‑PCMOF2.  
- **Key Steps**: Confirm uniform triazole incorporation via PXRD and solid‑state NMR, ensuring preservation of the framework structure.

## 5. Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation process for the MOF samples is aimed at converting the as‐synthesized a‑phase into the crystallographically robust b‑phase, ensuring optimal conditions for anhydrous proton conduction. This process involves both a thermal treatment and a pre‐activation pelletization step that are critical for achieving structural integrity and uniformity.

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
• **Definition:** The activation temperature is essential for promoting phase conversion and the controlled removal or rearrangement of loosely bound water molecules within the framework.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** Activation is carried out in a sealed hydrothermal bomb at 80 °C. This temperature is selected to facilitate the structural transformation while maintaining the integrity of the intentionally incorporated guest molecules, such as 1,2,4‑triazole, which are crucial for high-temperature proton conduction.  
• **Observations:** The controlled heating results in effective phase conversion with the maintenance of crystallinity, as confirmed by PXRD analyses.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
• **Definition:** Prior to thermal activation, applying hydrostatic pressure enhances particle contact and densification, which is important for uniform thermal conversion.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The MOF powder is pressed under a hydrostatic pressure of 5,000 kg for 2 minutes. This pelletization ensures that the materials achieve a homogeneous structure during the subsequent heating step.  
• **Observations:** The dense, uniform pellets exhibit improved consistency in phase conversion during activation.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas and Flow Rate  
• **Definition:** While gas atmospheres can be used to influence activation, in this specific pre‐use thermal treatment no dedicated gas flow is implemented.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** Although an inert argon atmosphere is employed during earlier synthesis stages to prevent unwanted reactions, the activation process itself is conducted in the sealed environment of the hydrothermal bomb without additional gas flow.  
• **Observations:** The absence of gas flow during the activation step avoids potential disturbances and ensures that the thermal treatment exclusively promotes phase conversion.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
• **Definition:** The duration of thermal activation ensures complete phase transformation and sufficient removal of excess water.  
• **Experimental Conditions:** The MOF is heated for 8 hours in the hydrothermal bomb.  
• **Observations:** This time period has been optimized to achieve a uniform and stable activated phase suitable for subsequent proton conductivity studies.

### 5.2 Application/Performance Evaluation

#### 5.2.1 Test Temperature  
The MOF’s application performance was evaluated across a range of temperatures to elucidate its proton conduction behavior. Proton conductivity measurements, particularly for the Tz-loaded variants, were carried out at temperatures up to 150 °C under anhydrous conditions. In detailed fuel cell testing, the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) was exposed to temperatures spanning from 23 °C to 150 °C. Notably, at 100 °C the b‑PCMOF2(Tz)0.45 sample achieved an open circuit voltage (OCV) of 1.18 V, though increasing the temperature to 120 °C and 140 °C resulted in OCV reductions (0.93 V and 0.77 V, respectively) attributed to fuel crossover effects.  
Observations indicated that while high temperatures favor intrinsic proton conduction, optimal fuel cell performance is maintained only within a defined thermal window.

#### 5.2.2 Test Pressure  
For the assembly and operation of the MEA, a hydrostatic pressure of 5000 kg was applied. This controlled pressure ensured robust contact between the electrodes and the MOF material, thus supporting reproducible performance during testing.  
Observations confirmed that meticulous control of pressure is essential to achieve consistent electrical connectivity and to reliably assess proton conduction properties.

#### 5.2.3 Test Gas Composition and Atmosphere  
Proton conductivity and fuel cell evaluations were conducted under a strictly anhydrous hydrogen (H₂) atmosphere, with H₂ supplied to the anode and air to the cathode in the fuel cell setup. The use of H₂ is central to facilitating proton conduction, while concurrently eliminating the interference of moisture from water-mediated conduction pathways. AC impedance spectroscopy, recorded using an impedance and gain-phase analyzer (SI model 1260), provided clear differentiation between the Tz-mediated and unmodified MOF systems.  
Observations confirmed that this gas composition allowed for the precise validation of a Grotthuss-type proton hopping mechanism, with Tz-loaded samples displaying markedly enhanced conductivity in anhydrous conditions.

#### 5.2.4 Test Duration  
Stability tests involved monitoring the fuel cell performance for 72 hours at 100 °C. Throughout this period, the b‑PCMOF2(Tz)0.45 sample maintained stable OCV readings, underscoring the material’s durability under prolonged high-temperature operation.  
This duration-based evaluation reinforces the reproducibility of the experimental setup and the MOF’s potential for sustained application in proton exchange membrane technologies.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
- **Description:** Crystal structure and phase purity were assessed using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and single-crystal diffraction. PXRD is essential for confirming the bulk crystallinity and phase identity of MOFs, while single-crystal diffraction provides detailed atomic arrangements.
- **Findings:** The PXRD patterns of pristine b‑PCMOF2 display a broad hump centered at 20.58° (2θ), attributed to incommensurate lattice packing within the crystalline framework. Experimental PXRD patterns closely match those simulated from single-crystal data, confirming high phase purity and structural integrity. Tz-loaded b‑PCMOF2(Tz)x samples (x = 0.3, 0.45, 0.6) are isostructural with the pristine MOF, as evidenced by matching PXRD patterns before and after guest loading, pellet pressing, and impedance analysis.
- **Observations:** The consistent presence of the broad hump at ~20.5° across all samples indicates it is intrinsic to the MOF structure. No impurity peaks were observed, and the framework remains intact after all processing steps.

#### 6.1.2 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphology and particle size were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), which provides insights into crystal habit and sample homogeneity.
- **Findings:** SEM images reveal homogeneous, rod-shaped or plate-like crystallites for both pristine and Tz-loaded MOFs. The rod-shaped morphology is retained after pellet pressing and impedance analysis, although some reduction in crystal size occurs due to grinding.
- **Observations:** The uniform morphology and absence of secondary phases confirm high phase purity and controlled crystallization.

#### 6.1.3 Thermal Stability
- **Description:** Thermal stability was evaluated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), which track weight loss and heat flow as a function of temperature.
- **Findings:** Pristine b‑PCMOF2 exhibits a two-step weight loss: gradual water removal from 25–300 °C, followed by framework degradation above 300 °C (sharp DSC peak at 378 °C). Tz-loaded samples show additional mass loss steps: two for water (25–250 °C), a third for triazole release (250–298 °C), and final framework degradation above ~360 °C.
- **Observations:** The separation of mass loss events confirms distinct incorporation of water and triazole guests. Both pristine and Tz-loaded MOFs are thermally stable up to at least 250–300 °C.

#### 6.1.4 Chemical Composition and Guest Incorporation
- **Description:** Elemental analysis (EA) and solid-state NMR (^1H and ^13C) were used to determine elemental composition and confirm guest molecule incorporation.
- **Findings:** EA results match theoretical values, confirming correct stoichiometry and absence of significant impurities. Solid-state NMR spectra show characteristic signals for incorporated triazole, with no evidence of free triazole. Variable temperature ^1H MAS NMR reveals changes in triazole protonation state, supporting its role in proton conduction.
- **Observations:** Uniform and controlled triazole loading is critical for achieving high proton conductivity while maintaining framework crystallinity.




### 6.2 Performance Results

#### 6.2.1 Proton Conductivity
- **Description:** Proton conductivity (σ) quantifies the ability of a material to transport protons under an applied electric field, typically measured in Siemens per centimeter (S·cm⁻¹). It is determined using a.c. impedance spectroscopy, with conductivity calculated from the bulk resistance (R_b) extracted from Nyquist plots:  
  \[
  \sigma = \frac{L}{R_b \cdot A}
  \]
  where \(L\) is the pellet thickness and \(A\) is the electrode area.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **b‑PCMOF2 (hydrated, unloaded):** Exhibits very low proton conductivity (<10⁻⁶ S·cm⁻¹) at all temperatures, with negligible conduction above 70 °C due to dehydration.
  - **b‑PCMOF2(Tz)x (Tz-loaded, x = 0.3, 0.45, 0.6):**  
    - At 150 °C in anhydrous H₂:  
      - x = 0.3: ~2 × 10⁻⁴ S·cm⁻¹  
      - x = 0.45: ~5 × 10⁻⁴ S·cm⁻¹  
      - x = 0.6: ~4 × 10⁻⁴ S·cm⁻¹  
    - Activation energy (Ea):  
      - 1.8 eV (50–90 °C), 0.34 eV (90–150 °C) for x = 0.45.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - Incorporation of 1,2,4‑triazole (Tz) as a guest molecule dramatically enhances proton conductivity under anhydrous conditions and at elevated temperatures, far surpassing the hydrated, unloaded MOF.  
  - Conductivity is maximized at intermediate Tz loading (x = 0.45), with higher or lower loadings resulting in reduced performance.  
  - The Tz-loaded MOFs maintain high conductivity above the boiling point of water, enabling operation at temperatures up to 150 °C.

#### 6.2.2 Fuel Cell Performance (Open Circuit Voltage)
- **Description:** Open circuit voltage (OCV) measures the maximum voltage of a fuel cell when no current is drawn, reflecting the membrane’s ability to conduct protons and block gas crossover.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **b‑PCMOF2(Tz)0.45 MEA:**  
    - OCV at 100 °C: 1.18 V (stable for 72 h)  
    - OCV at 120 °C: 0.93 V  
    - OCV at 140 °C: 0.77 V
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - The high OCV at 100 °C approaches the theoretical maximum for H₂–O₂ cells, indicating excellent proton conduction and gas separation.  
  - OCV decreases at higher temperatures due to increased fuel crossover, but remains substantial, demonstrating the MOF’s suitability as a high-temperature proton-conducting membrane.

#### 6.2.3 Cycling Stability
- **Description:** Cycling stability assesses the retention of conductivity and structural integrity after repeated heating/cooling and measurement cycles.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Tz-loaded MOFs were subjected to two full heating cycles to 150 °C (12–18 h) and cooling to 23 °C (~12 h), with conductivity measured at each stage.
  - Conductivity and impedance plots were highly reproducible across cycles.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - PXRD and SEM analyses confirm that the MOF structure and crystallinity are preserved after cycling.
  - No significant degradation in conductivity or framework integrity was observed, indicating robust cycling stability.

#### 6.2.4 Mechanistic Insights (Isotope Effect)
- **Description:** The kinetic isotope effect, observed by comparing proton conductivity in H₂ and D₂ atmospheres, provides evidence for a Grotthuss-type proton hopping mechanism.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - For deuterated MOF (d‑[b‑PCMOF2(Tz)0.5]), bulk resistance in D₂ was 1.5 times higher than in H₂.
- **Observations and Trends:**  
  - The reduced conductivity in D₂ confirms proton transfer via a Grotthuss mechanism, with Tz acting as an efficient anhydrous proton carrier.
