## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post-processing Steps

This section describes the experimental procedures for MIL-125 and its defect-engineered derivatives, outlining a step-by-step approach designed for reproducibility.

#### Solvothermal Synthesis of MIL-125
1. **Precursor Mixing/Dissolution**  
   • Mix 3.0 g terephthalic acid (18.1 mmol) with 54 mL N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMF) under stirring for 10 minutes to ensure complete dissolution.  
2. **Modulator or Structure-Directing Agent Addition**  
   • Add 6 mL methanol (0.15 mmol) which acts as both a modulator and solvent modifier, followed by 1.56 mL tetrabutyl titanate (4.58 mmol) as the Ti(IV) source.  
3. **Crystallization**  
   • Heat the mixture in a reflux setup at 130 °C for 20 hours.  
4. **Cooling and Collection**  
   • Allow the reaction mixture to cool to room temperature; collect the product by centrifugation.  
5. **Washing and Solvent Exchange**  
   • Wash the precipitate several times with DMF and methanol to remove unreacted species.  
6. **Drying**  
   • Dry the product in air, followed by degassing at 443 K under high dynamic vacuum (10⁻¹⁰ bar) for 12 hours.  
7. **Activation**  
   • Activate the dried MOF under dry nitrogen flow at 423 K for 2 hours, then cool to 298 K and, if required, expose to benzene vapor at room temperature.

#### Synthesis of MIL-125-Defect and Post-Synthetic Metal Incorporation
1. **Modified Precursor Mixing**  
   • Follow the same initial mixing and dissolution protocol but use a sub-stoichiometric 1.2 mL tetrabutyl titanate (3.52 mmol) to induce defect sites.
2. **Crystallization and Post-Treatment**  
   • Conduct the crystallization at 130 °C for 20 hours, then perform identical cooling, washing, and drying steps.  
3. **Metal Ion Incorporation**  
   • Disperse the defect-engineered product in methanol, add appropriate metal salts (e.g., Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn), and stir for 3 hours. Follow with centrifugation and washing to yield MIL-125-defect with anchored secondary metal ions.

This comparative approach highlights the advantages of controlled defect engineering—enabling versatile post-synthetic modifications—while maintaining the robust synthesis framework of MIL-125 under consistent, scalable conditions.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### MOF Preparation [1]: MIL-125

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Terephthalic acid (3.0 g, 18.1 mmol), DMF (54 mL), methanol (6 mL), and tetrabutyl titanate (1.56 mL, 4.58 mmol).
- **Conditions**: Initial mixing under stirring for 10 minutes at room temperature.
- **Process**:
  - Dissolve terephthalic acid in DMF by stirring for 10 minutes.
  - Add methanol and then tetrabutyl titanate gradually.
  - Ensure homogeneous dissolution, as precise reagent quantities guarantee a controlled metal-to-ligand ratio.
- **Key Steps**: Accurate measurement and addition order is crucial for reproducibility.

##### Step 2: MOF Assembly and Crystallization
- **Reagents**: The prepared solution from Step 1.
- **Conditions**: Heat at 130 °C for 20 hours under reflux using a reflux condenser.
- **Process**:
  - Transfer the mixture to a reflux setup.
  - Heat under constant reflux for 20 hours to facilitate the formation of titanium–oxo clusters connected by BDC linkers.
  - Cool the reaction mixture to room temperature.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain stable temperature and ensure proper reflux conditions to achieve crystalline product.

##### Step 3: Purification
- **Process**:
  - Collect the formed MIL-125 crystals via centrifugation.
  - Wash repeatedly with DMF and methanol to remove unreacted species and impurities.
- **Key Steps**: Ensure thorough washing; even brief solvent exchanges can significantly influence crystallinity.

##### Step 4: Drying and Activation
- **Drying Conditions**: Desolvate the material under a dry N₂ flow at 423 K for 2 hours, then cool to 298 K.
- **Activation**: This process ensures complete removal of residual solvents, yielding an activated framework ready for performance evaluation.

#### MOF Preparation [2]: MIL-125-defect and MIL-125-X Derivatives

##### Step 1: Modified Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**: Follow similar reagents as MIL-125; adjust tetrabutyl titanate to 1.2 mL to intentionally create defect sites.
- **Process**: Repeat the mixing and dissolution steps as above.

##### Step 2: Post-synthetic Metal Exchange (for MIL-125-X)
- **Reagents**: Disperse 0.5 g MIL-125-defect in 35 mL methanol and add 0.33 mmol of a secondary metal salt (e.g., ZnCl₂).
- **Conditions**: Stir for 3 hours at room temperature.
- **Process**:
  - After stirring, collect the product by centrifugation.
  - Wash with methanol and activate under dry N₂ flow.
- **Key Steps**: Maintain consistent stirring and purification to ensure uniform metal exchange, while quantitative analysis (e.g., ICP-OES) confirms a metal-to-titanium ratio near 0.9–1.1:7.

This detailed workflow ensures precise replicability from raw precursor mixing to final activation, aligning with the reproducibility standards expected in MOF synthesis for trace benzene capture.

## 4. Characterization Methods

### 4.1 PXRD  
- **Operational Description**: PXRD was performed to evaluate crystallinity and phase purity of the MIL-125 MOF samples. Samples were first solvent-exchanged (soaked in methanol for 3 days with daily acetone refreshment), air-dried, and then degassed at 443 K under high dynamic vacuum (10⁻¹⁰ bar) for 12 hours. Both laboratory and high-resolution synchrotron PXRD were employed.  
- **Instruments and Models**: An X'pert multipurpose diffractometer was used for conventional PXRD; synchrotron measurements were conducted at beamline I11 at the Diamond Light Source.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Laboratory PXRD used Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA; the synchrotron analysis utilized monochromated radiation (λ = 0.824388 Å) at room temperature.  
- **Test Parameters**: Data were recorded over a 2θ range of 5–50°.  
- **Key Notes**: This technique is ideal for crystalline samples; limitations may arise when analyzing amorphous structures.

### 4.2 TGA/DSC  
- **Operational Description**: Thermal stability was assessed by heating the samples under air flow while monitoring weight loss.  
- **Instruments and Models**: A SDTQ600 TA instrument was used.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Heating was performed at a rate of 5 °C/min up to approximately 620 K.  
- **Test Parameters**: Weight loss and thermal transition data were recorded.  
- **Key Notes**: Consistent thermal analysis confirms the metal-to-ligand ratio and sample stability.

### 4.3 BET Surface Area Analysis  
- **Operational Description**: N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured to determine surface area and porosity.  
- **Instruments and Models**: Micromeritics ASAP 2020 was employed.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Measurements were performed at 77 K after full degassing as described above.  
- **Test Parameters**: Relative pressure (P/P₀) was varied from 0 to 0.9.  
- **Key Notes**: BET analysis was derived from data in the appropriate P/P₀ range ensuring reliable porosity evaluation.

### 4.4 SEM/TEM Imaging  
- **Operational Description**: These methods mapped the particle morphology and distribution of metal ions.  
- **Instruments and Models**: SEM imaging was conducted with a working distance of 10 mm at 15 kV, while TEM was operated at 300 kV.  
- **Characterization Conditions**: Samples were mounted on carbon-taped aluminum stages (with carbon coating) for SEM; TEM samples were drop-cast onto gold grids with holey carbon films.  
- **Test Parameters**: Adjustments in voltage and resolution provided detailed image contrast.  
- **Key Notes**: Adequate sample preparation was crucial to avoid charging and obtain clear morphological details.

### 4.5 FTIR Spectroscopy  
- **Operational Description**: FTIR was applied for detecting changes in the binding environment after benzene adsorption. In situ FTIR measurements were performed after desolvation under dry N₂ at 423 K for 2 hours, cooling to 298 K, then controlled benzene dosing (0–127 mbar).  
- **Instruments and Models**: Data were collected at the MIRIAM beamline of the Diamond Light Source.  
- **Key Notes**: Although specific wavelength ranges were not detailed, this method provided critical insights into host–guest interactions.

### 4.6 XPS  
- **Operational Description**: XPS characterized the oxidation state and surface chemistry of metal centers.  
- **Instruments and Models**: A Kratos Axis Ultra Hybrid spectrometer with Al Kα (1486.6 eV) was used.  
- **Test Parameters**: Survey spectra with 80 eV pass energy and core-level scans at 20 eV.  
- **Key Notes**: Calibration was performed using C 1s (284.8 eV) and special care was taken to mitigate charging via carbon coating.

### 4.7 Solid-State NMR  
- **Operational Description**: Solid-state NMR probed local structural environments, particularly changes upon benzene adsorption.  
- **Instruments and Models**: A Bruker Avance III spectrometer (9.4 T, 400 MHz 1H Larmor frequency) equipped with a 4 mm HFX MAS probe was used.  
- **Test Parameters**: Magic angle spinning was operated at 12 kHz.  
- **Key Notes**: This method offered sensitive insights into framework bonding and defect sites.

### 4.8 Gas Sorption/Breakthrough Analysis  
- **Operational Description**: Breakthrough experiments evaluated benzene capture performance.  
- **Test Parameters**: An air stream containing 5 ppm benzene was directed through ~10 mg of desolvated MOF at 200 mL/min.  
- **Key Notes**: Breakthrough was defined when 1% of the initial benzene concentration was detected, ensuring reproducible evaluation of dynamic adsorption capacity.

## 5. Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
- **Definition:** Activation temperature plays a crucial role in removing solvent molecules from the pore system, thereby unblocking access and reopening the internal surface area of MOF materials.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The methanol-exchanged MOF samples were heated to 423 K for 12 hours under ultra‐high vacuum conditions. For membrane precursors, a dynamic vacuum treatment at 393 K was conducted overnight to eliminate residual THF.  
- **Observations:** These moderate temperatures ensure effective solvent removal while preserving the structural integrity, with post‐activation BET measurements confirming high porosity and crystallinity.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
- **Definition:** Low pressure is essential during activation as it enhances desolvation by providing conditions under which solvent molecules can be efficiently evacuated from the pores.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** The methanol-exchanged samples were activated under an ultra‐high vacuum of approximately 10⁻¹⁰ mbar.  
- **Observations:** Such stringent vacuum levels facilitate complete removal of solvent residues without inducing framework collapse.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Gas  
- **Definition:** Although the primary activation involved vacuum techniques, inert gases are sometimes employed during subsequent regeneration steps to avoid reintroduction of moisture and to maintain framework stability.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** In regeneration protocols following benzene adsorption, a flow of dry helium was used.  
- **Observations:** The inert nature of helium safeguards against any potential chemical interaction that might compromise the MOF’s structure.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Flow Rate  
- **Definition:** The flow rate of the activation gas during regeneration ensures that desorbed molecules are continuously removed from the environment, thereby enhancing the efficiency of the process.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A dry helium flow of 40 mL/min was applied during the regeneration step.  
- **Observations:** This controlled flow rate promotes steady sweeping of desorbed species and contributes to rapid and efficient regeneration.

#### 5.1.5 Activation Time  
- **Definition:** The duration of activation is critical to achieving complete solvent removal while preventing potential thermal degradation of the MOF framework.  
- **Experimental Conditions:** A consistent activation time of 12 hours was employed for the methanol‐exchanged samples at 423 K.  
- **Observations:** Extended activation duration was key to attaining maximum porosity without compromising the framework’s stability, as confirmed by subsequent performance evaluations.

### 5.2 Application/Performance Evaluation

#### 5.2.1 Test Temperature  
The performance evaluation of MIL‑125‐based MOFs incorporated multiple temperature settings to optimize adsorption and kinetic performance. For gas adsorption measurements, tests were carried out at 27 °C, 77 °C, 127 °C, 177 °C, and 227 °C (equivalent to 300, 350, 400, 450, and 500 K), enabling an assessment of adsorption capacity across a broad temperature range. In dynamic breakthrough experiments, the test temperature was maintained at 298 K, emphasizing its importance in influencing both adsorption capacity and kinetics.

#### 5.2.2 Test Pressure  
Pressure plays a critical role in determining adsorption performance. Experiments were conducted under low-pressure conditions with an initial pressure of 1.27 mbar and extending to partial pressures for benzene up to 127 mbar. These carefully controlled conditions allowed for precise monitoring of adsorption behaviors, particularly indicating that MIL‑125‑Zn exhibits enhanced uptake even at very low pressures.

#### 5.2.3 Test Gas Composition  
The gas composition for performance evaluation was strictly defined. Calibration experiments utilized a gas mixture comprising 5 ppm benzene balanced with air. In addition, the influence of competitive adsorption was examined by varying relative humidities (40% and 80% RH), which provided insights into the interactions between benzene and water vapors in the MOF matrix.

#### 5.2.4 Test Flow Rate  
Dynamic breakthrough tests were performed using a fixed flow rate of 200 mL/min. This constant flow ensured reproducibility and allowed for accurate determination of breakthrough times, as monitored by a mass spectrometer integrated into a customized permeation rig.

#### 5.2.5 Test Duration and Cycle Conditions  
Breakthrough experiments were defined by the time required for a 1% benzene breakthrough, with detailed observations noting that MIL‑125‑Zn required 7 hours for regeneration under a flow of dry helium. Remarkably, complete regeneration in only 10 minutes was achieved by heating at 80 °C under a dry N₂ flow. Furthermore, the MOF maintained its crystallinity and adsorption capacity over 15 consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles, demonstrating excellent performance stability.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of MIL-125, MIL-125-defect, and MIL-125-X (X = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn) were assessed using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), high-resolution synchrotron X-ray diffraction, and neutron powder diffraction (NPD). These techniques are essential for confirming framework integrity, long-range order, and successful incorporation of defects or secondary metal ions.
- **Findings:** PXRD patterns collected over 2θ = 5–50° (Cu-Kα, λ = 1.54056 Å) matched simulated patterns for all samples, confirming phase purity and retention of structure after activation, benzene exposure, and long-term air storage. High-resolution synchrotron PXRD (λ = 0.824388 Å) and NPD provided Rietveld-refined structures, verifying the incorporation of secondary metals (e.g., Zn(II) in {Ti₇Zn}) and quantifying defect sites.
- **Observations:** No impurity peaks were detected. The frameworks maintained crystallinity and phase purity after activation and post-synthetic modifications.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were determined by N₂ adsorption isotherms at 77 K using the BET method. These parameters are critical for evaluating the accessibility and capacity of MOF pores.
- **Findings:** After methanol/acetone solvent exchange and activation (443 K, 10⁻¹⁰ bar, 12 h), MIL-125 exhibited a BET surface area of 1,462 m²/g, while MIL-125-defect reached 1,866 m²/g. All samples displayed high porosity, with isotherms confirming consistent textural properties across the series.
- **Observations:** The activation protocol ensured complete desolvation, and the measured surface areas are in line with literature values, indicating robust and accessible pore structures.

#### 6.1.3 Thermal Stability and Composition
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed under air (5 °C min⁻¹) to assess thermal stability and metal-to-ligand ratios, which reflect framework robustness and defect incorporation.
- **Findings:** All MIL-125 variants were stable up to ~620 K. MIL-125-defect showed a 62.9% weight loss, corresponding to a metal-to-ligand molar ratio of 1.14 (theoretical: 1.16), consistent with ICP-OES data.
- **Observations:** Introduction of defects did not compromise thermal stability; TGA profiles confirmed compositional changes due to defect engineering.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Elemental Distribution
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were used to examine particle morphology, size, and elemental homogeneity.
- **Findings:** SEM (15 kV, 10 mm working distance) and STEM (300 kV) images revealed uniform particle morphology and size distribution for all samples. EDS mapping confirmed homogeneous distribution of secondary metal ions (e.g., Zn) in MIL-125-X.
- **Observations:** Controlled synthesis and post-synthetic treatments yielded consistent particle characteristics and even metal incorporation.

#### 6.1.5 Chemical and Electronic Structure
- **Description:** FTIR, XPS, and solid-state NMR were employed to probe the chemical environment, oxidation states, and host–guest interactions.
- **Findings:** FTIR spectra showed a single O–H band for MIL-125 and two distinct bands for MIL-125-defect and MIL-125-Zn, which shifted upon benzene loading, indicating guest binding at defect sites. XPS (C 1s at 284.8 eV calibration) confirmed Ti(IV) and the presence of divalent secondary metals. Solid-state NMR revealed shifts in ¹³C resonances and splitting of aromatic peaks upon benzene loading, evidencing framework adjustment and host–guest interactions.
- **Observations:** Spectroscopic data corroborated successful defect engineering, secondary metal incorporation, and dynamic host–guest chemistry within the MOF pores.



### 6.2 Performance Results

#### 6.2.1 Benzene Adsorption Capacity
- **Description:** Benzene adsorption capacity quantifies the amount of benzene adsorbed per gram of MOF under specified conditions, typically reported in mmol g⁻¹. This metric is determined from single-component adsorption isotherms at controlled temperature and pressure.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **MIL-125:** 1.92 mmol g⁻¹ at 1.2 mbar and 298 K; 0.15 mmol g⁻¹ at 0.12 mbar and 298 K.  
  - **MIL-125-defect:** 7.23 mmol g⁻¹ at 1.2 mbar; 3.83 mmol g⁻¹ at 0.12 mbar.  
  - **MIL-125-Zn:** 7.63 mmol g⁻¹ at 1.2 mbar; 5.33 mmol g⁻¹ at 0.12 mbar.
- **Observations and Trends:** Defect engineering and secondary metal ion incorporation (e.g., Zn(II)) significantly enhance benzene uptake, especially at low pressures, indicating the critical role of tailored binding sites.

#### 6.2.2 Dynamic Breakthrough Performance
- **Description:** Breakthrough time (min g⁻¹) and dynamic adsorption capacity (mmol g⁻¹) assess the MOF’s ability to remove trace benzene from air streams under continuous flow. Breakthrough time is defined as the time required for the effluent benzene concentration to reach a set fraction (1% or 10%) of the inlet concentration.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **MIL-125-Zn:** 1% breakthrough time: ~72,000 min g⁻¹ (dynamic capacity: 3.21 mmol g⁻¹); 10% breakthrough time: 111,000 min g⁻¹ (decreasing to 95,400 min g⁻¹ after air exposure).  
  - **MIL-125-defect:** 1% breakthrough time: ~40,200 min g⁻¹ (1.79 mmol g⁻¹); 10%: 76,200 min g⁻¹.  
  - **MIL-125:** 1% breakthrough time: 12,000 min g⁻¹ (0.48 mmol g⁻¹); 10%: 22,800 min g⁻¹.
- **Observations and Trends:** MIL-125-Zn outperforms both MIL-125 and MIL-125-defect, demonstrating superior trace benzene removal efficiency.

#### 6.2.3 Humidity Tolerance
- **Description:** Performance under humid conditions is critical for real-world applications. Breakthrough experiments were conducted at 40% and 80% relative humidity (RH).
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **MIL-125-Zn:** 1% breakthrough time: 51,000 min g⁻¹ (2.27 mmol g⁻¹) at 40% RH; 47,000 min g⁻¹ (2.09 mmol g⁻¹) at 80% RH.
- **Observations and Trends:** Only minor reductions in capacity and breakthrough time were observed under high humidity, confirming robust performance.

#### 6.2.4 Cycling Stability and Regenerability
- **Description:** Cycling stability evaluates the retention of adsorption capacity over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles. Regenerability assesses the ease of restoring adsorption performance.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **MIL-125-Zn:** Retained >99.99% of initial benzene capacity after 15 cycles at 298 K. Complete desorption achieved after 7 h under dry He at room temperature or 10 min at 80 °C in dry N₂.
- **Observations and Trends:** The material maintains crystallinity and porosity after repeated cycling and regeneration, even after exposure to moisture.

#### 6.2.5 Effect of Defect Engineering and Metal Incorporation
- **Description:** The impact of structural defects and secondary metal sites on performance is quantified by comparing uptake and breakthrough data.
- **Experimental Data:**  
  - **MIL-125-defect:** 3.83 mmol g⁻¹ at 0.12 mbar (vs. 0.15 mmol g⁻¹ for MIL-125); 1.79 mmol g⁻¹ in breakthrough (vs. 0.48 mmol g⁻¹ for MIL-125).
  - **MIL-125-Zn:** 5.33 mmol g⁻¹ at 0.12 mbar; 3.21 mmol g⁻¹ in breakthrough.
- **Observations and Trends:** Defect engineering and Zn(II) incorporation synergistically enhance benzene capture, with Zn(II) providing strong, dedicated binding sites and improved host–guest interactions.
