## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post-processing Steps

The synthesis of the investigated MOF—In‐MIL‐53D and its defect‐containing hybrids—is achieved via a solvothermal method using a DMF solvent system. This method relies on precise control of reactant ratios to induce targeted defects through the incorporation of a shorter linker (H₂BDC) as a modulator alongside the primary linker (H₂BPDC).

#### Solvothermal Synthesis Procedure

1. • Precursor Mixing / Dissolution  
  - Reagents: In(NO₃)₃, H₂BPDC, and H₂BDC (the latter acting as a modulator)  
  - Solvent: DMF  
  - The organic linkers are combined in specific ratios (e.g., varying from 10–50% H₂BDC relative to H₂BPDC) to control defect levels.  

2. • Modulator or Structure-Directing Agent Addition  
  - H₂BDC is introduced immediately after initial precursor dissolution to direct the formation of structural defects, ensuring reproducibility across synthesis batches.

3. • Crystallization  
  - The homogeneous solution is transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated under elevated temperature conditions conducive to nucleation and growth.  
  - Although exact temperatures and durations are not specified, the process is optimized for effective crystal formation under solvothermal conditions.

4. • Cooling and Collection  
  - Upon completion of the reaction, the autoclave is allowed to cool to room temperature.  
  - The solid product is then collected via filtration, ensuring minimal disturbance to the formed crystals.

5. • Washing and Solvent Exchange  
  - The filtered material is washed repeatedly with DMF and ethanol to remove any unreacted precursors and byproducts, enhancing purity.

6. • Drying  
  - The washed MOF is dried at controlled conditions to remove residual solvents prior to activation.

7. • Activation  
  - The final product is activated by drying under vacuum at 250 °C for 3 hours, a critical step for evacuating guest molecules and maximizing porosity.

This detailed procedure, with clearly defined steps and parameters, supports reproducibility and provides a comparative framework for evaluating synthesis complexity, scalability, and cost effectiveness in MOF production.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
- **Description:** The crystal structure and phase purity of In‐MIL‐53D and its defect-containing hybrids were assessed using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). PXRD is essential for confirming framework topology, phase identity, and the impact of defect incorporation.
- **Findings:** PXRD patterns of both pristine In‐MIL‐53D and hybrids with up to 25% short linker (H₂BDC) incorporation matched the simulated patterns for In‐MIL‐53D, confirming a 3D orthorhombic framework and high phase purity. The absence of peaks corresponding to other phases (e.g., In‐MIL‐68B) further validated the structural assignment. Whole pattern fitting and unit cell parameter calculations were performed using MDI Jade 9.0 software.
- **Observations:** At moderate defect levels, the PXRD patterns remained sharp and well-defined, indicating preserved crystallinity. With higher defect incorporation (Hybrid-3.5 and Hybrid-4.5), peak intensity decreased and broadening was observed, suggesting partial structural collapse and reduced crystallinity.

#### 6.1.2 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Surface area and porosity were evaluated by N₂ and CO₂ adsorption isotherms, analyzed using the BET method and NLDFT pore size distribution. These parameters are critical for understanding the MOF’s adsorption capacity and the effect of defect engineering.
- **Findings:** Pristine In‐MIL‐53D exhibited minimal porosity. Defect-containing hybrids, particularly Hybrid-4 (~39% H₂BDC), showed up to an 11-fold increase in CO₂ uptake at 1 P/P₀ and a 773% increase in N₂ uptake compared to the pristine material. Enhanced pore volume and activation of one-dimensional channels were confirmed by BET and NLDFT analyses.
- **Observations:** Optimal defect levels triggered gate-opening behavior, maximizing accessible porosity. Excessive defects led to decreased surface area and pore volume, correlating with framework destabilization.

#### 6.1.3 Morphology and Particle Size
- **Description:** Morphology and particle size were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), which provides insight into crystallite shape, size, and uniformity.
- **Findings:** SEM images revealed rodlike crystallites for both pristine and defect-containing In‐MIL‐53D up to 25% short linker incorporation. No phase separation or byproduct formation was observed.
- **Observations:** The rodlike morphology was maintained at moderate defect levels, supporting the preservation of framework integrity. At higher defect concentrations, reduced crystallinity observed in PXRD may be reflected in less defined or fragmented morphologies.

#### 6.1.4 Chemical Composition and Defect Analysis
- **Description:** The chemical composition and presence of framework defects were confirmed by ^1H NMR and IR spectroscopy. These techniques identify linker ratios and functional groups associated with defect sites.
- **Findings:** ^1H NMR of digested samples (in acetic acid-d₄ and DMSO-d₆) quantified the incorporation of H₂BDC and H₂BPDC, confirming controlled defect introduction. IR spectra showed characteristic bands for uncoordinated –CO₂H groups, indicative of open metal sites and structural defects.
- **Observations:** The successful incorporation of defects was directly correlated with enhanced adsorption performance, as verified by the presence of uncoordinated carboxylic acid groups and altered linker ratios.

#### 6.1.5 Unit Cell Parameters and Structural Flexibility
- **Description:** Changes in unit cell parameters, derived from PXRD data, reflect framework flexibility and pore structure modulation due to defect incorporation.
- **Findings:** Up to 25% H₂BDC incorporation, the a-axis increased (e.g., from 25.46 Å to 26.14 Å), while the b-axis decreased (from 16.70 Å to 16.50 Å), indicating expansion of one-dimensional channels. At higher defect levels, significant peak broadening and intensity loss indicated partial collapse.
- **Observations:** Moderate defect incorporation expanded the pore structure and enhanced flexibility, while excessive defects compromised framework stability.




### 6.2 Performance Results

#### 6.2.1 Gas Adsorption Capacity
- **Description:** Gas adsorption capacity quantifies the volume of gas adsorbed per gram of MOF under specified conditions, typically reported in cm³ g⁻¹. This metric is central to evaluating the suitability of MOFs for gas storage and separation applications.
- **Experimental Data:** For CO₂, Hybrid‑4 (with ~39% H₂BDC incorporation) achieved a CO₂ uptake of 351.5 cm³ g⁻¹ at 1.0 atm, representing an almost 11-fold increase compared to pristine In‑MIL‑53D (32.1 cm³ g⁻¹). N₂ adsorption at 1.0 atm also increased dramatically, with Hybrid‑4 exhibiting up to a 773% higher uptake relative to the pristine material.
- **Observations and Trends:** The enhancement in gas uptake is attributed to defect engineering via controlled incorporation of the short linker (H₂BDC), which generates open metal sites and activates gate-opening behavior. However, excessive defect levels led to decreased performance due to partial framework collapse, as indicated by reduced BET surface area and pore volume at the highest defect concentrations.

#### 6.2.2 Chemical Warfare Agent Simulant Capture
- **Description:** The adsorption capacity for chemical warfare agent (CWA) simulants, such as 2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide (CEES), is measured as the number of moles of CEES adsorbed per mole of MOF (molCEES/molMOF) after exposure to CEES vapor. This metric assesses the MOF’s potential for detoxification applications.
- **Experimental Data:** Hybrid‑4 demonstrated a saturated CEES adsorption capacity of 0.24 molCEES/molMOF after 3 hours, a 5-fold increase compared to pristine In‑MIL‑53D (0.05 molCEES/molMOF) under identical conditions.
- **Observations and Trends:** The significant improvement in CEES uptake is directly correlated with the presence of open metal sites and increased porosity resulting from optimal defect incorporation. This highlights the effectiveness of the defect-engineering strategy for enhancing CWA capture.

#### 6.2.3 Adsorption Kinetics
- **Description:** Adsorption kinetics describe the rate at which a MOF adsorbs a target molecule, often modeled using kinetic equations. In this study, a pseudo-first-order kinetic model was applied to CEES adsorption.
- **Experimental Data:** The time-dependent uptake of CEES by both pristine and hybrid MOFs fit well to the pseudo-first-order model, indicating that the adsorption process is governed by the availability of active sites and diffusion within the porous framework.
- **Observations and Trends:** The presence of defects and open metal sites in the hybrids accelerates the adsorption rate, as evidenced by the faster approach to saturation in Hybrid‑4 compared to the pristine material.

#### 6.2.4 Structure–Performance Relationship
- **Description:** The relationship between defect level, porosity, and adsorption performance is critical for optimizing MOF functionality.
- **Experimental Data:** Systematic variation of H₂BDC content revealed a pyramidal trend: gas and CEES uptake increased with defect incorporation up to an optimal point (~39% H₂BDC), beyond which further defects compromised structural integrity and reduced performance.
- **Observations and Trends:** This balance underscores the importance of precise defect control to maximize adsorption capacity while maintaining framework stability.
