### 1.3 Common Laboratory Equipment

The synthesis and characterization of COFs rely on a suite of common laboratory equipment designed to ensure precision, reproducibility, and control over experimental conditions. Standard laboratory devices are systematically integrated at various stages, from reagent preparation to post-synthesis handling, to maintain the structural integrity and performance of the COF samples.

#### Drying Equipment
Drying equipment is critical to eliminate residual solvents and moisture, which can otherwise compromise the COF structure through unwanted hydrolysis or degradation. For example, vacuum ovens provide controlled temperature settings—often around 120 °C under vacuum conditions—for efficient drying. Freeze dryers (such as Labconco FreeZone systems) are also employed to gently remove moisture without affecting sensitive crystalline structures, ensuring that the final product exhibits high purity and consistent performance.

#### Calcination Equipment
Calcination involves controlled thermal treatment to activate or modify COF precursors. Equipment such as muffle furnaces (e.g., Nabertherm L9/11/B170) offers precise temperature control and uniform heating, features that are essential for reliable annealing processes. These devices are typically equipped to maintain an inert atmosphere, thereby preventing oxidation or other side reactions during the calcination process.

#### Other Common Equipment
A variety of additional laboratory tools support the overall experimental workflow. High-precision analytical balances, capable of readings to 0.0001 g, ensure accurate mass measurements during reagent preparation. Magnetic stirrers play a crucial role in homogenizing reaction mixtures, while glass filtration setups—including Buchner funnels—are used during sample purification to remove unreacted materials and excess solvents. Furthermore, pipettes and syringes facilitate precise liquid handling, which is essential for adding catalysts, acids, bases, and washing solvents under controlled conditions. Standard reaction vessels and glassware are employed to provide secure environments for both room-temperature condensation reactions and controlled solvothermal processes.

Together, this curated set of equipment enables rigorous control over environmental conditions, ensuring that COF synthesis and subsequent analyses are carried out with high fidelity and reproducibility.

## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post-processing Steps
This section outlines the synthesis techniques applied for the preparation of chiral COFs (COF‑300 and COF‑304). Two main room‑temperature condensation methods are described. Both rely on precise reagent ratios, controlled reaction conditions, and sequential post‐processing to ensure reproducibility. A comparative analysis indicates that while both procedures use similar solvents and catalysts, the choice of monomeric components (e.g., benzaldehyde versus 2,3‑dihydroxyterephthalaldehyde) and specific amino acid derivatives leads to differences in crystallization behavior and chiral induction.

#### COF‑300 – Room‑Temperature Condensation Method
1. • Monomer Mixing / Solution Preparation  
   - Reagents: 0.4 mmol palmitoyl‑L‑AlaA or palmitoyl‑D‑AlaA dissolved in 25 mL water; 2 mL of 0.2 mol L⁻¹ sodium hydroxide added.  
   - Equipment: Standard reaction vessel with magnetic stirring.  
2. • Polymerization/Condensation  
   - Maintain the mixture at 50 °C while stirring for 10 minutes to form an emulsion.  
   - Add 2 mL of 0.2 mol L⁻¹ hydrochloric acid to adjust pH, followed by 0.2 mmol of benzaldehyde derivative and 0.1 mmol of tetrakis(4‑aminophenyl)methane (TAM) supplied in a 2 mL 0.2 mol L⁻¹ p‑toluenesulfonic acid (PTSA) solution.  
3. • Crystallization / Reaction  
   - Allow the reaction to progress at ambient conditions for 1 hour.  
   - Observation: Transition from an amorphous phase to increasing crystal formation accompanied by the emergence of circular dichroism signals.
4. • Washing / Purification  
   - Collect the product by filtration, then wash repeatedly with tetrahydrofuran (THF) and water to remove residual templates.
5. • Drying  
   - Air‐dry the sample to prepare for subsequent characterization and testing.

#### COF‑304 – Comparative Room‑Temperature Method
- Similar to COF‑300, but employs 0.4 mmol palmitoylglycine (C16‑GlyA) as the amino acid derivative and 0.2 mmol 2,3‑dihydroxyterephthalaldehyde in place of benzaldehyde derivatives.  
- Reaction conditions, washing, and drying steps mirror those of COF‑300, ensuring scalability and reproducibility.

Overall, the chosen room‑temperature procedures ensure simplicity, cost‑effectiveness, and clear observations of phase transitions, with PTSA playing a critical catalytic role in both frameworks. This step‐by‐step approach facilitates direct comparison between COF‑300 and COF‑304, emphasizing the impact of monomer selection on polymer crystallinity and chiral outcomes.

## 3. Synthesis Procedures

### 3.1 Reagent Preparation

In this section, the precise preparation of reagents for both COF-300 and COF-304 is described. The procedure is organized into three main stages—Weighing, Liquid Transfer, and Mixing—to ensure full reproducibility and effective synthesis of the chiral COFs.

#### 3.1.1 Weighing

##### 3.1.1.1 COF-300

- **Tetrakis(4-aminophenyl)methane (TAM)**: Weighed as prescribed by the formulation to maintain the desired molar ratio.

##### 3.1.1.2 COF-304

- **Amino Acid Derivative Template (C16‑GlyA / C16‑L‑AlaA / C16‑D‑AlaA)**: Weighed to achieve 0.4 mmol, ensuring chiral induction.

In both cases, the solid reagents are measured using standard analytical balances, ensuring precision in the mass measurements critical for reproducibility.

#### 3.1.2 Liquid Transfer

##### 3.1.2.1 COF-300  
- **Water**: Poured 25 mL into the reaction vessel using a graduated cylinder to guarantee accurate volume measurement.

##### 3.1.2.2 COF-304  
- **Water**: Poured 25 mL using a graduated cylinder.  
- **Sodium Hydroxide Solution (0.2 M)**: Transferred 2 mL via pipetting into the mixture to assist in emulsion formation.  
- **Hydrochloric Acid (0.2 M)**: Poured 2 mL into the reaction vessel using a pipette, facilitating subsequent pH adjustments.

#### 3.1.3 Mixing

##### 3.1.3.1 COF-300  
- **Precursor Mixture**: Stirred at 50 °C for 10 minutes to form a homogeneous emulsion, establishing the reaction environment essential for COF formation.

##### 3.1.3.2 COF-304  
- **Precursor Mixture**: Stirred at 50 °C for 10 minutes under ambient laboratory conditions. This controlled stirring integrates the chiral templates uniformly into the reaction system.

Each step is designed to maintain reproducibility and precision. The accurate weighing of solids, controlled liquid addition, and regulated mixing conditions collectively ensure that the COF frameworks are synthesized under consistent conditions, promoting reliable outcomes in both structural evolution and resultant performance.

### 3.2 Detailed synthesis process

#### COF Preparation [1]: COF-300 (Conformational Chiral Variants)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • 0.4 mmol palmitoyl-L‑alanine (C16‑L‑AlaA)  
  • 0.2 mmol benzaldehyde derivative (BDA)  
  • 2 mL of 0.2 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution  
  • 2 mL of 0.2 mol/L hydrochloric acid  
  • Additional solvents (water, acetone, tetrahydrofuran)
- **Conditions**:  
  Stir at 50 °C for 10 minutes to form a stable emulsion; adjust pH to 1 with hydrochloric acid.
- **Process**:  
  1. Dissolve 0.4 mmol of C16‑L‑AlaA with sodium hydroxide in water to generate an emulsion.  
  2. Slowly add 0.2 mmol of BDA after emulsion formation.  
  3. Adjust pH by adding hydrochloric acid.  
  4. Stir the homogeneous mixture for 10 minutes.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Ensure rapid and complete dissolution; maintain constant temperature and pH.

##### Step 2: COF Assembly
- **Reagents**:  
  • Tetrakis(4‑aminophenyl)methane (TAM, 0.1 mmol) dissolved in a 0.2 mol/L p‑toluenesulfonic acid (PTSA) solution.
- **Conditions**:  
  Reaction continues at 50 °C for 1 hour.
- **Process**:  
  1. Add the TAM/PTSA solution to the prepared precursor mixture.  
  2. Allow the condensation reaction to progress under steady stirring.  
  3. Monitor reaction time closely to ensure complete framework formation.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Maintain addition sequence to promote controlled polymerization and stereochemical induction.

##### Step 3: Purification, Drying & Activation
- **Process**:  
  1. Filter the reaction product and wash three times with tetrahydrofuran and water to remove unreacted species and residual templates.
  2. Dry the solid sample in an oven at 120 °C under vacuum for 12 hours.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Thorough washing and precise temperature control provide reproducibility and high pore activation.

#### COF Preparation [2]: COF-304 (Chiral Variants)

##### Step 1: Precursor Preparation
- **Reagents**:  
  • 0.4 mmol palmitoylglycine (C16‑GlyA)  
  • 0.2 mmol 2,3‑dihydroxyterephthalaldehyde (2,3‑DHTA)  
  • Similar amounts of sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid as COF-300.
- **Conditions & Process**:  
  Similar to COF-300: form an emulsion at 50 °C, adjust pH to 1, then add the aromatic precursor.

##### Step 2: COF Assembly and Purification
- **Reagents**:  
  • 0.1 mmol TAM with a catalytic amount of PTSA.
- **Process**:  
  Assemble under 50 °C for one hour, followed by filtering and washing (tetrahydrofuran and water), then allow the product to air dry.
- **Key Steps**:  
  Consistent reagent sequencing facilitates effective chiral induction; careful control of stirring and temperature is essential.

Both workflows emphasize accurate reagent measurements, step-wise addition, and targeted purification to guarantee reproducibility and the formation of high-crystallinity, chiral COF frameworks. ## 5. Evaluation Methods

### 5.1 Activation

The activation process for the COFs described in this study is executed in two distinct stages: a washing and natural air drying protocol during synthesis, and a subsequent thermal pre-treatment applied for characterization purposes. Both approaches are designed to remove guest molecules, including residual chiral templates and solvents, while preserving the framework’s integrity.

#### 5.1.1 Solvent Exchange and Natural Drying

During synthesis, the as‐formed COF materials undergo a series of washing steps with tetrahydrofuran followed by water. This sequential solvent exchange is performed three times and is critical for dissolving and removing the chiral templates and any residual by-products. The use of tetrahydrofuran, a proficient solvent for organic residues, ensures that the interior pores are cleared, thereby contributing to enhanced porosity. Following the washing process, the products are left to air dry under ambient conditions. This gentle drying method preserves crystallinity and prevents framework collapse that might occur under harsher conditions. No specific soaking times or refresh cycles are reported beyond the three sequential washing cycles.

#### 5.1.2 Thermal Pre-treatment for Characterization

For purposes related to gas adsorption measurements and other characterizations, a separate activation step is implemented. In this case, samples are thermally treated at 120 °C under vacuum for 12 hours. This controlled heating serves to remove high-boiling-point solvents—such as water and tetrahydrofuran—that might otherwise remain trapped within the COF matrix. Although this thermal activation effectively cleans the internal pore system, it is used exclusively as a preparatory step prior to characterization. It is also noted that higher activation temperatures (e.g., 300 °C and above) can induce framework degradation, as evidenced by a marked reduction in the chiral signal and weakened diffraction intensities.

Together, these procedures ensure that the COFs are appropriately “activated” for optimal porosity and performance, while employing conditions that maintain the framework’s stability and chiral integrity throughout the synthesis and subsequent testing stages.

### 5.2 Application/Testing

The application and testing of the COF materials focused primarily on gas adsorption performance under controlled conditions. The testing parameters were carefully selected to probe the unique structural transformations of the COFs and to allow direct correlation with phase changes observed by in situ PXRD.

Additional water vapor adsorption studies on COF-304 have been executed at ambient and elevated temperatures following thermal activation processes.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
The crystal structure and phase purity of COF-300 and COF-304 were primarily determined by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and three-dimensional electron diffraction (3D ED). PXRD patterns for COF-300 revealed sharp, well-defined peaks, notably at 2θ = 6.4° corresponding to the (200) plane in the “opened” (op) phase, indicative of high crystallinity and phase purity. Upon removal of chiral templates, this peak shifted to 2θ = 9.0°, signifying a transition to the “closed” (cp) phase. Further activation led to the “vacuum” (V) phase, with additional peak changes observed during in situ CO₂ adsorption (e.g., a new peak at 2θ ≈ 7.5°). Rietveld refinement of PXRD data provided precise unit cell parameters for each phase, confirming structural transitions and the successful formation of distinct, pure crystalline phases. COF-304 and its chiral variants also exhibited high crystallinity, with PXRD patterns distinct from the achiral form, supporting phase purity.

#### 6.1.2 Chiral Optical Activity
Chiral transmission and optical activity were assessed using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. COF-300L and COF-300D displayed nearly mirror-image CD spectra, with COF-300L-cp showing positive peaks near 339 and 471 nm and a negative peak at 611 nm, while COF-300D-cp exhibited reversed signals. The dissymmetry factor (g₍abs₎) decreased from 0.012 to 0.004 as the concentration of the chiral template was reduced, correlating with a red-shift in the UV–vis absorption peak from 470 to 500 nm. These results confirm the successful induction and tunability of conformational chirality within the COF frameworks. Variable-temperature CD studies demonstrated robust chiral stability up to 95 °C, with significant loss of chiral signal only above 300 °C.

#### 6.1.3 Covalent Bond Formation and Template Elimination
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy verified the formation of covalent imine bonds and the elimination of template molecules. The disappearance of N–H and C=O stretching vibrations (3300–3500 cm⁻¹ and 1650–1700 cm⁻¹, respectively) from the templates, alongside the emergence of a strong C=N stretching peak (1620–1640 cm⁻¹), confirmed successful imine linkage formation and template removal in both COF-300 and COF-304.

#### 6.1.4 Morphology and Particle Size
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), including continuous precession electron diffraction tomography (cPEDT), was employed to analyze morphology and particle size. Both chiral and achiral COFs exhibited similar average particle sizes (~431 nm). The concentration of chiral micelles during synthesis influenced the size of the assemblies, with lower concentrations yielding smaller particles and reduced cell volumes, supporting a controlled nucleation and growth mechanism.

#### 6.1.5 Porosity and Gas Adsorption Behavior
Porosity and dynamic framework behavior were characterized by gas adsorption isotherms and in situ PXRD. Nitrogen adsorption at 77 K and water vapor adsorption at 298 K demonstrated reversible uptake and release, with multiple cycles confirming structural robustness. In situ PXRD during CO₂ adsorption at 195 K revealed guest-driven, reversible phase transitions among the V, cp, and op phases, underscoring the dynamic and responsive nature of the COF frameworks.

#### 6.1.6 Thermal Stability
Thermal stability was evaluated by annealing and hydrothermal treatment. COF-300L retained its chiral CD signals and crystallinity after annealing at 200 °C and under hydrothermal conditions (120–200 °C). Significant loss of chiral signal and crystallinity occurred only above 300 °C, indicating that the frameworks are thermally robust under practical conditions.




### 6.2 Application/Performance

#### 6.2.1 Gas and Vapor Uptake Capacity
- **Description:** Gas and vapor uptake capacity refers to the amount of gas or vapor adsorbed by the COF per unit mass, typically measured via volumetric adsorption isotherms at controlled temperatures and pressures. This metric is central to evaluating the porosity and guest-accessibility of the framework.
- **Experimental Data:** For COF-300, nitrogen (N₂) adsorption isotherms were measured at 77 K, and carbon dioxide (CO₂) adsorption isotherms at 195 K, using a MicrotracBEL Max II analyzer. Water vapor adsorption was also assessed at 298 K. Samples were activated at 120 °C under vacuum for 12 hours prior to testing.
- **Observations and Trends:** The COF-300 materials exhibited reversible adsorption–desorption cycles for both N₂ and water vapor, indicating robust porosity and guest-accessible channels. Dynamic framework transformations were observed during CO₂ adsorption, with the structure transitioning between contracted (V), intermediate, and open (op) phases as a function of CO₂ pressure. These transitions were tracked by in situ PXRD, confirming guest-responsive behavior and repeatable uptake performance.

#### 6.2.2 Structural Responsiveness and Reversibility
- **Description:** Structural responsiveness refers to the ability of the COF framework to undergo reversible phase transitions in response to guest molecule adsorption. Reversibility is assessed by the repeatability of adsorption–desorption cycles and retention of structural features.
- **Experimental Data:** In situ PXRD during CO₂ adsorption at 195 K revealed that COF-300L-V remains stable up to P/P₀ = 0.2, then transitions through an intermediate phase (new peak at 2θ ≈ 7.5°) before fully converting to the open phase at higher pressures. These transitions are fully reversible upon desorption, as confirmed by repeated cycling.
- **Observations and Trends:** The COF-300 framework demonstrated robust and reversible guest-induced phase transitions, with PXRD patterns and adsorption isotherms remaining consistent over multiple cycles. This dynamic behavior distinguishes the chiral COFs from their achiral analogs and underpins their suitability for applications requiring repeatable adsorption and release.

#### 6.2.3 Chiroptical Performance and Tunability
- **Description:** Chiroptical performance is quantified by the absorption dissymmetry factor (g₍abs₎), measured via circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. This metric reflects the degree of chiral induction and optical activity in the COF.
- **Experimental Data:** For COF-300, g₍abs₎ values ranged from 0.004 to 0.012, with higher chiral template concentrations yielding stronger optical activity. CD spectral shifts (e.g., from 339 nm to higher wavelengths) were observed upon varying template concentration and during gas adsorption.
- **Observations and Trends:** The chiral optical response of COF-300 is tunable via template concentration, with lower concentrations leading to reduced g₍abs₎ values and cell volume contraction. During CO₂ adsorption, CD signals shifted dynamically, correlating with structural phase changes and demonstrating a direct link between framework stereochemistry and guest uptake.

#### 6.2.4 Thermal and Hydrothermal Stability
- **Description:** Stability is evaluated by the retention of crystallinity and chiral signals after exposure to elevated temperatures or hydrothermal conditions.
- **Experimental Data:** Variable-temperature CD spectroscopy (25–95 °C) and annealing at 200 °C showed that COF-300 retained both chiral signals and crystallinity. At 300–400 °C, significant loss of both was observed. Hydrothermal treatment at 120–200 °C led to gradual decomposition.
- **Observations and Trends:** The COF-300 framework exhibits excellent thermal stability up to 200 °C, maintaining both structure and chiroptical properties. Strong π–π interactions (3.2–3.7 Å) contribute to this stability, supporting repeated use in adsorption applications.

#### 6.2.5 Dynamic Behavior in COF-304
- **Description:** The dynamic adsorption and structural switching of COF-304 were evaluated using water vapor isotherms and PXRD.
- **Experimental Data:** Water vapor adsorption–desorption cycles confirmed reversible guest-induced transformations between hydrated and activated states, as evidenced by shifts in the (200) PXRD peak (2θ = 6.4° to 9.0°) and changes in unit cell volume.
- **Observations and Trends:** COF-304 demonstrated robust dynamic behavior and high crystallinity, with chiral induction confirmed by mirror-image CD spectra. The framework’s ability to switch between states underpins its potential for responsive separation and sensing applications.
