## 2. Synthesis Methods

### Extraction of Detailed Preparation and Post-processing Steps

This section details the experimental methodologies employed for synthesizing the two isomeric metal–covalent organic frameworks (MCOFs): E-MCOF via a kinetic (solvent‐thermal) pathway and Z-MCOF via a thermodynamically dominated route. A comparative analysis of these methods is provided in terms of synthesis complexity, reproducibility, scalability, and equipment requirements.

#### • E-MCOF Synthesis (Kinetic Pathway)
1. **Monomer Mixing and Solution Preparation**
   - Reagents: 2,4,6-trihydroxybenzene-1,3,5-tricarbaldehyde derivative (Tp–Gly) at 5.10 mg (0.0134 mmol) and copper nitrate dihydrate at 3.76 mg (0.02 mmol) are dissolved in 1.0 mL DMF and 1.0 mL MeOH.
   - Ultrasonicate the mixture to ensure homogeneity.
2. **Polymerization/Condensation**
   - Add 2.8 µL (0.02 mmol) of triethylamine (TEtA) and shake to initiate condensation.
3. **Crystallization / Reaction**
   - Incubate the mixture at 37 °C for one week, followed by heating at 100 °C for one additional day.
4. **Washing / Purification**
   - Collect the product by centrifugation and wash with 3 mL each of deionized water, ethanol, and acetone.
5. **Drying**
   - Dry the purified product in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 hours.

#### • Z-MCOF Synthesis (Thermodynamic Pathway)
1. **Monomer Mixing and Solution Preparation**
   - Reagents: Tp at 25.22 mg (0.12 mmol) and Z-Cu(Gly)₂ at 38.10 mg (0.18 mmol) are first ground into a homogeneous mixture.
2. **Polymerization/Condensation**
   - Add 50 µL of TEtA and grind until a viscous, uniform slurry forms; remove excess TEtA using an infrared lamp.
3. **Crystallization / Reaction**
   - Introduce 10 µL of water, further grind, and transfer the mixture into a 15 mL glass tube.
   - Degas using the freeze–pump–thaw method (three cycles to ~50 mTorr) followed by flame sealing under vacuum.
   - Heat the sealed tube at 90 °C for 72 hours.
4. **Washing / Purification**
   - Separate by centrifugation and perform solvent exchange with methanol and acetone.
5. **Drying**
   - Finally, dry the sample in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 hours.

### Comparative Analysis
The kinetic pathway for E-MCOF enables rapid framework formation with a sequential heating regime (initial mild conditions then high temperature) but may result in lower crystallinity. In contrast, the controlled, thermodynamically driven synthesis of Z-MCOF, though involving additional grinding and degassing steps, yields frameworks with enhanced periodicity and defined stacking, as evidenced by subsequent spectroscopic and microscopic characterization.

##### 3.1.3.1 COF-1 (E-MCOF)
- **Monomer solution**: Initially combined reagents were ultrasonically treated for 10 minutes at room temperature to ensure even dispersion.

- **Precursor solution**: The solid reagents were first gently ground in a mortar until a viscous texture was obtained, then subjected to sonication for enhanced uniformity.
- **Reaction mixture**: Following mixing, the sample was degassed using a freeze–pump–thaw protocol (three cycles) and stirred at 400 rpm under an inert N₂ atmosphere prior to flame sealing.

These clearly delineated steps—from precise reagent weighing to controlled liquid additions and systematic mixing—ensure the reproducibility and integrity of both COF syntheses, contributing directly to the observed differences in coordination configurations and subsequent electrochemiluminescence performance.

### 5.1 Activation

#### 5.1.1 Activation Temperature  
Activation temperature is critical for removing residual solvents and consolidating the COF’s crystalline framework. For both E-MCOF and Z-MCOF, the final activation step is performed by placing the product in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 hours. This thermal treatment aids in expelling high-boiling solvent residues and supports the formation of well-defined porosity. In contrast, other COF systems mentioned in the article, such as Cu–HHTP and Cu–HITP, undergo a similar vacuum drying process but at 50 °C.

#### 5.1.2 Activation Pressure  
The application of vacuum plays a crucial role in the activation process. Reaction vessels undergo degassing using the freeze–pump–thaw technique until the internal pressure reaches approximately 50 mTorr. This controlled low-pressure environment, achieved over multiple cycles, ensures the efficient removal of guest molecules and prevents oxygen or moisture interference, thereby maintaining the framework integrity.

#### 5.1.3 Activation Solvent  
Solvent exchange is performed prior to vacuum drying to replace high-boiling or residual synthesis solvents with more volatile ones. For E-MCOF, the post‐synthesis procedure involves washing in water, ethanol, and acetone, while Z-MCOF is subjected to a solvent exchange using methanol followed by acetone. This method not only aids in purging unreacted species but also helps maintain long-range order and enhances the resulting porosity, as later confirmed by improved PXRD and surface area measurements.

#### 5.1.4 Activation Time  
A defined activation duration of 12 hours in the vacuum oven is consistently applied across the reported COF systems. This period is established to ensure complete removal of entrapped solvents, which significantly contributes to enhanced framework stability and optimized catalytic performance. The article emphasizes that such timing, coupled with strategic solvent exchanges and controlled heating, directly correlates with improved electrochemiluminescence efficiency and porous network formation.

## 6 Results


### 6.1 Characterization Results

#### 6.1.1 Crystal Structure and Phase Purity
- **Description:** Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) was employed to assess the crystallinity, phase purity, and stacking models of the COFs. Pawley refinement was used to compare experimental and simulated patterns.
- **Findings:** E-MCOF exhibited a strong, narrow diffraction peak at 8.04°, with additional peaks at 13.90° and 15.07°, corresponding to the (100), (110), and (001) planes, respectively. Z-MCOF showed a broadened peak at 5.61° and another at 9.78°, with a broad feature from 5.43–6.41° and a (001) peak at 14.56°. Pawley refinement yielded low Rₚ and Rwp values (E-MCOF: Rₚ = 5.11%, Rwp = 6.54%; Z-MCOF: Rₚ = 4.30%, Rwp = 6.18%), confirming excellent agreement with simulated honeycomb (hcb) topologies.
- **Observations:** The distinct peak positions and sharpness confirm high crystallinity and phase purity, with stacking models consistent with the intended cis (E-MCOF) and trans (Z-MCOF) coordination environments.

#### 6.1.2 Chemical Bonding and Coordination Environment
- **Description:** FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, and solid-state NMR were used to verify framework formation and differentiate coordination isomers.
- **Findings:** FTIR spectra showed disappearance of amino (3262 cm⁻¹) and formyl (1641 cm⁻¹) bands, and broadening of the imine region (~1610 cm⁻¹), indicating successful condensation. The antisymmetric COO⁻ vibration shifted from 1627 to 1590 cm⁻¹ in E-MCOF. Raman spectra revealed cis-specific modes at 161, 208, and 279 cm⁻¹ for E-MCOF, and trans-specific modes at 196, 289, and 324 cm⁻¹ for Z-MCOF.
- **Observations:** These spectral changes confirm the formation of imine-linked frameworks and the distinct cis/trans coordination environments.

#### 6.1.3 Elemental Composition and Metal State
- **Description:** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) mapping were used to determine elemental composition and copper oxidation state.
- **Findings:** For Z-MCOF: C 74.25%, N 5.07%, O 18.74%, Cu 1.95%. For E-MCOF: C 68.68%, N 5.61%, O 23.15%, Cu 2.56%. XPS confirmed copper exclusively in the divalent state (Cu²⁺) in both COFs. EDS mapping showed uniform distribution of all elements.
- **Observations:** The elemental ratios and copper state are consistent with the proposed structures and confirm successful metal incorporation.

#### 6.1.4 Surface Area and Porosity
- **Description:** Gas adsorption measurements (N₂ at 77 K, CO₂ at 273 K) were performed to evaluate porosity and pore size distribution.
- **Findings:** E-MCOF showed negligible N₂-accessible porosity, while Z-MCOF exhibited a dominant pore size of 1.25 nm. CO₂ adsorption revealed a micropore size of 0.45 nm for E-MCOF and a broad distribution for Z-MCOF.
- **Observations:** E-MCOF’s uniform microporosity matches the AA-stacked model, while Z-MCOF’s broader distribution reflects its twisted, few-layered stacking.

#### 6.1.5 Morphology and Microstructure
- **Description:** Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and atomic force microscopy (AFM) were used to analyze particle shape and aggregation.
- **Findings:** E-MCOF forms plate-like structures (500 nm–1 μm), with thin, less densely stacked plates. Z-MCOF displays smooth, membrane-like surfaces in water and blocky stacks in dioxane; AFM shows nanosheets with diameters of 50–200 nm and thicknesses down to 1.4 nm.
- **Observations:** E-MCOF aggregates into thicker plates, while Z-MCOF forms highly dispersible, thin nanosheets, consistent with their respective stacking models.

#### 6.1.6 Thermal Stability
- **Description:** Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) assessed framework stability and metal content.
- **Findings:** Both COFs exhibit thermal stability consistent with theoretical Cu content (~20%), with decomposition onset typical for COFs above 300 °C.
- **Observations:** The frameworks are thermally robust, suitable for practical applications.

#### 6.1.7 Additional Functional Characterization
- **Description:** Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) and catalytic activity were evaluated to link structure to function.
- **Findings:** Z-MCOF shows approximately double the ECL intensity of E-MCOF, attributed to better exposure of Cu nodes and a higher d-band center, enhancing catalytic reduction of K₂S₂O₈.
- **Observations:** The coordination environment directly influences catalytic and ECL performance, validating the design strategy.



### 6.2 Application/Performance

#### 6.2.1 Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) Performance

- **Description:** Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) intensity and efficiency were used to evaluate the catalytic activity of the COFs, specifically their ability to catalyze the reduction of K₂S₂O₈ in a cathodic ECL system. The relative ECL efficiency (Φ_ECL) was calculated as the ratio of integrated ECL intensity to the total anodic charge:  
  \[
  \Phi_{\text{ECL}} = \frac{\int I\,dt}{Q_a}
  \]
  where \( I \) is the ECL intensity and \( Q_a \) is the anodic charge.

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Z-MCOF exhibited approximately twice the cathodic ECL intensity of E-MCOF under identical conditions (0.1 M PBS, 0.1 M KCl, 0.1 M K₂S₂O₈, pH 7.4; potential range 0.0 V to –1.7 V, scan rate 0.1 V s⁻¹, PMT voltage 700 V).
  - E-MCOF showed lower ECL intensity, attributed to less exposed copper centers.

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The enhanced ECL performance of Z-MCOF is ascribed to its trans coordination environment, which positions the copper d-band center closer to the Fermi level, facilitating more efficient adsorption and catalytic activation of K₂S₂O₈. The more accessible Cu sites in Z-MCOF promote higher electron transfer rates, resulting in superior ECL efficiency compared to the cis-coordinated E-MCOF.

#### 6.2.2 Gas Adsorption Properties

- **Description:** Gas adsorption capacity was assessed using N₂ isotherms at 77 K and CO₂ isotherms at 273 K. Pore size distribution was determined by NLDFT fitting, providing insight into the framework’s microporosity and its suitability for gas storage or separation.

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - E-MCOF displayed a CO₂ adsorption pore size of approximately 0.45 nm, consistent with theoretical predictions for AA-stacked structures.
  - Z-MCOF exhibited a broader pore size distribution, reflecting its more disordered, twisted stacking.

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The measured pore size for E-MCOF aligns with theoretical expectations, indicating successful framework formation and activation. Z-MCOF’s broader distribution suggests increased structural heterogeneity, which may enhance accessibility for guest molecules.

#### 6.2.3 Stability and Dispersibility

- **Description:** Stability was evaluated by monitoring structural integrity and dispersibility after solvent exchange and vacuum drying, as well as during electrochemical cycling.

- **Experimental Data:**  
  - Both E-MCOF and Z-MCOF retained their structural features after activation.
  - Z-MCOF demonstrated excellent dispersibility in water, maintaining a smooth, membrane-like morphology with low surface roughness (~1.87 nm), while E-MCOF showed higher aggregation and roughness (~27.2 nm).

- **Observations and Trends:**  
  The superior dispersibility and stability of Z-MCOF are attributed to interlayer repulsion and its twisted, trans-coordinated structure, which also supports sustained performance during ECL testing. E-MCOF, with greater aggregation, exhibited lower stability and less accessible active sites.
